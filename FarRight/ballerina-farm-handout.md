# Primary Source Handout: "Meet the Queen of the 'Trad Wives'" — Selected Excerpts (2024)

**For classroom use — selected passages with editorial framing**

*Hannah Neeleman, known online as @ballerinafarm, is one of the most-followed "tradwife" influencers in the United States, with 9 million Instagram followers, 7.4 million on TikTok, and 1.5 million on YouTube as of 2024. A Juilliard-trained ballet dancer, she married Daniel Neeleman — son of the JetBlue billionaire founder David Neeleman — and now lives on a 328-acre farm in Kamas, Utah, raising eight children, making sourdough bread, milking cows, and competing in beauty pageants. Her social media content depicts an intensely domestic life: home births by candlelight, raw milk poured straight into coffee cups, homeschooling on a Mormon-Christian curriculum, organic food preparation for a family of ten.*

*In July 2024, The Sunday Times sent journalist Megan Agnew to spend a day at the Neeleman farm. The resulting profile went viral, generating extensive debate about whether Neeleman's lifestyle represents genuine choice, cultural conservatism, or something more constraining. Neeleman herself explicitly does not identify with the "tradwife" label and has never expressed explicit far-right ideology on her social media platforms. She does not appear in Leidig's book, whose research covers 2015–2022.*

*These passages are drawn directly from Agnew's reported profile. Unlike the other primary sources in this course, this is journalism rather than far-right self-presentation — Agnew was physically present at the farm and observed the family directly. The passages are included here to test Leidig's framework against a contemporary case that post-dates her research and sits at the boundary between tradwife culture and the far right. Six months after the Sunday Times profile, Neeleman appeared on the cover of Evie magazine — a conservative women's outlet funded by Peter Thiel — alongside Eva Vlaardingerbroek, a Dutch far-right commentator who promoted conspiracies on Tucker Carlson's Fox show.*

## Passage 1: The Mrs. World Competition — Twelve Days Postpartum

> "Hannah Neeleman was pregnant with her eighth child and she had two due dates. The first was for a baby, obviously. The second, just under two weeks later, was for a beauty pageant."
>
> On January 2, Flora was born in the upstairs bedroom, without pain relief. "A one-push baby," Neeleman said on Instagram. In the first days she "laid low", not leaving the house. On the fifth day, she says, "I looked at Daniel and I said, 'I don't know if I'm going to be able to go. This is a lot.' I was bleeding and swollen, and milk was coming in."
>
> On the seventh day she rose from bed, doing her barre exercises in the bathroom. By day nine she was trying on outfits, zipping herself into a pair of leather trousers and skin-tight white ballgown. Day ten: spray tan. Day eleven: a two-hour flight with a newborn to Las Vegas, her husband and seven children following behind. And by day twelve she was on stage. Luckily, she says, she had stopped bleeding.

*— Megan Agnew, "Meet the Queen of the 'Trad Wives' (and her eight children)," The Sunday Times, July 20, 2024*

**[Discussion prompt: Leidig argues that "perceptions of authenticity and accessibility serve as the most powerful tools of the modern far right." But what is being authenticated here? Neeleman's account of the postpartum pageant is presented as an act of grit — she "prepared," she "kept fit," she did barre exercises on day seven. The physical cost (bleeding, swelling, engorged breasts) is included, but framed as evidence of strength rather than as a problem to be solved. Compare this to how Leidig describes far-right women influencers' "red pill" narratives in Chapter 2: personal suffering is consistently reframed as a journey toward a more authentic self. What is Neeleman's suffering being redeemed by? Who is the intended audience for this particular kind of toughness?]**

## Passage 2: The Ballet Studio

> The journalist observes: "The only space earmarked to be Neeleman's own — a small barn she wanted to convert into a ballet studio — ended up becoming the kids' schoolroom."

And later, on the subject of pain relief during childbirth:

> "The bedroom is also where she had her children... She also gave birth to them without pain relief. None at all? She shakes her head. Why? 'I don't know, I just have never loved taking it.' She stops herself. 'Except with Martha — I was two weeks overdue and she was 10lb and Daniel wasn't with me…' She lowers her voice. Daniel is currently out of the room taking a phone call. 'So I got an epidural. And it was an amazing experience.' Where was Daniel that day? 'It was shipping day [for the meat boxes] and he was manning the crew.' But the epidural was kind of great? She pauses — and smiles. 'It was kinda great.' "

*— Megan Agnew, The Sunday Times, July 20, 2024*

**[Discussion prompt: Two small moments — a converted barn, a quietly disclosed epidural — and a significant pattern: things that were Neeleman's own (her ballet, her pain relief preferences) have been reorganized around the household's needs or her husband's expectations, and the disclosures about them happen when Daniel is out of the room. The journalist doesn't editorialize. She doesn't need to. Leidig in Chapter 4 describes the tradwife lifestyle as genuinely porous with the far right precisely because "what tradwives don't always make clear is where the division lies." Apply that framing here: is what we're seeing constraint, complementarianism, or something that requires a different category? And notice the commercial operation behind the authentic facade: 3 farm employees, 30 at the warehouse, 10+ in the office, a creative director managing the website's visual identity. What happens to the concept of "authenticity" when it is managed by a creative director?]**

## Passage 3: Birth Control as Prayer — or Something Else

> On the subject of family planning:
>
> "Do you — I pause and look at her fixedly — plan pregnancies? 'No,' Daniel says. 'When he says no,' Neeleman responds gently, 'it's very much a matter of prayer for me. I'm, like, "God, is it time to bring another one to the Earth?" And I've never been told no.'
>
> 'But for whatever reason it's exactly nine months [after a baby] that she's ready for the next one,' he says.
>
> 'It's definitely a matter of prayer,' she says.
>
> 'It's a matter of prayer but somehow it's exactly nine months,' he says."

*— Megan Agnew, The Sunday Times, July 20, 2024*

**[Discussion prompt: Leidig notes in Chapter 3 that far-right women influencers "rarely, if ever, challenge traditional notions of femininity as tied to reproductive abilities" — the nuclear family and high birth rates are presented as divine imperatives. Neeleman's answer frames pregnancy as prayer and divine communication. Daniel's answer — twice — frames it as biology and timing. These are not the same claim, and the journalist lets the gap stand. Recall Leidig's discussion of Ayla Stewart's "white baby challenge" (Chapter 3) and Lauren Chen's "The Need to Breed" video (Introduction): far-right influencers situate high white birth rates in explicitly demographic and civilizational terms. Neeleman's framing is theological rather than racial. Does that difference matter analytically? What work does the theological framing do that the demographic framing does not?]**

## Passage 4: "I Feel Like I'm a Femin—"

> On the question of whether she is a feminist:
>
> " 'I feel like I'm a femin-,' she stops herself. 'There's so many different ways you could take that word. I don't even know what feminism means any more.' She 'absolutely' feels as though she has become politicised by other people. 'We try so hard to be neutral and be ourselves and people will put a label on everything. This is just our normal life.' "
>
> On the "tradwife" label specifically:
>
> " 'I don't necessarily identify with it,' she says, 'because we are traditional in the sense that it's a man and a woman, we have children, but I do feel like we're paving a lot of paths that haven't been paved before.' That is the biggest paradox: in selling the life of a stay-at-home mother, Neeleman and the other trad wives have created high-earning jobs. They are being paid to act out a fantasy."

*— Megan Agnew, The Sunday Times, July 20, 2024*

**[Discussion prompt: "I feel like I'm a femin—." The sentence is abandoned mid-word and what follows is a denial — not of the content of feminism but of the word itself. Compare this to the Alena Kate Pettitt passage in Leidig Chapter 4: "A tradwife is categorically not a white supremacist(!?)" Both denials follow the same structure: disavow the label while embodying the content. Leidig argues that this labeling problem is strategic rather than incidental — the far right and tradwife movements are more effective when they can claim ordinary status. But notice the journalist's parenthetical observation: Neeleman and other tradwives have "created high-earning jobs" by being "paid to act out a fantasy." What does it mean that the fantasy of domestic traditionalism is itself a form of entrepreneurial self-determination? Does the commercial success complicate or confirm Leidig's framework?]**

## Passage 5: The Reporter's Observation

> "I can't, it seems, get an answer out of Neeleman without her being corrected, interrupted or answered for by either her husband or a child. Usually I am doing battle with steely Hollywood publicists; today I am up against an army of toddlers who all want their mum and a husband who thinks he knows better."
>
> And separately, from behavioral scientist Leslie Root at the University of Colorado:
>
> " 'Trad wives are seen as a counterculture against the "rot" of low birth rates,' says Leslie Root. 'This isn't isolated, it is part of a wider political anxiety that I am worried might ultimately end in attempts to circumscribe women's lives.' "

*— Megan Agnew, The Sunday Times, July 20, 2024*

**[Discussion prompt: The journalist's embedded observation — that she cannot get an unmediated answer from Neeleman — is the article's most structurally significant moment, and it is offered without commentary. Leidig describes networked intimacy as "a deliberate strategy to gain followers even if the influencers frame it as genuine." But the Sunday Times profile wasn't produced by Neeleman — it was produced by a journalist with independent access. What the journalist saw does not appear in Neeleman's social media content: the husband who answers for his wife, the barn that became a schoolroom, the quietly-confessed epidural. Now apply Leslie Root's claim: the tradwife movement is part of "a wider political anxiety that might ultimately end in attempts to circumscribe women's lives." Root is not saying Neeleman is far-right. She is making a structural argument about where the cultural logic leads. Six months after this profile, Neeleman appeared on the cover of Evie — a Peter Thiel-funded conservative magazine — alongside a Dutch far-right commentator who appeared on Tucker Carlson's Fox show promoting conspiracies. Does that fact change how you read this profile? Should it?]**

## Reading Notes for Instructors

*(See separate instructor notes document for full analytical framing and discussion guidance.)*

**On what this document is:** Unlike the other primary sources in this course, this is not a far-right text — it is a journalistic profile of someone who explicitly does not identify with the far right. Its pedagogical function is different: it tests Leidig's framework against a harder case. The question is not "is this far-right?" but "what does Leidig predict, and does the evidence support her?"

**On the Evie sequel:** The piece becomes analytically complete only when paired with the December 2024 Evie cover. Neeleman denied the tradwife label in July 2024; by December she was on the cover of a Peter Thiel-funded magazine alongside a Tucker Carlson conspiracy theorist. That arc — denial followed by explicit alignment — is the Leidig pipeline made visible in six months. Worth presenting as a sequenced reveal rather than front-loading it.

**On the journalist's access problem:** Agnew's inability to get Neeleman alone — and her observation that Daniel answers for his wife — is the single most analytically loaded element of the piece. It is worth pointing out to students that this is *not* what appears in Neeleman's social media content. The gap between the curated self and the observed self is precisely what Leidig means by "calibrated amateurism" — the performance of naturalness that is, in fact, managed.

**On the commercial operation:** The creative director, the 30 warehouse employees, the branded sourdough starters and beeswax candles are Leidig Chapter 5 (far-right entrepreneurism) applied to a tradwife who doesn't claim to be far-right. The commercial infrastructure makes the "this is just our normal life" framing untenable on its own terms.
