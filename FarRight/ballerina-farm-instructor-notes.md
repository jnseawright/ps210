# Instructor Notes: "Meet the Queen of the 'Trad Wives'" (Sunday Times, 2024)
## Supplementary Material for Session 4A — Leidig, *The Women of the Far Right*, Chs. 1–4

---

## What This Document Is — and What It Isn't

Unlike every other primary source in this course, the Agnew profile is not a far-right text. It was not produced by the subject to promote a political agenda. It is a piece of embedded journalism from a mainstream British broadsheet — The Sunday Times — produced by a reporter who spent a full day physically present at the Neeleman farm. Megan Agnew had independent observational access that Neeleman's own social media does not provide and that Leidig's digital ethnography cannot replicate.

This distinction is pedagogically significant. The other primary sources in this course (BNP manifesto, EDL speeches, Breivik, Proud Boys, Bundyville) are examples of far-right self-presentation: texts produced by movement actors for movement purposes. The Agnew profile is a third-party account of a subject who explicitly disavows the far-right label. Students are therefore not being asked "what does this text reveal about far-right ideology?" — the usual framing — but something harder: **"Does Leidig's framework apply to a case that predates her research and resists her categories? And how do we know?"**

This is, among other things, a lesson in how to use social science frameworks as tools for analysis rather than templates for confirmation.

---

## Where This Material Fits in Session 4A

Place the Neeleman material **after Mini-Lecture 3** (Femininity as Propaganda Vehicle) and **in place of** — or as an extended version of — the Discussion Activity. It works best as approximately 20 minutes, structured as follows:

1. **Anchor passage** (2 min): Read Passage 1 (postpartum pageant) aloud or ask a student to read it.
2. **Leidig connection** (3 min): Brief instructor setup connecting the passage to the calibrated-amateurism argument from Ch. 2 and the tradwife pipeline from Ch. 4.
3. **Small-group work** (7 min): Students work through Passages 2–4 in pairs or groups of three, with the question: *Where does Leidig's framework fit? Where does it break down?*
4. **Full-class debrief and sequenced reveal** (8 min): Pull the class back together. At the end of the debrief — not before — introduce the Evie cover (see below).

If time is short and you need to cut, Passages 2 and 4 are the highest-yield. Passage 2 (the ballet studio and the epidural) is the most analytically loaded single moment in the piece; Passage 4 (the unfinished feminist sentence) connects most directly to the denial-and-disavowal pattern Leidig documents throughout. Passage 3 (birth control as prayer) is excellent for the civilizational birth-rate argument but can be assigned as prep rather than worked in class.

## The Core Analytical Payload

Three things make the Agnew profile pedagogically distinctive:

**1. The gap between curated self and observed reality.** Leidig's central concept — calibrated amateurism — refers to the performance of naturalness and authenticity that is, in fact, carefully managed. On Neeleman's social media, everything is organic: raw milk, candlelit home births, sourdough starter passed down the family, children running through sunlit fields. Agnew's embedded reporting reveals a different set of facts: the creative director managing the website's visual identity, 30 warehouse employees, 10+ in the office. The barn Neeleman wanted as a personal ballet studio became the children's schoolroom. Pain relief she found "kinda great" is disclosed quietly, while her husband is on a phone call in another room.

None of these details appear in Neeleman's social media content. The most analytically loaded observation in the article is Agnew's summary: *"I can't, it seems, get an answer out of Neeleman without her being corrected, interrupted or answered for by either her husband or a child."* That sentence is the one element of Neeleman's actual life that is structurally invisible in her influencer presence. The gap between those two realities is Leidig's "calibrated amateurism" made visible — and it was made visible only because a reporter was physically present.

**2. The denial-and-disavowal pattern.** Neeleman's "I feel like I'm a femin—" is the most extraordinary sentence in the profile because it is abandoned mid-word and replaced not with a counter-argument but with a semantic retreat: "There's so many different ways you could take that word. I don't even know what feminism means any more." Leidig documents this pattern repeatedly — Alena Kate Pettitt's "A tradwife is categorically not a white supremacist(!?)" being the purest example. Both follow the same structure: *disavow the label while embodying the content*. The denial is not incidental; it is, as Leidig argues, strategically optimal. The far right and the tradwife movement are most culturally effective precisely when they can claim ordinary status.

Ask students: what would Neeleman need to say or do for you to conclude she *is* tradwife? If the bar for disqualification is her own self-description, no amount of observable evidence would ever satisfy it. This is a useful methodological point about the limits of self-report data.

**3. The theological-vs.-demographic framing of reproduction.** Passage 3 (birth control as prayer) is the subtlest of the five passages but analytically rich. Neeleman frames her serial pregnancies as divine communication: "God, is it time to bring another one to the Earth?" Daniel's framing — twice, over her answer — is biological and rhythmic: "somehow exactly nine months." These are not the same claim. The theological framing performs innocence: a woman following God's call is not the same as a woman following a demographic imperative. Compare Ayla Stewart's "white baby challenge" (Ch. 3) and Lauren Chen's "The Need to Breed" (Introduction): both situate high white birth rates explicitly in civilizational and demographic terms. Neeleman's framing is stripped of racial content. Ask students: does the racial dimension *need* to be explicit for Leidig's framework to apply? What work is the theological framing doing that the demographic framing cannot?

## The Sequenced Reveal: The Evie Cover

Do not mention the Evie cover in your setup. Present it only at the end of the full-class debrief, as a new piece of evidence introduced *after* students have formed their initial assessments.

The setup: "I want to show you something that was published six months after this profile."

The reveal: In December 2024, Neeleman appeared on the cover of *Evie* magazine, a conservative women's media outlet founded with backing from Peter Thiel. She was featured alongside Eva Vlaardingerbroek, a Dutch far-right commentator who appeared on Tucker Carlson's Fox show promoting the "Great Replacement" theory. This is not a lifestyle magazine. This is a Thiel-funded ideological project whose orbit includes figures with direct ties to the replacement-theory wing of the far right.

Then ask: "Does this change how you read the profile you just analyzed? Should it?"

Two possible student positions worth drawing out:

- *Yes, it changes everything*: The Evie cover confirms the pipeline Leidig describes. Neeleman denied the tradwife label in July 2024; by December she was on the cover of a Thiel-funded publication alongside a European conspiracist. The six-month arc is the Leidig pipeline made empirically visible.

- *No, it doesn't change the profile*: The Evie cover is evidence about December 2024, not about July 2024. The question is whether the profile's evidence is sufficient to draw the analytical conclusions — and the Evie cover is subsequent confirmation, not retroactive proof. (This is actually the more methodologically careful position, and worth acknowledging.)

The pedagogically productive outcome is not consensus but awareness that *evidence gathered at different time points can change our interpretation of earlier evidence* — and that this is a general feature of analyzing radicalization trajectories, not a trick of this case.

## The Commercial Operation

Passage 2's mention of the creative director, 30 warehouse employees, and 10+ office staff is easy for students to pass over, but it is analytically important and worth pausing on. Neeleman's claim — "This is just our normal life" — becomes structurally untenable when the "normal life" is managed by a marketing infrastructure of that scale.

Connect this to Leidig Chapter 5 (far-right entrepreneurism), even though students haven't read it yet: far-right women influencers are not just cultural producers, they are commercial entrepreneurs who monetize their political-ideological content through Patreon, YouTube monetization, merchandise, book deals, and sponsored content. Neeleman's case is notable because she is a far *more* successful commercial operator than most of Leidig's subjects — and because her commercial scale makes the "authenticity" claim proportionally harder to sustain.

The creative director managing the farm's visual identity is doing exactly what Leidig means by "calibrated amateurism": the natural look is produced. The gap between the aesthetic claim (organic, unfiltered, genuine) and the commercial reality (branded, scaled, professionally managed) is not a contradiction Neeleman resolves — it is the product she is selling.

## Anticipated Student Moves

**"Isn't this just a lifestyle choice? Why is it our business if she wants to live this way?"**

Acknowledge the genuine normative tension here: individual autonomy matters, and there is real risk in pathologizing unconventional domestic arrangements. The analytical question is not whether Neeleman should be permitted to make the choices she is making. It is: *what does the representation of those choices do in the broader information ecosystem?* Leslie Root's argument (Passage 5) is structural, not individual: the tradwife genre — regardless of any single practitioner's intentions — is part of a cultural pattern that produces "a wider political anxiety that might ultimately end in attempts to circumscribe women's lives." Apply Bolet & Foos here: the concern is normalization (descriptive norms — the belief that this is what other people want) rather than persuasion (personal attitude change). You don't need Neeleman to be a white nationalist for her content to shift the Overton window on what constitutes acceptable domestic arrangements.

**"She's not far-right, though. She's never said anything explicitly political."**

This is the right objection, and it should be taken seriously rather than dismissed. Leidig's claim is not that tradwife influencers are card-carrying far-right members — it is that their cultural production creates the conditions in which far-right ideology can spread. The tradwife pipeline is porous and permeable, not a direct tunnel. The Elora/"Blonde Buttermaker" example from Chapter 4 is useful here: Elora's fermented butter videos contain no far-right content; her Red Ice TV sponsorship is the connection. The far-right infrastructure surrounds the content rather than appearing in it.

The Evie cover, if you have introduced it, is direct evidence that the porousness runs in both directions: Neeleman's content is valued by explicitly ideological operators even when — perhaps especially when — she does not produce explicitly ideological content.

**"The commercial operation just makes her a successful entrepreneur. Lots of lifestyle brands are corporatized."**

Correct — and the commercial operation alone does not establish a far-right connection. But it does something more limited and just as important: it destroys the specific claim to authenticity on which her cultural influence rests. Her audience follows her because they believe they are seeing real, unmediated domestic life. The creative director is the tell: the "real, unmediated" content is professionally produced. This is not a moral critique of entrepreneurship; it is an observation about the mechanism by which her influence operates.

**"How is this different from any lifestyle influencer who's just selling an aspirational image?"**

It isn't, structurally — and that's Leidig's point. The tradwife pipeline works because tradwife content is *indistinguishable* from aspirational lifestyle content. The distinction is in what the cultural logic points toward when it's followed to its endpoint: Ayla Stewart, Elora/Red Ice TV, the Evie cover. The lifestyle aesthetic functions as a gateway to a political project whether or not any individual practitioner intends it that way.

**"You're being condescending to women who've made a genuine choice."**

This objection often carries real emotional weight for students, and it deserves a direct response. The analysis is not a judgment on women who prefer domestic life or who reject feminist political positions. It is an analysis of *how a specific cultural form functions in the information ecosystem*, which is a different question. The course's analytical frame (Leidig, Welch, Stanley) consistently distinguishes between individual actors and structural effects. A woman can genuinely prefer the life Neeleman describes and that preference can simultaneously be being harvested by political entrepreneurs. Both things can be true.

## Laura Loomer: Contrast Case, Not Illustrative Case

The PBS transcript on Laura Loomer is *not* recommended as a companion piece for the Neeleman material in Session 4A — but it is worth knowing how the two cases relate, in case students ask about it or you want to use it separately.

Loomer is a pipeline *endpoint*, not an entry point. She makes explicitly conspiratorial claims (5G, COVID skepticism, anti-Muslim rhetoric), ran for Congress, was banned from major platforms including Twitter/X, Venmo, Uber, and Lyft, and has called herself "proud Islamophobe." She has no pretense of lifestyle content or apolitical domesticity. Placing her alongside Neeleman in the same session would short-circuit the analytical task: students would correctly identify Loomer as far-right and then incorrectly use her as a contrast to exonerate Neeleman.

If you want to use the Loomer material, it works better as a Week 7 (gender and violence) or Week 5 (normalization and mainstreaming) case — or as a coda to a later session on platform deplatforming, alongside the Bolet & Foos finding that deplatforming remains the most effective tool. The question "what does the pipeline produce when it runs to completion?" is Loomer's analytical function; it is not Neeleman's.

## Connections to Leidig — Chapter by Chapter

**Chapter 2 (The Alternative Influence Network / Microcelebrity tactics):**
- Calibrated amateurism — the professional production of "natural" content — is the central concept. The Agnew profile makes it empirically visible: the creative director, the warehouse operation, the farm's managed visual identity.
- Networked intimacy: Leidig argues that Instagram Stories function as unfiltered radicalization vectors because they invite direct-message intimacy. Neeleman's social media presence operates on exactly this model. What the Stories never show: the husband answering for his wife, the barn that became a schoolroom.
- Red pill vlogs as self-discovery narratives: Neeleman's public narrative is not a red-pill story, but it follows the same redemptive structure — suffering (postpartum pageant day 12) reframed as strength, the physical cost as evidence of character. The self is being authenticated through trial.

**Chapter 3 (Femininity Not Feminism):**
- Antifeminism as gateway: Neeleman's abandoned "I feel like I'm a femin—" is precisely the ideological threshold Leidig identifies. She stops short of claiming the word; the word has become contaminated.
- Complementarianism and religion as legitimating frameworks: Neeleman's birth control answer ("it's very much a matter of prayer") is the most theological version of the arguments Leidig identifies in Stewart, Chen, and Riley. Worth asking: is the Mormon-Christian frame doing the same cultural work as the Catholicism Leidig analyzes?
- Civilizational birth rates: The demographic imperative is stripped of racial language in Neeleman's framing but structurally present. The Thiel/Evie connection supplies the missing ideological context.

**Chapter 4 (The Making of a Tradwife):**
- Leidig's key observation: "What tradwives don't always make clear is where the division lies." The Agnew profile is the best available illustration: the line between the genuine domestic preference and the politically functional representation of that preference is precisely what the journalist cannot get Neeleman to draw.
- The Elora/"Blonde Buttermaker" comparison: Elora's lacto-fermentation content is indistinguishable from food influencer content; the Red Ice TV sponsorship is the tell. Neeleman's farm content is indistinguishable from aspirational lifestyle content; the Evie/Thiel/Vlaardingerbroek connection is the tell.
- Seyward Darby's "tradwives and white nationalists share core objectives, myths, and iconography": the Neeleman case is not a clean illustration of this claim (the racial content is absent from her stated positions), but the Evie cover puts her in direct symbolic company with figures who supply that content explicitly.

---

## Connections Forward

**Week 5 (Normalization and Mainstreaming):**
The Bolet & Foos finding about normalization (descriptive norms — the belief that others agree) rather than persuasion is directly applicable here. Neeleman's cultural function may be less about converting individual viewers to her worldview than about shifting perceived social norms around domesticity, birth rates, and women's roles. You don't need to agree with her position; you need only to believe that many people do. Leslie Root's worry ("attempts to circumscribe women's lives") is precisely a normalization argument.

Also: the Gramsci "morbid symptoms" frame (introduced in the Gomes-Franco reading for Session 4B) applies to the tradwife genre — the "old" postwar feminist consensus is dying, the new has not yet been born, and Neeleman is one of the "morbid symptoms" filling the interregnum.

**Week 6 (Violence and the Far Right):**
Tradwife culture's relationship to violence is indirect but structurally present. The Darby quote establishes shared objectives with white nationalists whose political project has, in its most extreme forms, produced violence (see: the "Great Replacement" theory's role in the Christchurch, El Paso, and Pittsburgh synagogue attacks). The concern is not that Neeleman's content produces violence — it does not, directly. The concern is the ideological supply chain: content that normalizes the demographic anxiety that, in its most extreme forms, produces eliminationist ideology.

**Week 7 (Gender, Sexuality, and the Far Right):**
Neeleman is a high-profile case for the question the whole week will address: why does far-right politics so consistently return to gender and reproduction? The tradwife genre is an answer: control of women's reproductive capacity is the movement's organizing demographic logic, expressed in forms ranging from the aesthetic (Neeleman's Instagram) to the explicitly political (Project 2025's anti-contraception provisions) to the eliminationist (replacement-theory violence). Laura Loomer as a pipeline endpoint case works well here for contrast.

## Discussion Timing Guide

| Segment | Time |
|---|---|
| Read Passage 1 aloud + Leidig anchor | 5 min |
| Small-group work on Passages 2–4 | 7 min |
| Full-class debrief | 5 min |
| Sequenced reveal (Evie cover) + final question | 3 min |
| **Total** | **20 min** |

If you are pressed for time, the discussion can be run in 12 minutes by reading Passage 2 and Passage 4 only, skipping small groups, and running the debrief as a direct plenary discussion followed immediately by the Evie reveal. The sequenced reveal should not be cut — it is the payoff that makes the analytical work concrete.

## A Note on Classroom Dynamics

This material tends to generate strong reactions, and the reactions tend to sort by gender in ways that are themselves pedagogically interesting. Female students often respond to the Passage 2 epidural moment with immediate recognition — the disclosure-while-husband-is-out-of-room resonates as a familiar social dynamic. Male students often lead with the "lifestyle choice" objection. Both responses are analytically useful: the first grounds the Leidig analysis in lived experience; the second restates the exact objection that Leidig is writing against. Neither should be shut down.

The most productive classroom discussion tends to come from pairing the two responses directly: "You're both identifying something real. The question is whether both of those things can be true at the same time — and if so, what that means for how we analyze this case."

If discussion feels stuck on the normative question (is this acceptable? is it our place to judge?), redirect to the structural question: "Set aside whether any individual woman should live this way. What does the *genre* do in the information ecosystem? What does it make it easier or harder to say, to believe, to legislate?"
