# Primary Source Handout: British National Party Policy Statements (c. 2010-2016)

**For classroom use -- selected passages with editorial framing**

*The British National Party (BNP) was founded in 1982 by John Tyndall, a veteran of openly neo-Nazi organizations. Under Nick Griffin's leadership from 1999, the party rebranded toward a "cultural nationalist" platform, distancing itself from biological racism while maintaining ethnic exclusion as its core goal. It achieved its electoral peak in 2009 with two European Parliament seats, then collapsed as UKIP absorbed its voter base. These passages are from the BNP's official website, last substantially updated around 2014-2016. They represent the party's own public voice -- not a summary or paraphrase.*

## Passage 1: Immigration

> "Without any vote or debate, successive governments in Britain have introduced the most pernicious and divisive policy since World War II, and transformed our country almost beyond recognition. In towns and cities up and down the country we feel like foreigners in our own country, our traditions derided and our future given away...
>
> Immigration has resulted in ghettos in many towns adding to the threat of Islamisation and breeding home grown terrorism by having introduced a fifth-column of Islamists. Even the most obsessive proponents of Multiculturalism have been forced to admit its disastrous failure. It's no wonder then, that more than 85% of the British public agree with the BNP that Immigration is too high.
>
> Immigration is the greatest threat to our precious and unique British identity. The BNP is the ONLY political party you can trust to stop Immigration. Britain's full and it's time to shut the door!"

And later, making the positive case:

> "We want Britain to remain -- or return to -- the way it has traditionally been. We accept that Britain always will have ethnic minorities and have no problem with this as long as they remain minorities and do not change nor seek to change the fundamental culture and identity of the indigenous peoples of the British Isles.
>
> India would not tolerate millions of non-Indians taking over that society. Pakistan would not tolerate millions of Hindus or Christians entering that country and changing it from a Muslim society into something else. Japan would not do it; China would not do it -- so why should Britain?
>
> Each nation has the right to maintain its own identity... This is all the British National Party seeks for Britain -- the right to be British. This is not an extreme demand -- it is actually just perfectly normal and completely in line with the rights granted to every other nation and with international law."

**[Discussion prompt: The BNP frames its position as globally universal -- every nation has the right to maintain its identity, including India and Pakistan. What does this move accomplish rhetorically? How does "we feel like foreigners in our own country" illustrate Stanley's victimhood tactic -- and Finchelstein's point that the dominant group presents itself as the dispossessed? What is the logical relationship between "we accept that Britain always will have ethnic minorities" and "as long as they remain minorities and do not... change the fundamental culture"?]**

## Passage 2: Democracy

> "The British National Party is proud to be in possession of some of the most modern and progressive concepts of democracy which are firmly at odds with the other parties' increasing totalitarianism.
>
> The British people invented modern Parliamentary democracy. Yet in recent years the British people have been denied their democratic rights. On issue after issue, the views of the majority of British people have been ignored and overridden by a politically correct elite which thinks it knows best.
>
> On immigration, on capital punishment, on the surrender of British sovereignty to the EU and in numerous other areas, democracy has been absent as Labour, Tories and LibDems conspire in election after election to offer the British people no real choice on such vital issues."

Their specific democratic proposals include:

> "-- Abolish 'anti-discrimination' laws which prevent people from making a free choice
> -- Abolish the 'Human Rights Act' which has been imposed on this country through the European Union, and which is nothing but an excuse to prevent British laws stopping the scroungers of the world parasiting off this nation
> -- Abolish all restrictions on traditional free speech; common law provisions against incitement to violence are the only proper limits in a free society"

**[Discussion prompt: The BNP calls itself "Britain's most democratic party" while proposing to abolish anti-discrimination laws and the Human Rights Act. Is this a contradiction, or is there an internally consistent logic here? How does "the British people have been denied their democratic rights" function as a populist claim? Recall Finchelstein: populism asserts that it alone represents "the people" -- who is included in "the British people" in this passage, and who is excluded?]**

## Passage 3: Reversing Islamisation

> "The BNP is implacably opposed to the Labour/Tory regimes' Mass Immigration policy which, if left unchecked, will see Britain and most of Europe colonised by Islam within a few decades.
>
> The BNP recognises that Islam, by its very nature, is incompatible with modern secular Western democracy."

Their proposals include:

> "-- Refuse permission for any further mosques -- including conversion or new build -- because Britain has enough for its Muslim population
> -- Deny permission for any more mosques and minarets -- which are little more than symbols of territorial conquest
> -- Ban the burqa and niqab
> -- Defend our traditional Christian faith
> -- Ban Sharia law
> -- Ensure that existing community buildings such as pubs and churches are not turned into mosques"

**[Discussion prompt: "Colonised by Islam" and "symbols of territorial conquest" are military metaphors applied to religious practice -- mosques as beachheads. How does this framing construct the external enemy? Compare to Breivik's manifesto: what does the BNP share with Breivik's worldview, and what separates them -- not just tactically but in the texture of how they describe the threat? The BNP says Islam is "by its very nature" incompatible with democracy -- is this a cultural claim or a racial one? Does the distinction matter legally? Analytically?]**

## Passage 4: Environment

> "The British National Party is this nation's only true Green party which has policies that will actually save the environment. Unlike the fake 'Greens' who are merely a front for the far left of the Labour regime, the BNP is the ONLY party to recognise that overpopulation -- whose primary driver is immigration, as revealed by the government's own figures -- is the cause of the destruction of our environment...
>
> Britain is one of the most densely populated countries in the world and our population is increasing -- due entirely to immigration -- which necessitates the building of ever more homes, which in turn places a strain on our infrastructure such as transport and water supplies.
>
> Independent environmental organisations believe that Britain's population needs to be significantly reduced. Our immigration policies will achieve this."

**[Discussion prompt: The BNP is positioning itself as the environmentally responsible party by reframing population reduction as conservation. This is known as "ecofascism" or green nationalism -- the idea that immigration, not industrial capitalism, is the root cause of environmental harm. What ideological work does this move do? How does calling the Green Party "a front for the far left" fit the pattern of the far right appropriating issues typically owned by the left? Does a party that proposes to significantly reduce the population through deportation have a credible claim to environmental concern?]**

## Passage 5: British Culture

> "The BNP will protect our unique and precious British identity from Mass Immigration, multi-culturalism, health'n'safety killjoys and globalisation.
>
> We will:
> -- Reassert that British law comes before any other in Britain
> -- Fly the Union Jack from all public government buildings
> -- Make Saint George's Day a national holiday
> -- Uphold our hard-won right to free speech
> -- End public funding of organisations advocating multiculturalism"

**[Discussion prompt: This is the shortest passage but worth pausing on. "Health'n'safety killjoys" sits alongside "Mass Immigration" and "globalisation" in the same list of enemies. What is the underlying logic that connects these grievances -- what is the common thread? Whose Britain is being "protected," and from what? How does this passage illustrate Stanley's "heartland" tactic -- the idea that authentic national values exist somewhere that needs to be defended against cosmopolitan corruption?]**

## Reading Notes for Instructors

**On the "populist" structure:** Every page of BNP policy enacts Finchelstein's "the part becomes the whole" move. The BNP does not claim to represent BNP voters -- it claims to represent "the British people," whose authentic will is being suppressed by conspiring elites. The three mainstream parties "conspire" to deny the British people real choice. The BNP alone speaks for the majority. This is the core populist logic, and it recurs regardless of the specific policy area.

**On welfare chauvinism:** The BNP's policy suite consistently pairs generous in-group provision with explicit exclusion of the out-group. NHS for British citizens, funded by removing "foreigners and other undesirables" from the budget. Environmental protection through population reduction via deportation. This is not a contradiction in their logic -- it is the logic. Social provision and ethnic exclusion are the same policy stated from different angles. This is the Volksgemeinschaft structure in contemporary form.

**On the global relativism argument:** The India/Pakistan/Japan/China comparison is rhetorically clever and worth careful analysis. It appropriates anti-imperialist language ("every nation has a right to self-determination") for ethnic exclusion. It invites students to either accept the symmetry (and thereby accept the BNP's framing) or explain why it is not symmetric. Good question: is there a difference between India's right to remain Indian and Britain's right to remain British? What would ground that difference? This is a genuinely difficult question worth letting students sit with.

**On the BNP-to-mainstream trajectory:** The BNP's specific policy positions -- leaving the EU, stopping immigration, banning the burqa, defending "British culture" against multiculturalism -- were considered extremist in 2005. By 2024, most of them had been adopted in some form by the Conservative Party. This is the normalization trajectory Camus and Lebourg describe. Worth asking: does a position become less far-right when it becomes mainstream, or does the mainstream become more far-right?

**On what to assign vs. discuss:** The Islamisation page has the most analytically rich material (minarets as "territorial conquest") but also risks becoming a catalogue of anti-Muslim positions that generates more heat than light. The Immigration and Democracy pages are probably the most productive for structured discussion because they show the populist logic most clearly and connect most directly to the week's analytical frameworks.
