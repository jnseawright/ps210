# Primary Source Handout: Bundyville — Selected Transcript Excerpts (2018)

**For classroom use — selected passages with editorial framing**

*"Bundyville" is a seven-part podcast series produced by Oregon Public Broadcasting and Longreads, reported by journalist Leah Sottile and first released in August 2018. It investigates the Bundy family — Nevada rancher Cliven Bundy and his sons, particularly Ammon and Ryan — and the anti-federal-government movement they came to symbolize. The excerpts below are drawn from the transcript of Episode 1 and focus on the movement's ideology, organizational dynamics, and relationship to broader currents of the American far right. Cliven Bundy's 2014 standoff with the Bureau of Land Management at his Nevada ranch, and Ammon Bundy's 2016 armed occupation of the Malheur National Wildlife Refuge in Oregon, are the events the podcast reconstructs.*

*The Bundy movement draws on at least three distinct ideological traditions: the "sovereign citizen" movement (which holds that federal authority over individuals and land is constitutionally illegitimate), the Sagebrush Rebellion (a decades-long conflict between Western ranchers and federal land management agencies), and the militia/Patriot movement documented in Van Dyke and Soule (2002). The Bundys are also members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, whose theological and historical relationship to federal authority is complex — a dimension Bundyville develops in later episodes.*

## Passage 1: "I Don't Recognize the United States Government as Even Existing"

Cliven Bundy stops paying federal grazing fees in 1993, refuses buyouts accepted by every other rancher in the county, and accumulates over a million dollars in unpaid fees over two decades. He explains his position:

> "We run cattle here, graze this natural resource off this public land, and I abide by all of Nevada state law, but I don't recognize the United States government as even existing."

And, when confronted about trespassing on federal land:

> "Who is the trespasser here? Who is the one out here with 200 armed agency surrounding my home and my ranch and parts of Clark County? Is Cliven Bundy doing those things or is the United States government doing these things?"

*— Cliven Bundy, quoted in Leah Sottile, "Bundyville" Episode 1 (2018)*

**[Discussion prompt: "I don't recognize the United States government as even existing" is an extraordinary statement from a U.S. citizen on U.S. soil in the 21st century. What kind of claim is it — legal, philosophical, religious, performative? Compare it to the John Birch Society's arguments from Dallek Chapter 1: Welch and his colleagues believed the federal government had been captured by communist conspirators. The Bundys believe the federal government has no legitimate authority over state land. Both positions hold that the existing government is not the real government — that authentic American sovereignty lies elsewhere. Where does each locate it? How are these related, and how are they different?]**

## Passage 2: The Legal Argument — and Its Limits

Reporter Leah Sottile explains the constitutional theory the Bundys deploy:

> "If you're around the Bundys and their supporters for any amount of time, they'll hand you a pocket-size copy of the Constitution and they'll tell you about their favorite part, Article one, section eight, clause 17... Legal scholars call it the Enclave Clause and it basically says the government needs to get state permission to use land outside of Washington, DC. The Bundys claim, even when they have that permission, the feds can only build things like forts and military dockyards. The problem with this interpretation is that it completely ignores the rest of the Constitution. There are other clauses in long-settled Supreme Court cases that say the government definitely can own land."
>
> "The other ingredient here is sovereign citizen ideology. Sovereign citizens believe they are independent nations unto themselves, and when you're your own nation, you don't have to abide by the country's laws. Federal courts aren't valid. Federal judges aren't real judges. Things like taxes and grazing fees to federal agencies don't apply to you."

*— Leah Sottile, "Bundyville" Episode 1 (2018)*

**[Discussion prompt: The Bundys' constitutional argument is legally incorrect by any mainstream reading — federal land ownership in the West has been upheld in settled Supreme Court precedent for over a century. But the argument functions. It recruits supporters, inspires militia mobilization, and — crucially — produced jury acquittals in Oregon. What does it mean for a legal argument to "work" in a political movement even when it is legally wrong? Compare this to the JBS's conspiracist arguments from Dallek: Welch's claim that Eisenhower was "a dedicated, conscious agent of the communist conspiracy" was factually absurd, yet it organized thousands of members and built a durable donor network. What is the function of unfalsifiable or demonstrably false belief systems in far-right movements?]**

## Passage 3: Militia Mobilization — Who Answers the Call

The 2014 standoff draws armed supporters from across the country:

> "People from across the country with all kinds of gripes with the government had picked up on the Bundy story through right-wing media. Many of them didn't know the first thing about raising livestock. They were just looking for a fight."
>
> One early arrival, militia trainer James Yeager, had posted a video threatening civil war over proposed gun restrictions: "I'm telling you that if that happens, it's going to spark a civil war and I'll be glad to fire the first shot. I'm not letting anybody take my guns. If it goes one inch further, I'm going to start killing people."
>
> "Militia's, like the Three Percenters and the Oath Keepers, assemble at the ranch."

*— Leah Sottile, "Bundyville" Episode 1 (2018)*

**[Discussion prompt: Van Dyke and Soule (2002) identified the predictors of militia/Patriot movement organizing in the 1990s: economic restructuring (loss of manufacturing and farming), demographic threat, and anti-"New World Order" ideology. The Bundy case is from 2014, eighteen years later. Does the Van Dyke/Soule model still explain who shows up at Bundy Ranch — or does the arrival of urban gun-rights activists, militia members from other states, and right-wing media figures suggest something different is going on? What role does right-wing media play in the 2014 mobilization that probably wasn't as available in the 1990s context Van Dyke and Soule studied?]**

## Passage 4: "The First Patriot of the Second American Revolution"

Among the Bundy supporters, a man named Gary Hunt discusses Timothy McVeigh, who killed 168 people in the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing of a federal building:

> "He's the first one to actually take an active action against the United States government. It was a government building, and in that respect, he is the first patriot of the second American revolution. Not that I agree with it, but from an objective observation, I give him credit for doing what other people had talked about."

Sottile contextualizes:

> "The Las Vegas federal courthouse... is the first building to comply with blast-resistant codes put in place after the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing. That was the work of an anti-government extremist named Timothy McVeigh, and it left 168 people dead... Within their ranks of followers, you will find people who agree with what Timothy McVeigh did back in 1995."

*— "Bundyville" Episode 1 (2018)*

**[Discussion prompt: The language of "patriot" is doing a lot of work here: a man who murdered 168 people, 19 of them children, is being framed as a revolutionary hero. This is the "accelerationist" argument in its most elemental form — violence against the state as a sacred act (compare Week 6's Aarons reading on accelerationism). But note the hedge: "Not that I agree with it." This qualified endorsement is itself analytically interesting. What does the hedge accomplish? Who is the intended audience? Compare this to the Proud Boys' "We Are Not Alt-Right" post: the Proud Boys explicitly expel white nationalists, but do so in a way that keeps those people in view as reference points. How do movements manage the relationship between their more extreme and their more mainstream members?]**

## Passage 5: "The Death of Rural America" — The Jury's Frame

After the Malheur occupation trial, an Oregon juror explains why the Bundys were acquitted despite the government's documentary evidence:

> "This case is about the death of rural America. That has got me thinking, if your farmers don't have the ability necessarily to prosper in those venues in which they're going to raise, whether it's logging or animal stuff... It's just got me thinking a lot about that."
>
> Kevin Sali, a defense attorney who observed the trial, reflects: "One of the choices you always have to make as a lawyer when you're facing a counter-argument is, how much do you want to engage, and thereby dignify that argument, as opposed to dismissing it?"
>
> "So they hid it away. They didn't want to talk about the Bundys' disproven views on the Constitution, or how many years they'd been working the land. Ammon Bundy sat on the stand for three days talking, telling jurors his family's way of life was under attack. When it came time for the prosecutors to cross-examine him, they questioned him for less than 15 minutes."

*— Leah Sottile, "Bundyville" Episode 1 (2018)*

**[Discussion prompt: The juror doesn't say "I believe Cliven Bundy owns the land." The juror says "This case is about the death of rural America." The Bundys won not on their constitutional theory but on a narrative — a story about who is disappearing and why. Van Dyke and Soule found that the strongest predictor of militia organizing was the loss of manufacturing jobs and farms — not general economic decline but sectoral disruption. The juror is articulating exactly that grievance. How should we think about the relationship between economic restructuring as a social movement driver (Van Dyke/Soule) and the legal arena where that grievance gets translated into jury nullification? Is the juror's sympathy with "rural America" a political position, an economic one, or something else?]**

## Passage 6: Ammon Bundy's Call to Occupation

After the protest march in Burns, Oregon, Ammon Bundy makes his pitch:

> "Those that know what's going on here and have seen it for many, many, many, many years, those who are ready to actually do something about it, I'm asking you to follow me and go to the Malheur National Wildlife Refuge, and we're going to make a hard stand."
>
> He frames the government's land ownership as a conspiracy: "It's crucial that you understand what's going on here... It's about our federal government taking over private properties adversely or using the taxpayers' dollars after they ruined the ranchers to buy it... If they can get control of the land and resources, if they can control, for example, the water, then they can have full control over the people."

*— Ammon Bundy, quoted in Leah Sottile, "Bundyville" Episode 1 (2018)*

**[Discussion prompt: Ammon Bundy's framing is explicitly conspiratorial: federal land management is a strategy for "full control over the people." Compare this to Robert Welch's JBS argument that communists had captured the federal government as a prelude to totalitarian control. Both arguments hold that bureaucratic-administrative power (a federal agency in Bundy's case; the State Department, IRS, and CIA in Welch's) is a cover for an agenda of total domination. The enemy in each case is not just policy but a hidden plan. What does the conspiracist framing add to a political movement that a straightforward policy argument would not? What does Camus and Lebourg's concept of normalization suggest about what happens when these conspiratorial arguments enter the mainstream?]**

## Reading Notes for Instructors

**On the diversity of the US far right:** The Bundy movement occupies a distinct ecological niche that differs from all three Week 3 cases. The KKK used organized terror against Black political participation — explicitly racial, explicitly anti-democratic (Byman). The militia/Patriot movement documented by Van Dyke and Soule was primarily reactive to economic restructuring and coded as white supremacist without always being explicitly so. The JBS was a donor-organized, elite-funded anti-communist network with a conspiratorial worldview (Dallek). The Bundy movement combines elements of all three — militia mobilization, conspiratorial anti-government ideology, and a rural Western cultural identity — but adds something distinctive: a specific, highly local, historically grounded grievance about federal land in the American West that has no real European parallel. This is a movement you could not export.

**On the Civil War / Reconstruction link:** The states' rights argument — that the federal government has no legitimate authority over land within state borders — is the structural descendant of the Calhoun/Lost Cause argument that federal authority cannot override the sovereignty of individual states. Byman shows that the original KKK's core demand was to reverse federal enforcement of Reconstruction — to reassert state authority against the 14th Amendment and the Enforcement Acts. The Bundy movement's Enclave Clause argument is the same underlying claim in a different legal register: federal power over this territory is constitutionally illegitimate. The argument has migrated from race to land, but the structure — authentic local sovereignty vs. tyrannical federal overreach — is remarkably stable across 150 years.

**On US/European comparisons:** The Bundy movement is arguably the most distinctly American case we encounter in the entire course. It is built on:
- The specific institutional history of federal land ownership in the American West (the BLM manages land that has no European administrative equivalent)
- A theological underpinning from Latter-day Saint theology and its complicated history with federal authority (polygamy prosecutions, the Utah War)
- The mythologized frontier identity of the American West — cowboys, independence, the open range — that functions differently from European nationalist myths of ancestral soil

European far-right movements have their own rural dimensions (Farmer protests in the Netherlands, Front National's rural French vote), but these are not built on a claim of constitutional illegitimacy of the central state's land ownership. The absence of a European parallel is itself analytically interesting: it suggests that at least some strands of the American far right are deeply embedded in peculiarly American institutions and cannot be explained by general theories of far-right grievance.

**On the carrot-stick framework (Spenkuch & Tillmann, Week 2):** The government's prosecutorial strategy — ignore the constitutional argument, present factual evidence, secure convictions — failed. The acquittals in Oregon and the mistrial in Nevada suggest that the "stick" without an acceptable alternative narrative lost to the Bundy movement's "carrot" of authentic rural American identity. The jury did not accept the government's framing. Worth asking students: what would the "carrot" equivalent look like in a land-rights dispute?

**On media and mobilization (bridging to Week 4):** The 2014 standoff was mobilized, in part, through right-wing media — YouTubers, bloggers, the emerging right-wing media ecosystem that would later include Fox News prime time and eventually social media influencers. This is a transition case from the 1990s militia movement (which mobilized through shortwave radio and physical networks) to the contemporary far right (which mobilizes through social media). The Bundy case is a useful bridge to Week 4's readings on internet and far-right mobilization.
