# Primary Source Handout: English Defence League -- Mission Statement and Policy Documents (2016)

**For classroom use -- selected passages with editorial framing**

*The English Defence League (EDL) was founded in 2009 in Luton, England, by Tommy Robinson (born Stephen Yaxley-Lennon) and Kevin Carroll, following a counter-protest against a small Islamist demonstration at a homecoming parade for British soldiers returned from Afghanistan. Unlike the BNP, the EDL was never an electoral party: it was a street protest movement, organizing demonstrations across English towns and cities, typically in areas with large Muslim populations. At its peak in 2010-2011 it drew thousands to demonstrations; it subsequently declined sharply, and Robinson resigned in 2013. The website texts below were approved for release by the EDL Committee in January 2016, when the organization had contracted to a rump. The Q&A section dates from November 2016. These documents represent the EDL's own carefully curated public voice.*

*A note on organizational form: the BNP sought votes; the EDL sought bodies in the street. This difference in organizational form shaped everything -- how they recruited, what they said, who they attracted, and how the state responded to them.*

## Passage 1: The EDL as a Human Rights Organization

*From the Mission Statement, Section 2:*

> "The English Defence League is a human rights organisation.
>
> We stand for free speech in practice -- The EDL argues for the rights and ability of all people to protest against Islam's encroachment into the lives of non-Muslims. We also reject the unjust assumption that all Muslims are complicit in or somehow responsible for the actions of other Muslims. So we stand firm in support of the right of both Muslims and non-Muslims to speak freely about these actions and encroachments.
>
> We stand for free speech in the law -- The EDL calls for the repeal of legislation that prevents effective freedom of speech. Freedom of speech under legal protection is essential if human rights abuses that may be associated with Islam are to be named, exposed, discussed, criticised, stopped and prevented.
>
> We stand for the right of British Muslims to speak freely about problems deriving from Islam -- We recognise that Muslims themselves are often the victims of some Islamic traditions and practices. Our laws, officials and institutions should protect the human rights of individual British Muslims to openly criticise Islamic orthodoxy, challenge Islamic leaders without fear of retribution, receive full equality before the law (including equal rights for Muslim women), demand reform of their religion, and leave Islam if they see fit, without fear of censure."

**[Discussion prompt: The EDL positions itself as defending Muslims from Islam -- protecting Muslim women from patriarchy, Muslim apostates from retribution, Muslim reformers from conservative orthodoxy. What does this framing accomplish? Is it possible to distinguish a genuine concern for the rights of Muslim individuals from a rhetorical move that instrumentalizes those individuals? The BNP's framework is explicitly ethnic: "indigenous peoples of the British Isles." The EDL's framework is explicitly ideological: the enemy is Islam as a belief system, not Muslims as a group. What are the practical and analytical differences between these two forms of exclusion?]**

## Passage 2: "We Refuse to Tolerate the Intolerant"

*From the Mission Statement, Section 5:*

> "We refuse to tolerate the intolerant -- The EDL welcomes support from people of all races, all faiths, all political persuasions, and all legal lifestyle choices. Under our umbrella, all people in England, whatever their background or origin, can stand united in a desire to stop the imposition of the demands of Islam on non-believers. In order to ensure the continuity of our culture and its institutions, the EDL stands opposed to the creeping Islamisation of our country."

And from the Mission Statement, Section 1 (on the founding):

> "The English Defence League has risen from the English working class to act, lead and inspire in the struggle against global Islamification -- The EDL was founded in the wake of the shocking actions of Muslim extremists who, at a regimental homecoming parade in Luton, openly mocked the sacrifices of our service personnel... Examples include: denigration and oppression of women, organised sexual abuse of children, female genital mutilation, so-called honour killings, homophobia, racism, anti-Semitism, demanding separate schools, the preference for sharia law over English law, intolerance of non-Muslims, unceasing demands that English society change to accommodate Muslim practices and preferences, and continued support for terrorist ideology and even terrorist atrocities."

**[Discussion prompt: "We refuse to tolerate the intolerant" is a direct citation of Karl Popper's "paradox of tolerance" -- Popper's 1945 argument that a tolerant society must be intolerant of intolerance to preserve itself. Notice that the EDL claims to welcome "all races, all faiths, all political persuasions" in the same breath as calling for opposition to Islam specifically. What holds these two claims together -- and what tension remains? The founding list of grievances (honour killings, homophobia, anti-Semitism) mixes genuine human rights abuses with assertions about "unceasing demands." How does lumping them together shape the analysis? Is the list falsifiable?]**

## Passage 3: The Anti-Sharia Strategy

*From "A national anti-sharia strategy to reduce the Islamist threat" (website policy page, undated):*

> "No one in the UK is putting forward a coherent, detailed program to protect our country from sharia and the excesses of Islam.
>
> The English Defence League is therefore proposing a 'UK Anti-Sharia Strategy' to fill this yawning gap."

Their 17-point strategy includes:

> "-- Refusing any recognition of sharia courts and councils
> -- Banning the wearing of burkas and niqabs in public places
> -- Reinstitute the sedition law and close mosques and organisations that promote sedition
> -- Ceasing state aid for Islamic faith schools and stopping Islamic home schooling
> -- Banning all new mosque construction and expansion
> -- Ceasing foreign funding of mosques, madrasas, schools, Islamic organisations
> -- Stopping amplified public calls to prayer
> -- Opening all mosque services and meetings to monitoring by an 'Ofsted for mosques'
> -- Freeing employers from any obligation to pay wages for time used for prayer or other religious observance during work hours, or to provide facilities or leave for religious observance
> -- Allowing criticism, parody and ridiculing of Islam, its scriptures and advocates to be as unimpeded as is criticism, parody and ridiculing of other ideologies and belief systems."

The document concludes:

> "Conclusion -- Islamism and sharia are real threats to the UK and the West... We argue that the British people need to put up deliberate and effective defences against sharia now. Our anti-sharia strategy will make the UK a less favourable environment for expansionist Islamists, while not impeding the faith observance of Muslims who are content to live in British society without demanding it become more and more Islamic."

**[Discussion prompt: The document distinguishes throughout between "Islamists" (the target) and ordinary Muslims (not the target) -- and yet the 17-point list bans mosque construction, ends prayer-time accommodation at work, and subjects all mosque services to state monitoring. Evaluate the consistency of this distinction. The framing "make the UK a less favourable environment" echoes immigration enforcement language -- note that the strategy explicitly says it "avoids restricting Muslim entry into the UK" because it's "impracticable." What does the word "impracticable" rather than "unjust" reveal about the underlying logic? How does this strategy compare to the BNP's Islamisation proposals?]**

## Passage 4: English Culture and Its Enemies

*From "We are proud of English culture!" (website post, October 2016):*

> "People on the political, international left, especially the young, Marxist revolutionaries and romantic utopians, proclaim that there is no such thing as English culture. Or they dismiss English culture as being expressed only through quaint minority hobbies like Morris dancing and bird watching.
>
> Here is a list of achievements that are either distinctively English, or else they are 'Helleno-Judeo-Christian' in origin and then shaped and combined and made into the foundation of Western civilisation by the English..."

The list includes: the freedom of the individual, habeas corpus, common law, representative democracy, freedom of expression, critical thinking, dispassionate inquiry, civil society, science and innovation, the English language and its literature, Christian heritage, folk and choral music.

> "English culture is opposed by Islam -- We don't see a lot of support for these in Islamic scripture, nor in the public participation by British Muslims in English civil society. What's more, in no Islamic society in the world are any of these features given priority. On this basis it is close to certainty that, as Britain becomes more Islamified, we stand to lose each of these -- and to lose the whole which is greater than the sum of its parts."

**[Discussion prompt: The EDL's list of English cultural achievements is largely a list of Enlightenment values: habeas corpus, free expression, critical thinking, dispassionate inquiry. Notice what the list does not include -- Empire, class hierarchy, ethnic homogeneity, the things a racial nationalist might put first. This is the EDL's distinctive move: appropriating liberal, universalist values as the content of cultural particularism. Is this coherent? Can Enlightenment values be the property of a particular culture in a way that justifies excluding others? The passage ends with "it is close to certainty that, as Britain becomes more Islamified, we stand to lose each of these." Evaluate this claim as an empirical prediction. What evidence would be required?]**

## Reading Notes for Instructors

**On the shift from ethnic to civilizational nationalism:** The BNP's core logic is ethnic: there are "indigenous peoples of the British Isles" whose demographic and cultural majority must be preserved. The EDL's core logic is civilizational: there is a "Judeo-Christian" Western civilization whose values (Enlightenment liberalism, rule of law, free expression) are threatened by a specific incompatible ideology (Islam). The EDL explicitly says it welcomes people of all races and even welcomes Muslims who want to leave or reform Islam. This shift is politically significant -- it is harder to prosecute under racial incitement laws, harder to dismiss as simple racism, and potentially more recruitable across ethnic lines.

**On the Popper appropriation:** The "tolerate the tolerant, not the intolerant" move recurs constantly in EDL rhetoric. Popper's actual argument in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) was narrower: a tolerant society should suppress *calls to violence*, not beliefs it finds incompatible. The EDL expands this to suppress an entire religion's public expression. Worth asking students: does Popper's paradox justify this extension, or is the EDL using liberal philosophical authority for illiberal ends?

**On the street movement form:** The EDL is analytically important as a model of **mobilization without electoral ambition**. It demonstrated that a far-right mass movement could be sustained through demonstration-based organizing rather than party-building, that it could recruit across class and racial lines (the EDL had Black and Sikh members at various points), and that its social media presence could sustain it through periods when street activity waned. This model influenced subsequent groups across Europe and North America.

**On the relationship to Breivik:** Breivik cited the EDL and Counterjihad blogosphere extensively. He shared their civilizational frame entirely -- the enemy is "cultural Marxism" enabling Islam, not racial diversity as such. But he concluded that street protest and politics were futile and turned to mass violence. This is the radicalization trajectory in compressed form: from EDL's "we are loud and proud, but then we go back home to our families" to Breivik's "the democratical struggle to save Europe has been lost." Neither the BNP nor the EDL endorsed Breivik; the EDL explicitly condemned the attacks. But the ideological materials are largely shared.

**On what to assign vs. discuss:** The Anti-Sharia Strategy is the richest analytical document because it shows the gap between the stated distinction ("we're targeting Islamists, not Muslims") and the operational proposals (which target Muslim religious practice broadly). The English Culture passage generates the most philosophical discussion because it raises questions about whether liberalism can be the content of nationalism. These two passages together, with the "human rights" framing, give a complete picture of the EDL's rhetorical architecture.
