# Week 1 Session Notes: What Is Fascism?
### The Politics of the Far Right

**Week 1 readings:**
- Part 1 (Session A): Stanley, Jason. *How Fascism Works: The Politics of Us and Them*. Chapters 1--5.
- Part 2 (Session B): Finchelstein, Federico. *From Fascism to Populism in History*. Chapters 1--2.

---

## Instructor Framing (Both Sessions)

Week 1 establishes the analytical architecture for the entire course. By the end of the two sessions, students should be able to articulate two competing frameworks -- Stanley's tactical account of fascism and Finchelstein's historical-genealogical account of populism -- apply them to primary source material, and understand why the disagreement between the two frameworks is productive rather than merely definitional.

**The core intellectual tension:** Stanley treats fascism as a set of recurring tactics deployable in any democratic context; Finchelstein treats it as a specific historical phenomenon with a precise genealogy and a postwar successor (populism) that is meaningfully different. These frameworks often point to the same contemporary movements but make different predictions about what those movements are, how dangerous they are, and what response is appropriate.

The two sessions are designed to introduce one framework per session, with primary source application in each, and a full synthesis in Session 1B. The opening diagnostic in Session 1A should not be resolved until the closing of Session 1B.

# Session 1A: Stanley and the Tactics of Fascist Politics

**Assigned reading:** Stanley, *How Fascism Works*, Chapters 1--5
*(The Mythic Past, Propaganda, Anti-Intellectualism, Unreality, Hierarchy)*

**Session goal:** Students leave with a clear command of Stanley's tactical framework as introduced in the chapters assigned for today -- specifically the first five of his ten tactics -- and have begun applying it to contemporary primary source material. The full ten-tactic framework is previewed in the Introduction; today's deep dive is on Chapters 1--5.

---

## Session 1A Architecture (75 minutes)

### 1. Opening diagnostic (10 min)

Before any lecture, put a simple question on the board:

> *"Write one sentence: What is fascism?"*

Give students 2 minutes to write, then cold-call 4--5 students. Don't evaluate their answers -- just collect them on the board. You'll return to these at the close of Session 1B.

**Why this works:** It surfaces the folk theory most students carry in -- usually something like "fascism = extreme nationalism + violence + Hitler/Mussolini." This makes the subsequent complication feel earned rather than pedantic. The contrast between today's starting definition and what they'll be able to say at the end of Session 1B is itself the pedagogical payoff.

---

### 2. Mini-lecture: Stanley's framework (25 min)

**Start with the Introduction's overview (5 min):**

Stanley is not interested in fascism as a finished ideology you can look up in a dictionary. He is interested in **fascist politics as a tactical toolkit** -- a set of moves that politicians use to accumulate power by dividing a population into "us" and "them." He identifies ten recurring tactics. Flag to students that the full list appears in the Introduction; today's assigned chapters cover the first five. It is worth briefly listing all ten so students see where the course is going:

1. The Mythic Past
2. Propaganda
3. Anti-Intellectualism
4. Unreality
5. Hierarchy
6. Victimhood
7. Law and Order
8. Sexual Anxiety
9. Heartland / Sodom and Gomorrah
10. Dismantling Public Welfare

Key quote to put on the board:
> "My interest in this book is in fascist politics. Specifically, my interest is in fascist tactics as a mechanism to achieve power... Fascist politics does not necessarily lead to an explicitly fascist state, but it is dangerous nonetheless." (Introduction)

**Then take Chapters 1--5 in sequence (20 min):**

**Chapter 1: The Mythic Past.** A glorious national past that was stolen; the goal is restoration, not progress. The mythic past doesn't need to be historically accurate -- it needs to be emotionally compelling and grievance-generating. Something was taken from "us" that we are owed back. This is what distinguishes fascist nostalgia from ordinary conservatism: the past was not just better, it was robbed by a specific enemy. *Ask students*: What is the "us" whose past was stolen? How does the movement define it?

**Chapter 2: Propaganda.** Propaganda in Stanley's account is not primarily about persuading people to believe false things. It is about mobilizing emotion in ways that bypass rational evaluation entirely. The propagandist doesn't argue -- they trigger. This is why fact-checking often fails: you are bringing a rational instrument to an emotional problem. Put Welch's Huxley line on the board (you'll return to it in Session 2A): *"The propagandist is a man who canalises an already existing stream."* Fascist propaganda works with grievances that already exist; it gives them direction and an enemy.

**Chapter 3: Anti-Intellectualism.** Universities, scientists, journalists, and experts are presented as a corrupt elite out of touch with the "real" people. Expertise is reframed as ideology; evidence-based reasoning is reframed as politically motivated. The effect is not just to discredit particular claims but to establish the principle that lived feeling and commonsense outrank technical knowledge. This is important structurally: if you can get people to distrust all institutional knowledge-production, the charismatic leader's personal authority becomes the only alternative truth-anchor.

**Chapter 4: Unreality.** Where Chapter 3 attacks the *producers* of knowledge, Chapter 4 attacks the *shared epistemic ground* itself. Conspiracy theories, deliberate false information, and the promotion of mutually contradictory claims work not to convince but to exhaust. When people can't distinguish true from false across a sustained assault, they stop trying. "Nothing is real" is not a neutral conclusion -- it is a precondition for authoritarianism, because it makes the strong man's personal assertion the only available certainty.

**Chapter 5: Hierarchy.** Fascist politics insists that some people are naturally superior and others naturally inferior -- by race, religion, national origin, or gender. The democratic claim of equal human worth is not just wrong in the fascist worldview but unnatural, a lie invented by elites to corrupt the proper order. This chapter is important for the course because it is where fascist politics most clearly departs from ordinary conservative politics: most conservatives defend inequality of outcome but accept equality of status; fascist politics rejects even the latter.

**The "I am your voice" move** (from Stanley's Introduction): Trump's 2016 RNC line is Stanley's example of what he calls the defining gesture of fascist politics. The leader doesn't *represent* the people -- the leader *is* the people. This collapses the distinction between ruler and ruled that liberal democracy depends on. Note it here; it will recur across many weeks' primary sources.

### 3. Primary source analysis: Project 2025 (25 min)

**Setup (2 min):** Briefly explain what Project 2025 is: a 900-page governing blueprint prepared by the Heritage Foundation and roughly 100 conservative organizations for a future Republican president, released in 2023. The foreword is by Kevin D. Roberts, Heritage Foundation president. This is your analytical test case for Stanley's framework.

**Distribute or display these three passages, which map directly onto Chapters 1--3:**

---

**Passage A -- The Mythic Past (Chapter 1):**

> "In 1979, the threats we faced were the Soviet Union, the socialism of 1970s liberals, and the predatory deviancy of cultural elites. Reagan defeated these beasts by ignoring their tentacles and striking instead at their hearts... This is the duty history has put before us and the standard by which our generation of conservatives will be judged."

---

**Passage B -- Anti-Intellectualism (Chapter 3):**

> "Today, nearly every top-tier U.S. university president or Wall Street hedge fund manager has more in common with a socialist, European head of state than with the parents at a high school football game in Waco, Texas. Many elites' entire identity, it seems, is wrapped up in their sense of superiority over those people. But under our Constitution, they are the mere equals of the workers who shower after work instead of before."

---

**Passage D -- Unreality / Administrative State (Chapters 3--4):**

> "how to fire supposedly 'un-fireable' federal bureaucrats; how to shutter wasteful and corrupt bureaus and offices; how to muzzle woke propaganda at every level of government... bring the Administrative State to heel, and in the process defang and defund the woke culture warriors who have infiltrated every last institution in America."

---

**Small-group analysis (12 min):** Groups of 3--4, each takes one or two passages:
1. Which of Stanley's five assigned chapters does this passage most clearly illustrate?
2. What specific *move* is being made -- who is "us," who is "them," what is the grievance?
3. Is anything missing from this passage that you'd expect if Stanley's framework is right?

**Full-group debrief (11 min):** Go passage by passage. Focus on pinning the analysis to the specific chapters assigned rather than the full 10-tactic framework.

---

### 4. Preview of the Finchelstein tension (10 min)

Before closing Session 1A, plant the question that Session 1B will answer:

> "Stanley's framework gives us tools to identify fascist *tactics*. But does finding those tactics mean we're looking at *fascism*? A Heritage Foundation policy document is, after all, a presidential transition plan for an administration that will take power through an election. Is that fascism? Or is it something else?"

Introduce Finchelstein as the scholar who would push back: he would say we're looking at *populism* -- fascism's democratic reformulation -- which is dangerous in its own right but analytically different. Session 1B will develop that argument. Save the Project 2025 Passages C and E for Session 1B, where they will be revisited through Finchelstein's lens.

**Don't resolve the question.** End on the tension, not the answer.

---

### 5. Closing (5 min)

Brief housekeeping. Tell students that Session 1B takes up Finchelstein's Chapters 1--2, which are dense and historically specific -- it will help to come with a clear sense of what Finchelstein thinks fascism *was* historically, before asking what it became.

# Session 1B: Finchelstein and the Populism Distinction

**Assigned reading:** Finchelstein, *From Fascism to Populism in History*, Chapters 1--2
*(What Is Fascism in History? / What Is Populism in History?)*

**Session goal:** Students leave with a clear grasp of Finchelstein's historical argument -- fascism as a specific 20th-century phenomenon and populism as its postwar democratic reformulation -- and have applied it to primary source material in dialogue with Stanley's framework. The Le Pen ad is the session's analytical centerpiece.

---

## Session 1B Architecture (75 minutes)

### 1. Bridge from Session 1A (5 min)

Briefly recall the tension planted at the end of 1A: Stanley gives us tactics, but does finding tactics mean finding fascism? Today's session develops Finchelstein's answer -- which is "not necessarily" -- and asks students to work out what's at stake in that distinction.

---

### 2. Mini-lecture: Finchelstein, Chapters 1--2 (22 min)

**Chapter 1: What Is Fascism in History? (10 min)**

Finchelstein wants to preserve the historical specificity of fascism as a term. Fascism has:
- A **birth date**: Italy, 1919 (Mussolini's *Fasci di Combattimento*)
- A **specific ideology**: anti-Enlightenment counter-revolution; rejection of liberal democracy *and* socialist internationalism; violence as a political value in itself, not just a means
- A **totalitarian ambition**: not just winning elections but eliminating the distinction between state and society, absorbing civic life entirely into the movement
- A **transnational dimension**: fascism spread from Italy to Germany and across Europe, but it was genuinely internationalist in its inspiration even while being nationalist in its content

Key quote:
> "Fascism represented a radical and violent reaction against liberalism and socialism. It sought to destroy their basis in Enlightenment reason and universal human rights." (Chapter 1)

The critical point for the course: **Fascism ended in military defeat in 1945.** That defeat was not just political -- it was moral and civilizational. After the Holocaust, no movement could openly embrace the core fascist positions and expect mass support. This is the problem that produces populism.

**Chapter 2: What Is Populism in History? (12 min)**

Finchelstein's argument: when fascists lost the war, they had to adapt. The key move was to **accept elections while preserving the authoritarian logic**. This is populism.

The founding case: **Juan Perón's Argentina** (1946). Perón combines a charismatic leader who claims to embody the authentic people, nationalist exclusion of "anti-Argentine" opponents, anti-liberal and anti-communist framing -- but he wins through elections and governs through a kind of democratic legitimacy. This is not fascism. But it is not liberal democracy either. Finchelstein calls it **authoritarian democracy**.

Key concepts:
- **"The part becomes the whole"**: The populist leader and their movement claim to represent not just their constituency but the entire authentic nation. Everyone who opposes them is, by definition, not really "the people." This is not a claim to represent a majority; it is a claim to *be* the people.
- **Authoritarian democracy**: democratic in form (elections, constitutions, popular legitimacy) while eroding democratic substance (independent courts, free press, minority rights, opposition).
- **The crucial distinction**: Fascism says *we will destroy our opponents by any means*. Populism says *we will defeat our opponents at the ballot box and then use democratic legitimacy to entrench our power*. This distinction matters for predicting what these movements do and what can stop them.

Key quote:
> "Populism constantly changes, but the fundamentals remain. Since its postwar ascent to power, populism has asserted a democratic third way between liberalism and socialism. In that context, fascism became populism in history." (Chapter 2)

**Finchelstein's warning about the contemporary moment:** He is not reassuring. The new 21st-century right-wing populism, he writes, "is less defensive about its nationalism and racism" and "remains close to the interwar past" in ways that mid-20th-century populism didn't. So the populism/fascism distinction is analytical, not a verdict of safety.

**The key diagnostic question**: Is there a moment at which electoral defeat would be accepted? That's the line between populism and fascism in Finchelstein's account. Populism uses elections; fascism, when it loses elections, rejects them.

### 3. Primary source analysis: Marine Le Pen, "Au nom du peuple" (2017) (30 min)

**Video:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FYWnuQc5mYA
**Runtime:** 2 min 16 sec | French with English subtitles

**Cold screening (5 min):** Show the ad once without framing. Ask students to jot down: what is this ad doing? What feelings does it produce? What is the central claim?

Collect 3--4 first impressions. Note them on the board without evaluating.

**Structured re-screening with framework questions (5 min):** Show it again. Ask students to watch specifically for: (a) where Stanley's five tactics appear; (b) where Finchelstein's "part becomes the whole" move appears.

**Discussion (20 min):**

*Finchelstein's framework on this ad:*

"In the name of the people" is Finchelstein's central concept literally in the title. The entire ad builds structurally toward that final line. Le Pen opens as a private individual ("I am a woman, I am a mother, I am a lawyer"), expands to intimate identification with France ("I suffer insults to France as if they were addressed to me directly"), and then in the final seconds collapses the distance between herself and the entire nation: "in your name... in the name of the people." The populist claim is not "I represent the French people" -- it is "speaking for myself is speaking for you, because I am France." Walk students through the structure: this is a two-minute enactment of the argument Finchelstein makes abstractly.

*Stanley's framework on this ad:*

- **Mythic Past (Ch. 1)**: "this age-old nation which cannot be subdued" -- France as ancient, eternal, now under threat
- **Propaganda (Ch. 2)**: the ad operates entirely in the register of affect and identification -- there are no policy proposals, no statistics, no legislation; the emotional architecture is complete before any policy content appears
- **Anti-Intellectualism (Ch. 3)**: implicit in "those who have lied, failed and betrayed" -- the technocratic elite who lost France
- **Hierarchy (Ch. 5)**: the civilizational claim: "It is a determining choice for civilisation" -- some civilizations are worth defending; others, implicitly, are threats

*The gender move -- most analytically interesting:*

"I am a woman, and as such I experience the ever-increasing restrictions on liberty in our country through the development of Islamic fundamentalism like an act of violence."

This is Stanley's sexual anxiety tactic (Chapter 8 -- not today's assignment, but worth flagging) deployed through explicitly feminist language. She frames Islamic practice not as a cultural or demographic threat but as a gendered threat -- experienced by women specifically as violence against their bodies and freedom. Students who would resist the sexual anxiety label on a more overtly patriarchal text tend to find this one harder to categorize, which is productive.

*What is absent:*

There are no policy proposals. The only named enemy is "Islamic fundamentalism" -- not Islam, not Muslims -- carefully hedged language that provides legal and rhetorical cover. The emotional work is complete before any policy content appears. Ask: what does it mean that a compelling two-minute case for a candidate contains not a single concrete proposal?

*The binary closing:*

"Either you continue with those who have lied, failed and betrayed... or you decide to restore order to France." Two choices, no third option. This is Finchelstein's authoritarian democracy in condensed form: democratic in procedure (she is running in an election) but structurally exclusionary (there is only one legitimate choice).

### 4. Synthesis: Both frameworks on the same material (13 min)

Return to Project 2025 Passages C and E, which were held back from Session 1A:

**Passage C -- Sexual Anxiety (Stanley Ch. 8, not yet assigned):**

> "Pornography, manifested today in the omnipresent propagation of transgender ideology and sexualization of children, for instance, is not a political Gordian knot... Its purveyors are child predators and misogynistic exploiters of women. Their product is as addictive as any illicit drug and as psychologically destructive as any crime. Pornography should be outlawed."

**Passage E -- The populist "we are the people" move:**

> "The United States belongs to 'We the people.' All government authority derives from the consent of the people... Progressive elites speak in lofty terms of openness, progress, expertise, cooperation, and globalization. But too often, these terms are just rhetorical Trojan horses concealing their true intention -- stripping 'we the people' of our constitutional authority over our country's future."

Pose the direct question: **Is Project 2025 fascist?**

- **Stanley's answer** would likely be: yes, it uses fascist *tactics* extensively across all five of today's chapters plus several others. Whether the regime that results would be explicitly fascist depends on what happens when these tactics succeed.

- **Finchelstein's answer** would likely be: it is *populism* -- it works within democratic-electoral logic, targets opponents through institutional capture rather than violence, and its leader-as-people move is the classic populist formulation. But it's a form of populism unusually close to the interwar authoritarian past.

**The key diagnostic question Finchelstein would ask:** Is there a moment at which electoral defeat would be accepted -- or would it be claimed fraudulent? That's the line between populism and fascism in his account.

**Good closing question:** Does it matter which word we use? What's at stake analytically and politically in calling something fascism vs. populism?

## Anticipated Student Moves and How to Handle Them (Both Sessions)

**"Project 2025 isn't fascism -- it's just conservative policy."**
Good -- worth taking seriously. Ask: what would it take to count? Does Stanley's framework require anything beyond the tactics? Does Finchelstein's? Push them to specify the threshold.

**"Stanley is just calling Republicans fascists to score political points."**
Fair concern about motivated reasoning in scholarship. Ask: what's his actual analytical claim? Is it falsifiable? What would *disconfirm* it? If they can articulate that, the objection becomes a productive methodological question.

**"Hasn't 'fascism' just become a word for 'things I don't like'?"**
This is the Godwin's Law objection and worth engaging directly. Both Stanley and Finchelstein are trying to give the word analytical purchase. Does either framework succeed? How would you know?

**"Fascism requires racism/antisemitism -- this isn't that."**
Push back on the narrow definition. Stanley's framework includes racial hierarchy as one tactic among many, not a necessary condition. Finchelstein's genealogy shows fascism as transnational and varied in its specific targets.

**"The Le Pen ad sounds feminist -- how can it be far right?"**
Exactly right that there's tension here. The feminist framing of anti-Islam sentiment is the EDL's move and the Breivik move too. Ask: can feminist language be deployed for anti-democratic ends? What would Finchelstein say about a female populist leader?

**"Why does the populism/fascism distinction matter practically?"**
Because the category shapes the response. Fascist movements historically required military defeat; populist movements can be addressed through elections. The diagnostic question -- would electoral defeat be accepted? -- is not academic.

## Possible Discussion Questions (Use Selectively)

1. Stanley says fascism is about tactics, not ideology. What's the difference? Does it matter whether someone *believes* fascist ideas or just *uses* fascist tactics?

2. Finchelstein says populism is fascism's democratic reformulation. Does that make populism more or less dangerous than fascism? Why?

3. Project 2025 calls for firing "un-fireable" federal bureaucrats and dismantling the administrative state. Is that Stanley's "dismantling public welfare" tactic, or something else? Does the motivation matter?

4. Le Pen's ad contains no policy proposals. Is that a feature or a bug? What work is affect doing that argument cannot?

5. Both Project 2025 and the Le Pen ad claim to speak "in the name of the people." What does each mean by "the people"? Who is included? Who is excluded?

6. Finchelstein argues the new right-wing populism is unusually close to the interwar fascist past. What evidence from the Le Pen ad or Project 2025 supports or challenges that claim?

7. If you had to choose one of the two frameworks as your primary analytical lens for the rest of the course, which would you choose and why?

## Notes on Seminar Management

This is a charged topic and students will have strong prior views.

- **Model intellectual suspension**: the goal is not to reach a verdict but to understand the frameworks. You can model this by applying each framework faithfully before evaluating it.
- **Anchor to text**: whenever discussion drifts toward general political argument, pull back to "what does *Stanley* say about that?" or "what passage are you drawing on?" Establishing text-anchored norms in Week 1 pays dividends all term.
- **Acknowledge the stakes**: these are frameworks developed to explain real historical atrocities. Definitional precision matters not as academic hair-splitting but as calibration.
- **Don't resolve the question**: resist the temptation to tell students what the right answer is. The ambiguity is the lesson. End Session 1B on: "we'll come back to this question throughout the term with more evidence and more frameworks."
- **The Le Pen ad**: some students will find it unsettling to analyze a far-right ad as a formal object without condemning it. It is worth saying explicitly that analytical practice does not mean moral neutrality, and that understanding how these things work is a precondition for evaluating and responding to them.
