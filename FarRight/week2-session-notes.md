## Instructor Framing (Both Sessions)

Week 1 gave students frameworks for asking *what is fascism?* Week 2 tests those frameworks against the historical record and then carries the analysis forward to contemporary actors. Session 2A stays in the historical material: what do Welch and Spenkuch & Tillman tell us about *how* Nazism actually worked in practice? Session 2B then bridges to the present: how does Camus and Lebourg's account of the post-1945 far right map onto three British movements of the 2000s--2010s?

The key intellectual move across both sessions is to **resist two symmetrical errors**:
- **Over-assimilation**: everything that echoes historical fascism *is* fascism; the BNP = the NSDAP; Breivik = Hitler. This collapses real analytical distinctions.
- **Under-assimilation**: because the BNP supports the NHS and Breivik despised biological racism, these movements have nothing to do with historical fascism. This misses the structural family resemblances Camus and Lebourg spend the book mapping.

A secondary goal across both sessions is to sharpen the **analytical vocabulary** students will use for the rest of the course: Volksgemeinschaft logic, street vs. electoral movements, the post-1945 revision and denial move, welfare chauvinism, the carrot-stick problem in elite influence.

# Session 2A: The Mechanics of Nazi Success

**Assigned readings:** Spenkuch & Tillman (2018) + Welch (2004)

**Session goal:** Students leave with a clear command of two empirical arguments about Nazi success -- Welch on propaganda and the Volksgemeinschaft, Spenkuch & Tillman on elite influence and religious cleavages -- and understand what each implies for how we analyze contemporary movements.

---

## Session 2A Architecture (75 minutes)

### 1. Opening question (8 min)

Before any lecture, put this on the board:

> *"What drove Nazi electoral success? What's your prior?"*

Give students 2 minutes to write a sentence, then collect 4--5 responses. You'll return to these at the end of the session. Typical student priors: economic anxiety / the Great Depression; Hitler's charisma; propaganda and media control; antisemitism was widespread. Each of these has a relationship -- complicated, in most cases -- to what the readings actually show.

---

### 2. Mini-lecture: Welch on Propaganda and the Volksgemeinschaft (18 min)

**Core argument:**

Nazi propaganda was not an autonomous force that created beliefs from scratch. It worked by **canalization** -- directing and reinforcing already-existing attitudes, grievances, and desires.

Key quote to put on the board:
> "Propaganda gives force and direction to the successive movements of popular feeling and desire; but it does not do much to create these movements. The propagandist is a man who canalises an already existing stream. In a land where there is no water, he digs in vain." (Huxley, quoted by Welch)

Implications: Nazi propaganda was not magic. It required pre-existing streams -- resentment over the Versailles settlement, economic humiliation, status anxiety in the middle classes, antisemitic traditions in German culture. What it did was give these streams a direction, an enemy, and a promise. This matters for the contemporary cases: the BNP and EDL are also canalizing existing streams. The question is always: what streams, and what are they being directed toward?

Welch's second major point: **Propaganda is as much about confirming as converting.** The goal is not primarily to change minds but to give people permission to act on what they already feel.

**The Volksgemeinschaft (15 min):**

The *Volksgemeinschaft* -- "people's community" -- was the central organizing concept of Nazi propaganda. It promised a society that had transcended class divisions through *ethnic* unity. The "national community" would replace horizontal solidarity (trade unions, class consciousness) with vertical ethnic solidarity (all Germans as one authentic people).

Key structural features:
- **In-group definition**: who belongs to the Volk is ethnically defined; "German" is a racial, not civic, category
- **Welfare provision as in-group benefit**: the Nazi welfare state was explicitly for Volksgenossen (members of the community) -- generous provision for those inside, exclusion and eventually elimination for those outside
- **The out-group as constitutive**: the Volksgemeinschaft is defined as much by who it excludes as who it includes; Jews, Roma, the disabled, homosexuals are not just irrelevant but actively threatening

Welch argues there was always a gap between the propaganda myth and social reality -- class divisions persisted, ordinary Germans remained skeptical of many Nazi claims -- but the myth was powerful enough to produce at least passive acquiescence and active collaboration from significant portions of the population.

**For the contemporary cases:** The Volksgemeinschaft concept is the analytical hinge between historical fascism and the contemporary British cases students will encounter in Session 2B. All three movements exhibit Volksgemeinschaft *logic* -- in-group solidarity sustained by out-group exclusion -- but they draw the boundary of the in-group differently:
- BNP: ethnic/indigenous ("indigenous peoples of the British Isles")
- EDL: civilizational/ideological (all races welcome under opposition to Islam-as-ideology)
- Breivik: pan-European Christian (explicitly rejecting biological racism)

Ask students to hold this variation in mind.

---

### 3. Mini-lecture: Spenkuch & Tillman on Elite Influence (22 min)

**The central finding (7 min):**

Religious composition alone explains more than 58% of county-level variation in Nazi vote shares -- dwarfing unemployment rates, occupational composition, and every other socioeconomic variable, individually and combined. Catholics were roughly 25 percentage points less likely to vote Nazi than their Protestant counterparts after accounting for all observable differences.

This is a strong finding about the *limits* of economic determinism in explaining far-right voting. Pause here and return to the opening priors: economic anxiety and the Great Depression are real context, but they are not the key variable in predicting who voted Nazi.

**The mechanism: elite influence (8 min):**

The paper's core argument is that this Catholic-Protestant gap was primarily driven by the Catholic Church's active opposition to the NSDAP. The Church warned parishioners explicitly and repeatedly -- sermons, confessional pressure, denial of sacraments to suspected Nazi supporters. This "immunized" Catholic constituencies against radicalization.

The "brown priest" finding: In villages where a known Nazi-sympathizing Catholic priest was stationed within 10 kilometers, the Catholic-Protestant gap in Nazi vote shares *shrank by 32--41%*. Local clergy contradicting the official position was a major channel through which radicalization spread even in otherwise protected Catholic areas. The mechanism is local, personal, and credible precisely because the defectors were institutional insiders.

**The carrot-and-stick framework (5 min):**

The theoretical model explains a puzzle: the Church could immunize Catholics against the far right but *failed* to protect them against the far left (communists), despite equally vigilant efforts in both directions. The explanation: the Centre Party (Zentrum) was positioned to the right of center, making it an acceptable alternative to the NSDAP for voters who might otherwise radicalize rightward. There was no equivalent Catholic left-wing alternative. The model's key conclusion:

> "The effectiveness of explicit proscriptions (i.e., the stick) depends critically on whether elites are able to offer voters a palatable alternative (i.e., the carrot)... it may take a populist to save democracy from the fanatics."

**The collapse moment (2 min):**

When Bishop Bertram issued a statement in March 1933 signaling accommodation with the Nazi government, Catholic resistance collapsed rapidly: the estimated gap between Catholics and Protestants shrank from nearly three standard deviations to less than half a standard deviation within months. Decades of immunization reversed when the elite signal changed.

---

### 4. Discussion: Evidence, Methods, and Implications (20 min)

**On the evidence (10 min):**

Both papers make empirical claims. Ask students to engage with the evidence specifically:

- Welch's evidence is qualitative and interpretive -- he analyzes propaganda texts, memoirs, contemporary reports. What can and can't this method establish?
- Spenkuch & Tillman's evidence is quantitative -- county-level election data combined with 16th-century religious geography as an instrumental variable. What does the IV strategy do? (Intuition: the Peace of Augsburg in 1555 determined which German territories adopted Protestantism, and that historical accident is correlated with 1930s religious composition but plausibly uncorrelated with other causes of Nazi voting.) What can and can't this method establish?

**On the implications for contemporary movements (10 min):**

Return to the three questions that connect these readings to the contemporary cases (which will be developed in Session 2B):

1. **The economic explanation**: Both papers suggest economic conditions matter but are not the key variable. In the Spenkuch & Tillman model, the Depression provided grievance fuel; religion and elite signals determined who radicalized. Does this pattern hold in the contemporary British cases? (Preview: BNP, EDL, and Breivik all state their grievances in cultural/identity terms, not primarily economic ones.)

2. **The carrot-stick problem**: If elite proscriptions only work when paired with an acceptable alternative, what would the carrot look like in contemporary British politics? The BNP, EDL, and Breivik all diagnose the absence of the carrot in their own way -- they exist because (in their own framing) no mainstream party addresses their concerns. When UKIP eventually provided something more palatable on immigration, the BNP collapsed. Does this confirm or complicate the Spenkuch & Tillman model?

3. **The brown priest equivalents**: Who in the contemporary media and political ecosystem plays the role of the brown priest -- the institutional insider who contradicts official proscription by validating the movement's core claims? What is the mechanism in the age of social media and online communities?

---

### 5. Closing bridge to Session 2B (7 min)

Preview what Session 2B does: Camus and Lebourg provide a taxonomy and narrative of how the post-1945 European far right adapted to the historical defeat of fascism. The three British case study handouts give primary source material to analyze through both the Session 2A frameworks (Volksgemeinschaft, elite influence) and the Session 2B framework (normalization, the post-fascist far-right family).

Close with the question: "These movements knew fascism was discredited. How do you rebuild a fascist-adjacent movement in a world where 'fascist' is an insult? That's what Session 2B is about."

# Session 2B: From Historical Fascism to Contemporary Movements

**Assigned reading:** Camus & Lebourg, *Far-Right Politics in Europe*, Introduction + Chapter 1

**Supplementary classroom materials:** BNP handout, EDL handout, Breivik handout (distribute at start of session)

**Session goal:** Students leave with a command of Camus & Lebourg's taxonomy and normalization framework, and have applied it -- alongside the Volksgemeinschaft and elite-influence frameworks from Session 2A -- to three contemporary British far-right actors.

---

## Session 2B Architecture (75 minutes)

### 1. Opening: The revision problem (8 min)

Open with a direct question:

> *"After 1945, fascism was discredited across Europe. What do you do if you still hold fascist beliefs? What are your options?"*

Brief discussion -- 3--4 responses. Students will generate something like: go underground, deny you were a fascist, repackage the ideas, find new enemies. Write these on the board.

Then introduce Camus and Lebourg's key observation: the post-war far right's first move, before any ideological renovation, was **historical revision** -- beginning with Holocaust denial in 1946--48. This isn't incidental; it's structural. If you want to defend anything adjacent to National Socialism, you first have to sever its association with genocide. This sets up the analytical frame for the session: **the contemporary far right is always in some sense negotiating its relationship to the historical record.**

---

### 2. Mini-lecture: Camus & Lebourg, Introduction + Chapter 1 (18 min)

**The taxonomy (8 min):**

Camus and Lebourg resist treating the far right as a monolith. Chapter 1 identifies several distinct organizational types that emerged after 1945:

- **Neo-Nazi groups**: those that explicitly defend National Socialism, biological racism, and Holocaust denial; the most marginal but ideologically purest postwar formation
- **Nationalist revolutionary groups**: reject both liberal capitalism and communism; seek "third way" alternatives; often with paramilitary organization
- **Populist parties**: the most electorally successful type; accept democratic norms, update ideological content to fit national context, contest elections; examples include the Front National in France, the FPÖ in Austria, the Lega Nord in Italy
- **Street movements**: mobilize outside electoral politics through demonstrations, intimidation, and occupation of public space; the EDL is an example

These types are not mutually exclusive and individuals and ideas move between them.

**Normalization (10 min):**

The concept of **normalization** is central to Camus and Lebourg's argument and will recur across many weeks of the course. Normalization refers to the process by which far-right ideas and actors move from being outrageous and beyond the pale to being accepted as part of the normal political spectrum.

Key mechanisms they identify:
- Fringe movements shift the Overton window; mainstream parties adopt positions that were initially far-right
- Media platforming of far-right actors and ideas gives them credibility regardless of the editorial frame
- Electoral success generates imitation from mainstream parties (the "contagion" effect, which will be studied more in Week 9)

**The comparative claim on economics:**

Their analysis of the French far right's strength in Perpignan vs. the near-absence of far-right parties in Spain during the same recession makes a methodological point that connects to Spenkuch & Tillman: economic conditions are **necessary but not sufficient**. The far-right vote depends on three intersecting factors: (1) a coherent political offering that fits national context; (2) a social demand for authoritarianism; (3) a sense that a community of common destiny is disintegrating. The economic crisis provides grievance fuel, but only ignites if these other conditions are present.

---

### 3. Primary source analysis: Three contemporary actors (32 min)

Distribute the three handouts (BNP, EDL, Breivik). Divide into three groups; each group takes one actor and answers a structured comparison question set. 20 minutes group work, 12 minutes report back.

**Structured comparison questions** (write on board):

1. Who is "the people" for this actor, and who is excluded from the authentic community?
2. What is the primary enemy -- internal, external, or both?
3. Does this actor operate through elections, street mobilization, or individual violence? What does the choice of tactic tell us?
4. Where do you see Volksgemeinschaft logic -- in-group provision paired with out-group exclusion?
5. What is explicitly *different* from 1930s fascism -- not just cosmetically, but analytically?
6. Applying Stanley's framework from Week 1: which of his ten tactics are present? Which are absent?

---

**Group A: British National Party (BNP)**

*Key passages from handout to anchor discussion:*

"In towns and cities up and down the country we feel like foreigners in our own country, our traditions derided and our future given away." / "India would not tolerate millions of non-Indians taking over that society. Pakistan would not tolerate millions of Hindus or Christians entering that country... so why should Britain? Each nation has the right to maintain its own identity." / "The BNP is the ONLY party to recognise that overpopulation -- whose primary driver is immigration... is the cause of the destruction of our environment... Our immigration policies will achieve this."

*Instructor notes for debrief:*

**Resonances**: The BNP's logic is structurally Volksgemeinschaft -- welfare provision and environmental protection framed as in-group benefits conditional on out-group exclusion. Social welfare and ethnic exclusion are not competing priorities but the same policy stated from different angles: NHS for the indigenous British, funded by removing demographic competition. The "voluntary resettlement" euphemism echoes postwar neofascist adaptation -- working within democratic forms while pursuing ethnically exclusionary goals.

The "every nation has the right to maintain its identity" argument appropriates anti-imperialist language (national self-determination) for ethnic exclusion. It invites students to either accept the symmetry between India's right to remain Indian and Britain's right to remain British, or explain why the symmetry fails. This is a genuinely hard question worth letting sit.

The "most democratic party" claim alongside proposals to abolish anti-discrimination law and the Human Rights Act illustrates Finchelstein's authoritarian democracy with unusual clarity: democratic form claimed as cover for gutting democratic substance.

**Departures**: The BNP explicitly disavowed Nazi/fascist heritage and reframed itself as a constitutional British nationalist party. It supported the NHS, opposed the Iraq war, and positioned itself on issues traditionally associated with the left -- precisely the adaptive "fitting to national society" Camus and Lebourg describe. The primary target is not Jews but non-white (especially Muslim) immigrants. The mechanism is deportation through "voluntary" incentives rather than extermination.

**Camus & Lebourg's framework**: The BNP is a populist party in their typology -- the most electorally successful postwar far-right formation type. It peaked at about 1 million votes in the 2009 European elections, then collapsed partly through internal dysfunction and partly because UKIP absorbed its electoral niche.

**Spenkuch & Tillman connection**: The BNP existed in its own diagnosis because no mainstream party offered voters a palatable alternative on immigration and identity. When UKIP provided something more palatable, the BNP collapsed. Does this confirm the carrot-stick model (the carrot drained the radical movement) or complicate it (UKIP's rise legitimated the underlying concern and shifted the Overton window further toward the BNP's positions)?

---

**Group B: English Defence League (EDL)**

*Key passages from handout to anchor discussion:*

"We refuse to tolerate the intolerant -- The EDL welcomes support from people of all races, all faiths, all political persuasions, and all legal lifestyle choices." / "English culture is opposed by Islam -- We don't see a lot of support for these [Enlightenment values] in Islamic scripture... it is close to certainty that, as Britain becomes more Islamified, we stand to lose each of these." / Anti-Sharia Strategy: "Banning all new mosque construction and expansion," "Opening all mosque services and meetings to monitoring by an 'Ofsted for mosques'," "Freeing employers from any obligation to pay wages for time used for prayer."

*Instructor notes for debrief:*

**Resonances**: The EDL is structurally the most similar to historical fascism's **street movement** phase -- not an electoral party but a mobilizing force that uses demonstrations, marches, and tactical confrontation to claim physical and symbolic ownership of public space. The analogy to the SA (Sturmabteilung) is imperfect but worth examining: the SA also grew out of organized male violence and was repurposed into political mobilization.

The gap between the EDL's liberal public face ("human rights organization," "all races and faiths welcome") and the private reality (mosque attacks, racist social media posts documented by IRR) maps onto Welch's canalization point: the public mission statement is designed to attract sympathizers uncomfortable with explicit racism, while the private voice reveals the actual stream being directed.

**Departures**: The EDL's explicit multiculturalism -- Jewish Division, LGBT Division, Sikh spokesperson -- represents a genuine structural departure from historical fascism's racial hierarchy. The organization staked its public legitimacy on religion-not-race as the basis of exclusion. Camus and Lebourg classify this type as distinct from neo-Nazism and observe that contemporary far-right movements have generally replaced biological racism with "culturalism" -- the enemy is a culture, not a race, which provides legal and rhetorical cover.

The appropriation of Enlightenment values (habeas corpus, free expression, critical thinking) as the *content* of English cultural particularity is analytically significant: the claim is that these universal values are specifically English, that Islam is structurally incompatible with them, and therefore defending English culture is the same as defending liberalism. Ask students: is this coherent? Can Enlightenment values be the property of a particular culture?

**The Spenkuch & Tillman connection**: The EDL's "Islam is incompatible with Western values" frame mainstreamed faster than the BNP's ethnic nationalism, despite the EDL never running for office. David Cameron's 2011 speech declaring multiculturalism a "failure" used language indistinguishable from the EDL's public position. This is the brown priest dynamic at a higher level: mainstream politicians partially validating the threat diagnosis while maintaining distance from the movement. In the Spenkuch & Tillman model, this should reduce the protective effect of official proscriptions -- and the EDL's subsequent decline tracks not with its ideas losing credibility but with those ideas being absorbed into the mainstream.

**The Breivik connection** (brief): Breivik referenced the EDL approvingly in his manifesto and had briefly been in contact with EDL members. This connection is analytically important: the permeable boundary between electoral/street movements and individual violence is not accidental. Both share almost the entire ideological vocabulary; what differs is the tactical conclusion drawn from the shared diagnosis.

**Group C: Anders Breivik / *2083: A European Declaration of Independence***

*Key passages from handout to anchor discussion:*

"Much of the information presented in this compendium has been deliberately kept away from the European peoples by our governments and the politically correct mainstream media... The growing numbers of nationalists in Western Europe are systematically being ridiculed, silenced and persecuted by the current cultural Marxist/multiculturalist political establishments." / Frankfurt School passage: "Critical Theory was essentially destructive criticism of the main elements of Western culture... These criticisms have destroyed every defensive structure of European society which has laid the foundation for the Islamisation of Europe." / "We have come to the conclusion that the democratical struggle to save Europe has been lost."

*Instructor notes for debrief:*

**Resonances**: The "cultural Marxism" framework is structurally parallel to classical fascism's antisemitic conspiracy theory -- a shadowy intellectual elite is secretly engineering the destruction of the authentic people from within. The enemy has been updated: where Goebbels blamed a Jewish-Bolshevik conspiracy, Breivik blames a Frankfurt School-multiculturalist conspiracy. The logic is formally identical: the authentic people (European civilization) is under attack from internal traitors enabling an external threat. The mythic past being defended is "European civilization" -- the title *2083* refers to the 400th anniversary of the Battle of Vienna (1683), when Ottoman forces were repelled from Central Europe.

Welch's canalization point applies with force: Breivik did not invent "cultural Marxism." He assembled existing streams -- the Free Congress Foundation's pamphlets, the Counterjihad blogosphere, paleoconservative American writers -- into an unusually systematic document. The manifesto is more an aggregation node than an original work.

**Departures**: Breivik explicitly rejected biological racism, admired Israel as a front-line state against Islam, and was contemptuous of electoral politics. He represents a different political form: the lone-wolf networked terrorist, radicalized through online communities, who acts outside any organizational structure. Spenkuch & Tillman's elite-influence finding has no purchase here -- Breivik specifically acted *against* all existing elites, including conservative ones. The Camus & Lebourg taxonomy doesn't quite fit either: he is not a neo-Nazi, not a populist party, not a street movement. He represents what happens when the ideological content of far-right movements is combined with radicalization pathways that bypass organizational gatekeepers entirely.

His manifesto explicitly addresses future actors, not a membership. This anticipates the Islamic State's distributed-inspiration model.

**Why he attacked Utøya**: The internal/external enemy architecture explains the target. Breivik's primary enemy is the "cultural Marxist" traitors who enabled Islamization -- not Muslims directly. So he attacked a Labour Party youth camp, not a mosque. Understanding this requires tracing the logical structure of the manifesto rather than assuming the act was random or irrational.

### 4. Full-group synthesis: Comparison grid (12 min)

Draw the comparison grid on the board during the group report-backs:

|  | Historical Fascism | BNP | EDL | Breivik |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organizational form | Party + street movement | Electoral party | Street movement | Individual actor |
| Primary "enemy within" | Jews / Communists | Non-white immigrants | "Radical Islam" / Left | Multiculturalist elites |
| External enemy | Allied powers / degeneracy | EU / non-Western nations | Islam | Islam |
| Volksgemeinschaft equivalent | Ethnic Germans | Ethnic British | English (culturally defined) | European civilization |
| Relation to democracy | Anti-democratic | Formally democratic | Ambivalent | Explicitly anti-democratic |
| Relation to violence | Institutionalized | Rhetorical distancing | Unofficial / deniable | Explicit |

Key synthesis questions:
- **Does the shift from biological racism to "culturalism" represent a fundamental break or a surface adaptation?** Camus and Lebourg's position: adaptation, not break -- the exclusionary logic is preserved even as the basis shifts from race to religion/culture.
- **Does the BNP's support for the NHS make it less fascist?** Or is that the Volksgemeinschaft working in a contemporary register? (The Nazi welfare state also included social provision -- for members of the community.)
- **Where the in-group boundary is drawn is analytically central -- and each movement draws it differently.** BNP: ethnic/indigenous. EDL: civilizational/ideological. Breivik: pan-European Christian. All three exhibit Volksgemeinschaft logic, but the boundary varies. What does this mean for available legal and political responses?
- **The Spenkuch & Tillman problem: where is the carrot?** All three movements diagnose the failure of mainstream institutions to offer a viable alternative on their stated concerns. What would a functioning carrot look like? What are the risks in Spenkuch & Tillman's warning that "it may take a populist to save democracy from the fanatics"?

### 5. Closing question (5 min)

## Anticipated Student Moves and How to Handle Them (Both Sessions)

**"The BNP and EDL aren't fascist because they support democracy."**
Push back carefully. Finchelstein would say that's precisely the distinction between fascism and *populism* -- not that the latter is harmless, but that its logic is different. Stanley might say supporting democracy while systematically dehumanizing a target population is exactly how fascist politics operates in a democratic context.

**"Breivik is just a mentally ill loner -- he's not representative of a movement."**
The Norwegian court found him legally sane. More importantly, his manifesto is a *compilation* -- he assembled it from existing far-right intellectual currents that circulate across movements and borders. His ideology has a genealogy; his radicalization has a context. The question isn't whether he was typical but what conditions produced him and why his document functions as a genre-establishing text for subsequent attackers.

**"The 'cultural Marxism' framework sounds paranoid, but isn't he right that multiculturalism is a real policy people disagree about?"**
There's a difference between a legitimate policy debate about multiculturalism and the conspiracy theory framing in which it's a Frankfurt School plot to destroy European civilization. What does the conspiracy framing add to -- and what does it do to -- a legitimate policy disagreement? (Answer: it transforms political opponents into traitors, which licenses violence against them.)

**"How is the EDL different from legitimate protest movements that oppose radical Islamism?"**
Ask what the evidence shows about the EDL's actual targets: mosques in general (not radical ones), left-wing bookshops, anti-cuts protestors, Kurdish kebab shops. The stated target (radical Islam) and the operational targets are different. That gap is analytically significant.

**"Why does it matter which category these groups fall into?"**
Because the category shapes the response. Fascist movements historically required military defeat; populist movements can be addressed through elections. Neo-Nazi groups can be legally banned in Germany; populist parties cannot. The stakes of classification are real.

**"Isn't the economic explanation just more parsimonious?"**
Bring in Spenkuch & Tillman: religion explains more variation than economic variables, individually or combined. And none of the three British primary sources states its grievances primarily in economic terms. The economic substrate may be present but has been entirely sublimated into cultural/identity terms -- or the cultural/identity grievance is prior and irreducible.

## Notes on Session 2B Classroom Dynamics

Discussing Breivik requires some care. Students may have personal connections to this material, or may find it genuinely disturbing. It is worth:
- Naming at the start of discussion that this is hard material and why it's analytically necessary
- Making clear that the goal is to understand the ideology's structure, not to evaluate the validity of any part of it
- Not reading violent or graphic passages aloud
- Being prepared for a quieter, more subdued room than usual -- that's appropriate

On the other side, some students may find conspiracy theories like Breivik's "cultural Marxism" thesis uncomfortably resonant with things they've encountered in mainstream conservative media. If that comes up, it's worth addressing directly: the question is how far the conspiracy logic travels from its extremist origins into mainstream discourse, and what that movement does to the discourse.
