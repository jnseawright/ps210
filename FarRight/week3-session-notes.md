## Session 3A: Terror, Paramilitarism, and Reactive Mobilization

**Readings**: Byman (2021); McVeigh, Cunningham & Grannis (2014); Van Dyke & Soule (2002)  
**Duration**: 75 minutes  
**Core question**: What do the KKK, the militia movement, and political violence have in common — and what makes each distinct?

---

### Orientation Note for the Instructor

Session 3A covers three articles about three different organizational forms of the US far right. The intellectual payoff is not in memorizing each finding separately but in holding them together:

- **Byman** treats the KKK as an *insurgency and terrorism problem* — a counterinsurgency lens applied to Reconstruction
- **McVeigh et al.** treat 1960s Klan activity as a *political realignment mechanism* — how movement violence restructures partisan social networks for decades
- **Van Dyke & Soule** treat the militia/patriot movement as *reactive mobilization* — a social movement emergence question about which structural conditions produce armed far-right organizing

Byman and McVeigh address the same historical period (KKK activism) but from completely different disciplinary angles (security studies vs. historical political sociology), which offers a natural methodological conversation. Van Dyke & Soule shift to the 1990s and to quantitative social movement theory, setting up the Bridge question: is the militia movement a new phenomenon or an old one in new clothes?

### Mini-Lecture 1: The KKK as Insurgency (Byman) (20 minutes)

#### The Central Move

Byman's core contribution is *disciplinary translation*: he takes a historical event that most US historians narrate as a story of racism and political failure and reframes it using the conceptual vocabulary of counterinsurgency, post-conflict peacebuilding, and terrorism studies. The gain is analytical precision about why the federal government failed; the cost is that some of the political and racial specificity of the case can recede. Both the gain and the cost are worth surfacing in class.

**His thesis in one sentence**: Reconstruction failed primarily because white supremacists used insurgency and terrorism to reverse Black political gains, and because the federal government — despite having structural advantages — made a series of interconnected policy failures that enabled them to succeed.

#### Three Levels of Explanation

Byman uses a three-level framework: structural factors, federal-level policy factors, and local-level policy factors.

*Structural factors* (mixed picture):
- Favoring insurgents: South's economic collapse, governance vacuum, weapons/organizational endowments from Civil War, patrols inherited from slave system
- Favoring counterinsurgents: federal government's decisive military victory, rapid Black political mobilization, strong political will (until it waned), proscription of Confederate leadership initially

*Federal-level policy failures* (the crux):
- Insufficient troops: peak of 17,657 (1868) fell to fewer than 5,000 by 1877; need for modern stability operations was ~180,000
- Lack of clear, shared goals among civilian and military leadership (Lincoln dead, Johnson hostile, Congress divided)
- Weak civilian capacity: Freedmen's Bureau had only 900 personnel across the entire South
- No planning for a long-term occupation or a credible withdrawal plan
- The Compromise of 1877 as the canonical example of what Byman calls "paper promises" without enforcement capacity

*Local-level failures*:
- State and local governments lacked legitimacy among white Southerners
- Insufficient local collaborators — white Republicans under constant social pressure and physical threat
- The "security dilemma" around Black militias: arming the Black community would have been the most effective countermeasure, but Republican leaders feared it would trigger white countermobilization (it did even when they tried it cautiously)

#### Key Violence Pattern: Spiking Before Elections

**Key passage for lecture**: "Although violence occurred for many reasons, it often spiked before elections, both state and federal. Before the 1875 state elections, Democrats in Mississippi vowed to win 'the election peaceably if we can, forcibly if we must.'" (p. 76)

This pattern — electoral violence as political strategy — is the core of Byman's terrorism framing. The KKK was not committing violence for its own sake but to produce a specific political outcome: suppress Black voting, destroy Republican electoral coalitions, and reverse Reconstruction governance. This connects directly to the McVeigh et al. finding about lasting electoral effects.

Other key facts for lecture:
- Louisiana alone: 1,000+ killed between April and November 1868 elections; 2,000 more killed or wounded before the 1871 election
- Arkansas: 2,000+ killed in connection with the 1868 presidential election
- Colfax Massacre (1873): 165 Black residents killed by white Democrats with cannons
- One Army officer in Texas: "The murder of negroes is so common as to render it impossible to keep an accurate account of them."

#### The Enforcement Acts as Critical Juncture

The Enforcement Acts of 1870–71 (the "KKK Acts") showed that *when the federal government acted decisively, it worked*: the Klan's back was temporarily broken in several states. This is Byman's strongest counterfactual evidence. The South Carolina crackdown via habeas corpus suspension led to hundreds of arrests and a dramatic decline in violence. But this was the exception; political will flagged as Northern public opinion shifted after the Panic of 1873.

**Key passage**: "Use of the Enforcement Acts demonstrated that, if the federal government had consistently employed force aggressively, it would have been possible to suppress white supremacist violence." (p. 79)

#### Path Dependence

The early failures had compounding consequences: unpunished violence emboldened further violence, further violence required more troops to suppress, and the rising political cost of suppression made action less likely. This is the path-dependent logic Byman applies. Early junctures (1865–67) mattered enormously; by 1877, reversing the order would have required even greater coercion.

**Useful analogy**: Byman compares Reconstruction to occupations of Iraq and Afghanistan — the structural conditions (contested occupation, divided society, spoilers, weak civilian capacity) are recognizable across cases even when the details differ profoundly.

#### Contemporary Lessons (can be brief)

Byman draws four policy implications:
1. Half measures are dangerous — they embolden spoilers without achieving security
2. Elections without rule of law become instruments of tyranny (voting alone did not save Reconstruction)
3. Failed democratization leaves collaborators most vulnerable
4. Compromise with losers after civil war is often fraught — the Compromise of 1877 entrenched injustice while nominally ending conflict

### Mini-Lecture 2: The KKK and Partisan Realignment (McVeigh et al.) (15 minutes)

#### The Central Move

McVeigh, Cunningham & Grannis address a completely different question than Byman. They are not asking why Reconstruction failed, but why counties with 1960s KKK chapters vote more Republican than comparable counties 40 years later. Their answer is the "takeoff issue" mechanism: extreme movement activity polarizes a community in ways that restructure social networks along partisan lines, and those restructured networks persist long after the movement itself has faded.

**Their thesis**: 1960s KKK activism had a lasting positive effect on Republican vote share in Southern counties across five presidential elections (1960–2000), with an effect size of 3–5 percentage points after controls — not through changing individual racial attitudes but through embedding individuals in social networks that reinforced Republican partisan identity.

#### The Design (worth explaining)

The fixed-effects panel design is elegant and worth a brief explanation for students:
- Unit of analysis: Southern counties
- Treatment: presence of a KKK chapter in the 1960s (from HUAC records)
- Outcome: Republican presidential vote share across 5 elections (1960, 1968, 1980, 1992, 2000)
- Key innovation: by comparing the *same county to itself over time*, they rule out many confounds (geography, pre-existing political culture, economic structure)

The individual-level data from the 1992 ANES adds a mechanism test: racial resentment predicts Republican voting in 1992, but *only in counties where the Klan had been active in the 1960s*. This interaction effect is the smoking gun for the social network mechanism — the Klan restructured communities, and in those communities racial attitudes became more tightly coupled with partisan choice.

#### The "Takeoff Issue" Concept

Invite students to think of the KKK's civil rights confrontations as a "takeoff issue" that forced community members to choose sides in a publicly visible way. Once people took sides — through signing petitions, joining organizations, appearing at rallies or counter-rallies, losing or gaining social ties — their networks realigned. Network ties then carried partisan information and social pressure across subsequent elections, even after the original issue receded.

This is a genuinely powerful theoretical contribution. It explains how social movements produce political effects that outlive the movement, and it suggests that the most important legacy of the KKK may not be its direct violence (which Byman covers) but its restructuring of Southern political communities.

**Key passage**: "Local movement activity creates polarizing events ('takeoff issues') that precipitate sharp shifts in local partisan balance, with enduring consequences. These communities become more strongly aligned with one party, and the effects of movement activity grow over time." (p. 1237, approximately)

#### Bridge to Van Dyke

The McVeigh et al. finding about the militia movement's antecedents is brief (they note it as a historical parallel) but Van Dyke & Soule pick up where they leave off: if the KKK restructured partisan networks in the South, what explains *where* the militia movement emerged in the 1990s? The answer is not history but economics.

### Mini-Lecture 3: Reactive Mobilization and the Militia Movement (Van Dyke & Soule) (10 minutes)

This mini-lecture can be shorter because the Van Dyke & Soule argument is more methodologically familiar to students and less historically rich than Byman or McVeigh.

#### The Central Argument

The 1990s patriot/militia movement (~800 organizations by 1996, across all 50 states) is best understood as a *reactive social movement* — one mobilizing in response to perceived loss of economic or political resources. Van Dyke & Soule test three types of structural "threat" as predictors:

- **Demographic threat**: above-average increase in non-white population → *mixed results* (significant at county level, not state)
- **Political threat**: percent female legislators → *significant* (women's political gains trigger reactive organizing)
- **Economic restructuring**: loss of manufacturing jobs + loss of farms → *strongest and most robust predictor* at both state and county levels

The economic restructuring finding is the most important: states that lost manufacturing jobs between 1990–93 had **71% more** patriot/militia organizations; those that lost farms had **51% more** (state level). At the county level, effects were even larger (152% and 107%). Crucially, *general unemployment rate was NOT significant* — it is sectoral economic disruption (manufacturing, agriculture), not overall economic distress, that predicts militia organizing.

#### What This Tells Us About the Movement

The Van Dyke finding helps explain the militia movement's *geography* (rural, de-industrializing regions), *social base* (manufacturing workers, farmers, rural white men), and *ideology* (anti-globalization, anti-NAFTA, anti-"New World Order"). The groups that joined were not simply economically desperate — they were workers from sectors that had *previously* provided stable livelihoods and community status, and who now faced structural displacement.

**Key passage for lecture**: "Our findings indicate that the rhetoric articulated by the patriot/militia movement is filtered through the personal experiences of individuals. To some, in particular those hard hit by economic restructuring, the messages will ring true and serve as a call to action." (p. 514)

The movement's ideology — opposition to the UN, fears of a "New World Order," anti-semitic conspiratorialism — was Birch-adjacent (Van Dyke & Soule note the JBS as a historical antecedent). But the *mobilizing grievance* was material: job loss and farm loss. This anticipates the Week 6–7 material on economic anxiety vs. cultural threat as drivers of far-right mobilization.

#### Brief Note on Methods

Van Dyke & Soule use *negative binomial regression* — a technique for count outcomes (number of militia organizations per state/county) that corrects for overdispersion. Students may want to note this as an example of quantitative social movement research: SPLC data as the dependent variable, Census and economic data as predictors, two-level analysis as a robustness check.

### Discussion: Comparing the Three US Forms (20 minutes)

#### Frame the Discussion

Remind students of the syllabus question: *Do these groups belong together or apart?* Now that they've seen three quite different movements, they can engage this question with analytical traction.

#### Suggested Discussion Questions

**1. What do all three have in common?**
Expected student observations:
- All are predominantly white male organizations
- All mobilize around perceived threats to racial or economic status
- All have complex relationships to mainstream politics (KKK and Democrats, militias and GOP, JBS and conservatism)
- All use violence or its threat strategically

Push further: *Are these organizations united by a common worldview, or just common demographics and grievances?* What would Byman say? What would McVeigh say? What would Van Dyke say?

**2. What makes them analytically distinct?**
- The Reconstruction KKK targeted *political participation specifically* — it was electoral violence
- The 1960s KKK was a more diffuse community organization that restructured social networks (McVeigh's point)
- The militia movement was *paramilitary but not primarily violent* — it organized, trained, and threatened without systematic assassination campaigns
- They have different relationships to the state: the Reconstruction KKK operated in a governance vacuum; militias are *specifically anti-state* in ideology

**3. Do the European frameworks travel?**

Work through the Week 2 frameworks:
- **Volksgemeinschaft**: Does the KKK's "racial solidarity" function like Volksgemeinschaft? Note that Byman shows that *white Southern solidarity across class lines* was one of the KKK's strengths — the white planter and the white poor farmer both provided cover and recruits. This is structurally similar but ideologically distinct (Christianity + American constitutionalism, not Nordic racial science).
- **Canalization**: Does the "takeoff issue" mechanism in McVeigh et al. resemble the canalization process in Welch (Week 2)? Both describe how extreme movements restructure institutions in ways that outlast the movement. But the mechanisms differ: Welch describes conversion of individuals, McVeigh describes restructuring of communities.
- **Carrot-stick** (Spenkuch & Tillman): Were mainstream political parties offering adequate "carrots" to the constituencies that joined these movements? For the militia movement, Van Dyke & Soule's finding about economic restructuring suggests the answer is no — declining manufacturing and farming communities had legitimate grievances that neither party adequately addressed.
- **Normalization**: Did the KKK normalize political violence in ways that shifted the Overton window? Byman says yes explicitly: "Victories by white racists during Reconstruction gave them a repertoire of violence to draw on in subsequent years." This is the Camus & Lebourg normalization concept applied to the US context.

**4. The Finchelstein question**: He argued that fascism became populism by entering the electoral arena. The US far right largely operated differently — either *outside* elections (militia movement) or as a *spoiler within* them (KKK using violence to suppress opposition). Does this make the US cases pre-fascist, post-fascist, or simply non-fascist?

#### Anticipated Student Moves to Watch For

*"These are all just racist organizations"* — True but analytically insufficient. Push: what specific kind of racism? What institutional form? What relation to the state? What claims about democracy?

*"The militia movement isn't really comparable to the KKK"* — Good instinct. Ask: what's the basis of comparison? Organization? Violence? Ideology? Constituency? Each dimension yields a different answer.

*"Economic anxiety explains everything"* — Challenge via Byman: the KKK was not mobilizing around economic anxiety but around political power. And Van Dyke note that it's *sectoral restructuring*, not general poverty, that matters. Not all economically distressed groups join militias.

## Session 3B: The John Birch Society and the Radicalization of the American Right

**Reading**: Dallek, *Birchers: How the John Birch Society Radicalized the American Right* (2023), Chapters 1, 2, 11, 12  
**Duration**: 75 minutes  
**Core question**: Is the JBS best understood as a failed fringe movement or as the seed of mainstream conservatism? And how does it fit within the broader comparison of US far-right forms?

---

### Orientation Note for the Instructor

Dallek's book is intellectually generous in its structure: Chapters 1–2 establish the founding (1958–59) and early organizational development, while Chapters 11–12 jump to the Bush presidency, the Tea Party, and Trump. This makes the book ideal for a seminar precisely because students must do the work of connecting the two time periods.

The book's central thesis — "Birchers won by losing" — is the single most important idea to foreground. The JBS failed on almost every policy goal it set in the 1960s (Earl Warren was not impeached; the US did not withdraw from the UN; the civil rights movement was not stopped). But its organizational infrastructure, donor networks, conspiratorial ideological framework, and grassroots political tactics survived and eventually powered movements that did achieve power.

Session 3B should help students see the JBS as an analytical pivot: it connects the violent/paramilitary far right of Session 3A to the mainstream conservative/populist right they will encounter in Weeks 5–9. It is the hinge between those two worlds.

---

### Opening: The Revision Problem (8 minutes)

Begin with a quick provocation. Write on the board:

> "The John Birch Society was a fringe extremist movement that failed."  
> "The John Birch Society radicalized the American right."

Ask: Can both be true? What evidence would you need to evaluate either claim?

This surfaces the course's underlying methodological question: what does it mean for a political movement to "succeed" or "fail"? Students who read Camus & Lebourg last week may recall the normalization framework; students who read Finchelstein may recall his claim that fascism succeeded by *becoming populism* — transforming rather than conquering. Dallek is making a version of that argument for the US case.

### Mini-Lecture 1: The Founding of the JBS (Chapters 1–2) (20 minutes)

#### Who Were the Founders?

The Indianapolis meeting of December 1958 is the origin point. Welch gathered eleven men for a two-day listening session at a home he chose for its secrecy. The social profile of the attendees is analytically important:

- Predominantly wealthy, white, Christian industrialists — leaders of major corporations (oil, heavy machinery, steel, refrigeration)
- Several had served on the board of the National Association of Manufacturers
- They held positions of institutional prestige (YMCA leadership, Rotary Club speakers, state chamber of commerce presidents) while simultaneously believing an existential communist conspiracy was destroying America
- Fred Koch (president of Rock Island Oil and Refining) — father of Charles and David Koch

**Key passage**: "The men who came to Indianapolis believed that wealthy business executives should lead the United States and that popular democratic representation could easily slip into mob rule, tyranny of the majority." (Ch. 1)

This is analytically critical: the JBS was *never* a populist movement in Finchelstein's sense. Its founding was elite-driven, organized *against* mass democracy as well as against communism. The fear of "mob rule" is the anti-democratic subtext that runs through all four chapters.

#### Robert Welch: Profile

Welch is one of the most vivid political personalities students will encounter in this course. A candy manufacturer turned anticommunist visionary, he provides Dallek's vehicle for tracing how fringe ideas attained organizational form.

Key biographical details:
- Born 1899 in rural North Carolina to Baptist preachers; prodigy; left Harvard Law after clashing with Felix Frankfurter, whom he called a Marxist
- Failed at his own candy company during the Depression; joined his brother's company; became wealthy
- Anti-interventionist before WWII; joined America First; took a "relatively benign view of Adolf Hitler"
- Built his anticommunist profile through NAM board service, speaking tours, and self-published books
- Believed Eisenhower was "a dedicated, conscious agent of the communist conspiracy" — his private letter on this became *The Politician*

**Key passage on Welch's worldview**: "Communists pulled the strings. The murder of Birch was a heinous crime. The cover-up was worse." (Ch. 1) — summarizing Welch's logic about John Birch's death

This combination — elite respectability + conspiratorial extremism — is the JBS's defining paradox, and it is what Dallek is tracking across the entire book. The same men who held "prestigious" institutional positions as NAM board members, YMCA leaders, and state Republican Party figures simultaneously believed America was being destroyed from within by a communist conspiracy.

#### NAM as Incubator

The National Association of Manufacturers connection is more important than students typically appreciate. NAM gave the future JBS founders:
- A network of like-minded wealthy conservatives
- Experience with political organizing and alternative media
- A template for combining business respectability with ideological radicalism
- A conflict with moderate corporate pragmatists that sharpened their oppositional identity

**Key passage**: "[The future Birch founders] developed a feel for who sided with them and how to handle the George Romneys of the world." (Ch. 1) — on their experience fighting moderates within NAM

Note the Spenkuch & Tillman connection: the JBS founders were precisely the elites who *refused* to offer carrots — they fought within NAM against unemployment insurance, minimum wage, and any accommodation with organized labor.

#### Chapter 2: Early Activism and Internal Tensions

Chapter 2 (not covered in this summary in detail, but assigned) covers the JBS's first years: its semi-secret recruitment structure, its use of chapter meetings and reading materials (*American Opinion* magazine), its attacks on Republican moderates, and the first major public controversies.

Key points to bring in:
- The JBS used grassroots organizing tactics borrowed partly from communist organizing manuals (ironically) — chapters, study groups, letter-writing campaigns
- It tried to control members' public statements to avoid embarrassing publicity
- It immediately attracted anti-Semites and white supremacists at the margins, creating a permanent internal tension between the leadership's desire for respectability and the membership's harder-line elements
- Early campaigns: "Impeach Earl Warren" billboard campaign; opposition to fluoridation; attacks on UNICEF

The fluoridation conspiracy (the idea that fluoridating water was a communist plot to weaken Americans) is worth one minute in lecture — it's both historically accurate and pedagogically useful for thinking about how conspiracy theories function as organizing tools. Welch recognized that conspiracy narratives were *adaptable* to almost any issue and created the emotional intensity needed for sustained activist commitment.

### Mini-Lecture 2: From Fringe to Center — Chapters 11 and 12 (20 minutes)

#### The Arc: What Happened in Between (Chs. 3–10, not assigned)

Very briefly contextualize what students didn't read: between the 1960s founding and the Bush/Obama/Trump era, the JBS went through a long period of decline, marginalization, and internal fracture. It was disowned by William F. Buckley and mainstream conservatism in the 1960s. It was associated with the losing Goldwater campaign. Its membership shrank. Most political scientists in the 1970s and 1980s treated it as a historical curiosity. Dallek's argument is that even during this apparent decline, its networks, ideas, and organizational templates were being maintained and transmitted.

#### Chapter 11: "Takeover" — The Bush Years and the Tea Party

Dallek's Chapter 11 treats the Bush presidency as the pivotal enabling condition for the Birch inheritance's reassertion. Bush's failures created the political vacuum the far right had been waiting for:

- **Ron Paul** as the key transmission belt: Paul had personal ties to the JBS, employed former JBS staffer Lew Rockwell, and his 2008 presidential run brought Birch ideas (End the Fed, anti-NATO, anti-NAFTA, nativism, isolationism) into mainstream Republican presidential debates

- **Jesse Benton** (Paul's 2012 campaign chair): "Just eight years earlier, it was only 'fringy people in the John Birch Society' who advocated policies like auditing the Federal Reserve. 'Now it's the Republican Party.'"

- **The Tea Party** as a more electorally effective successor movement: same donor networks (Koch, Bradley, DeVos), same conspiracy frameworks, same Christian nationalist fusion, but with primary election capacity that could oust incumbents

- **The media ecosystem**: Fox News, Glenn Beck (who drew directly on JBS author W. Cleon Skousen), Alex Jones (who discovered the Birch tradition through Gary Allen's *None Dare Call It Conspiracy*) — these created an infrastructure for Birch-style ideas that the 1960s JBS could only dream of

- **Birtherism** as the Tea Party's signature conspiracy: painting the first Black president as literally foreign replicates the JBS framing of civil rights as a "foreign communist plot"

**Key passage**: "Just eight years earlier, it was only 'fringy people in the John Birch Society' who advocated policies like auditing the Federal Reserve. 'Now it's the Republican Party.'" (Benton, quoted in Ch. 11)

#### Chapter 12: "Radicalization" — Trump and January 6

Dallek's argument in Chapter 12 is not that Trump was a Bircher but that he was the most effective exponent of Birch-style politics ever produced: conspiracy theory, white nationalism, anti-internationalism, anti-civil-rights sentiment, and anti-democratic tendencies combined into a governing coalition.

Key elements:
- Trump's 2016 final campaign ad: "A political establishment trying to stop us is the same group responsible for our disastrous trade deals, massive illegal immigration, and economic and foreign policies that have bled our country dry. It is a global power structure that is responsible for the economic decisions that have robbed our working class." — Dallek notes this is explicitly antisemitic in its imagery (George Soros, Janet Yellen, Lloyd Blankfein shown)

- **Stephen Miller's immigration agenda** (drawn from white nationalist sources including *The Camp of the Saints*); child separation; asylum elimination

- **COVID-19 response**: framing public health as statist conspiracy parallels JBS opposition to fluoridation and polio vaccines

- **January 6** as the Birch tradition's logical end point — the insurrectionists replicated the Birch pattern where "words became weapons beyond the realm of traditional political clashes"; Confederate flags, gallows, anti-media violence, white Christian nationalism

- **QAnon, Proud Boys, Oath Keepers** as organizational heirs to the Minutemen and other violent auxiliaries that Birch rhetoric always attracted

**Key passage**: "Tapping into the Birchers' alternative political tradition, Donald Trump became a savvy exponent of a set of ideas that had been simmering on the extreme right for decades." (Ch. 12)

**Key passage**: "Robert Welch once described Birchers as 'a phalanx of tens of thousands of spears, which can be hurled simultaneously as one mighty weapon against any vulnerable spot in the Communist line.' The insurrectionists who marched on the Capitol operated as if they were that 'one mighty weapon.'" (Ch. 12)

#### "Birchers Won by Losing" — Dallek's Central Thesis

This phrase from the Conclusion is the analytical center of the book and the pedagogical anchor for Session 3B.

The JBS failed on virtually every immediate policy goal:
- Earl Warren was not impeached
- The US did not withdraw from the UN
- Civil rights legislation passed
- Medicare passed
- The Birch candidate (Goldwater) lost in a landslide

But it succeeded in building:
- A donor network (Koch, others) that funded the conservative movement for 60 years
- Organizational templates (chapters, study groups, alternative media) that were copied by movement after movement
- A conspiratorial ideological framework (government = communist threat) that proved infinitely adaptable
- A definition of "real America" (white, Christian, anti-statist, anti-internationalist) that became the emotional core of the MAGA coalition

**The question for discussion**: Is this success? What theory of political change does "winning by losing" imply? Does it require that the goals of the 1958 founders were actually achieved, or just that something they set in motion achieved power?

### Discussion: The JBS in Comparative Perspective (22 minutes)

This discussion should accomplish three things: (1) test the "group together or apart" question across all Week 3 cases; (2) apply Week 2 European frameworks to the JBS; (3) raise the course's central methodological question about how to study far-right success and failure.

#### Discussion Questions

**1. Where does the JBS fit among the US far-right forms we've studied?**

Students should now be able to distinguish four US far-right forms:
- Reconstruction KKK: electoral terrorism, anti-Black, paramilitary insurgency
- 1960s KKK: community-level polarization engine, civil rights backlash
- Militia/patriot movement: reactive mobilization around economic restructuring
- John Birch Society: elite-organized, idea-driven, anti-communist, anti-statist

Where do the lines fall? Suggested probes:
- The JBS is notably *non-violent* institutionally, even though it attracted violent auxiliaries (the Minutemen). Is non-violence a meaningful distinction here?
- The JBS explicitly worked through education and political organizing, not intimidation. Does this make it more or less dangerous than the KKK?
- Van Dyke & Soule note the JBS as a historical antecedent of the militia movement. What does the JBS transmit to the militias?

**2. Do the European frameworks apply to the JBS?**

*Volksgemeinschaft*: The JBS defined itself around "Americanism" — a Christian, white, anti-communist, constitutionalist identity. This is structurally similar to Volksgemeinschaft logic: in-group solidarity sustained by out-group exclusion (communists, civil rights activists, liberal elites). But the ideological content is distinctively American: it invokes the Founders, the Constitution, and Christian civilization rather than ethnic or racial science.

*Canalization* (Welch): The JBS founders were themselves practitioners of the canalization Welch described — they converted the social infrastructure of NAM, the Republican Party, and civic organizations into vehicles for a different political project. Dallek describes this explicitly: NAM board membership became a recruiting ground, a training ground, and a legitimacy resource for the JBS.

*Normalization*: The Dallek thesis is essentially a story of normalization across 60 years. The JBS's ideas — Birther-adjacent conspiracy about communists in government, anti-internationalism, white Christian nationalism — moved from the fringe to the center. The mechanism was not sudden rupture but gradual infiltration via alternative media, primary elections, and donor networks.

*Carrot-stick* (Spenkuch & Tillman): Was there a mainstream conservative alternative that could have inoculated the Republican Party against Birchification? Dallek implies no — partly because the donor networks that funded "respectable" conservatism (Buckley, Reagan) overlapped with the Birch donor network, making the line between acceptable and unacceptable harder to maintain.

**3. Finchelstein's question revisited**

Finchelstein argued that fascism became populism by entering the electoral arena and claiming to speak for the people against the elite. Dallek's story runs in a fascinating opposite direction: the JBS *began* as an elite movement (wealthy industrialists in a rich woman's living room) and only achieved popular resonance through the Tea Party and Trump, who repackaged its ideas in populist form. What does this tell us about the relationship between elite-organized far-right movements and popular mobilization?

**4. The Stanley question**

Stanley's framework (Week 1) identifies fascism's key features as mythic past, grievance politics, anti-intellectualism, hierarchy, victimhood, law and order, sexual anxiety, and anti-communism. How many of these apply to the JBS? Is the JBS fascist in Stanley's sense? Or does Stanley's framework need to be modified for the US context?

Expected student observation: the JBS is strongly anti-communist rather than fascist — it explicitly aligned itself with American constitutionalism rather than a strongman. But Dallek shows this distinction eroded over time as the JBS's anti-democratic tendencies surfaced (the Republic-not-a-Democracy doctrine; Welch's contempt for "mob rule").

### Key Passages for Lecture/Board

1. **On the anti-democratic subtext**: "The men who came to Indianapolis believed that wealthy business executives should lead the United States and that popular democratic representation could easily slip into mob rule, tyranny of the majority." (Ch. 1)

2. **On conspiracy theory as organizing tool**: "Ultimately, conspiracy theories that government was rife with communist agents had practical appeal. They became metaphors for what Welch and his followers believed to be a long arc of American decline... their genius lay in their shape-shifting adaptability to almost any issue." (Ch. 1)

3. **On transmitting the tradition**: "Just eight years earlier, it was only 'fringy people in the John Birch Society' who advocated policies like auditing the Federal Reserve. 'Now it's the Republican Party.'" (Ch. 11, Jesse Benton)

4. **On Jan. 6 as culmination**: "By the time hundreds of Americans stormed the US Capitol in a bloody insurrection, the extreme right had achieved what in many ways was the logical end point of the Birch-slash-MAGA movement: an actual war on the government, waged in the name of taking their country back." (Ch. 12)

5. **On the core thesis**: "In the long run, Birchers won by losing." (Conclusion)

## Anticipated Student Moves (Both Sessions)

**"The US far right is just about racism"** — Correct but underspecified. The specific *form* of racism matters: electoral violence (Byman), social network restructuring (McVeigh), ethnic competition as reactive mobilization (Van Dyke), anti-communist racial nationalism (Dallek). These have different dynamics and different remedies.

**"This is all just working-class economic anxiety"** — Van Dyke & Soule support the economic threat thesis for the militia movement, but Byman shows the Reconstruction KKK mobilized wealthy planters too, and Dallek's JBS founders were millionaires. Economic anxiety is not a unified explanation.

**"The JBS failed, so it doesn't matter"** — This is the move Dallek is directly contesting. Engage it seriously: what would "success" look like for a movement? Immediate policy wins? Long-run ideological influence? Organizational survival? Party capture?

**"Trump is obviously fascist / Trump is obviously not fascist"** — Both confident claims deserve scrutiny. Useful redirect: what specific features of the Dallek account (Chapter 12) would count as evidence for or against the fascist label? What features require a different framework?

## Notes on Methods Across Session 3A

The three Part 1 articles use strikingly different methods, and a brief meta-observation is pedagogically valuable:
- **Byman**: comparative case study / qualitative historical analysis; draws on counterinsurgency theory as an interpretive framework; makes counterfactual claims
- **McVeigh et al.**: fixed-effects panel regression; individual-level interaction effects; two-level evidence strategy (county-level historical + individual-level 1992 survey)
- **Van Dyke & Soule**: cross-sectional negative binomial regression; state and county levels; SPLC count data as dependent variable

What does each method let you see that the others don't? Byman can narrate process and mechanism in ways the regressions cannot. McVeigh and Van Dyke can rule out confounds and establish effect sizes that case studies cannot. The combination is stronger than any single approach.
