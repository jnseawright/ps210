**Readings:**
- Part 1 (Session 4A): Leidig, *The Women of the Far Right* (2023), Chs. 1–4
- Part 2 (Session 4B): Bastos & Mercea (2018); Gomes-Franco et al. (2024); Bolet & Foos (2025)

## Session 4A: Women, Influencers, and the Social Media Radicalization Pipeline

**Focus:** Leidig's central argument is that far-right women influencers — American and Canadian women operating on YouTube and Instagram between roughly 2015 and 2022 — are not incidental to the contemporary far right but constitutive of its spread. They normalize and legitimize far-right ideology for mainstream audiences through the tactics of influencer culture: relatability, authenticity, and networked intimacy. The session should focus on how social media changes the mechanism of radicalization and what role gender plays in that mechanism.

---

### Opening Hook (10 min)

Begin with a question the students can visualize: "When you picture a far-right activist, what do you see?" Collect responses. The expected default: an angry young white man — possibly violent, possibly anonymous.

Now complicate that image. These are some of the major figures Leidig studies:

- **Lauren Southern**: 23-year-old Canadian YouTuber, discussing her women's studies class "undercover," posting about Islam, feminism, and Western civilization. Millions of views.
- **Rebecca Hargraves ("Blonde in the Belly of the Beast")**: A former finance professional who felt alienated by work culture, discovered alt-right content on YouTube, quit her job to make far-right vlogs full-time.
- **Robyn Riley**: Instagram post — "before" photo: pink hair, nose ring, bad relationship, heavy smoker, "feeling numb." "After" photo: natural brunette, married and pregnant, "feeling joy." She called it a "glow up."
- **Ayla Stewart ("Wife with a Purpose")**: Master's degree in women's spirituality. Former feminist. Former Vagina Monologues performer. Now a prominent far-right tradwife influencer.

These women are not footnotes to the far right. Lana Lokteff, co-founder of the white-nationalist outlet Red Ice TV, described the strategy explicitly: "I think it's important for right-wing women to show themselves. I think it's good to be spokeswomen for our movement and to kind of sell it a little bit. **This is propaganda; it's good marketing.** The more of us who put out content can be highly impactful."

That sentence — propaganda and marketing in the same breath, and offered as a boast — is the thesis of the book.

---

### Mini-Lecture 1: Mapping the Space (15 min)

**What is the alt-right, and how does it relate to the far right?**

Leidig draws a careful distinction. The alt-right was a specific historical formation: roughly 2015–2022, largely online, no formal membership, ideologically heterogeneous, transatlantic in reach but American in origin, now largely defunct. The far right is the broader category — the alt-right was one iteration of it, repackaged for millennial social media audiences.

Lauren Chen's own 4-tier taxonomy (from a 2016 YouTube vlog) is analytically useful for showing both the spectrum and its internal tensions:

- **Tier 1:** "Western nationalism" — pro-Western but not explicitly pro-white
- **Tier 2:** "Pro-white nationalism" — the West is inseparable from whiteness
- **Tier 3:** "Racial homogeny and determinism" — racial identity defines society's culture
- **Tier 4:** "White supremacy and anti-Semitism" — the ZOG conspiracy; explicitly debunked as scientific racism

Chen placed herself at Tier 1 while the book shows her Tier 1 rhetoric shades seamlessly into Tier 2 content (e.g., Muslims don't share Western values; migrants from the 2015 "refugee crisis" are rapists). This is Leidig's point: the tiers are porous, and self-placement at the lower end is often a cover.

**Two distinctions worth preserving for students:**

1. *Far-right influencers vs. mainstream conservatives*: These women are not Turning Point USA or PragerU. They are more explicitly supremacist in their dehumanization efforts. The boundary between conservatism and the far right has become increasingly blurred since Trump's rise — but Leidig's subjects sit on the far-right side of that blur.

2. *Metapolitical vs. electoral strategy*: These women are not running for office or building party organizations. They are pursuing metapolitical change — shifting mainstream social and cultural values through cultural production. The Gramscian frame is explicit in the book: the far right engages in "a cultural or ideological war for hegemony." This distinguishes them from the Birchers (who built donor networks and ran candidates) and places them closer to the European Identitarian movement.

### Mini-Lecture 2: The Radicalization Pipeline (15 min)

**How does the pipeline work?**

The mechanism Leidig documents is not a sudden conversion but an incremental process. Robyn Riley: "I can't really pinpoint to one specific thing that caused that to happen; it was like a domino effect." Rebecca Hargraves: "Like most people, it wasn't a definitive event that caused it." The far-right women influencers consistently describe radicalization as *self-discovery* — finding one's authentic self — not ideological recruitment.

Four structural features enable this:

1. **Algorithmic discovery**: YouTube's algorithm does not distinguish between conservative and far-right content. Searching for Ben Shapiro or Tucker Carlson quickly generates recommendations to Milo Yiannopoulos, then Stefan Molyneux, then Rebecca Hargraves. Caleb Cain's radicalization — documented in the *New York Times* "Rabbit Hole" podcast and recounted here — followed precisely this path.

2. **Microcelebrity tactics** (Rebecca Lewis's "alternative influence network"): These influencers build trust through *relatability*, *authenticity*, and *accountability* — the same tools used by beauty vloggers and lifestyle influencers, now weaponized for far-right content. Crystal Abidin's definition of influencers applies perfectly: "everyday, ordinary internet users who accumulate a relatively large following through the textual and visual narration of their personal lives."

3. **Red pill vlogs**: Personal radicalization narratives — "Origin Story" vlogs, "I Lost All My Friends in the Culture War" — frame the journey into the far right as *self-improvement*. The movement offers: confidence, community, moral purpose. The cost of not joining: social alienation, personal failure, cultural collapse.

4. **Networked intimacy**: Instagram Stories (which disappear after 24 hours, are low-stakes, and invite direct message replies) function as unfiltered radicalization vectors. The format creates parasocial closeness; the content is often more extreme than what appears in edited YouTube videos.

**The audience problem**: Lauren Chen's own analytics showed 85% of her viewers were male. Robyn Riley's goal was "to at least get to a sixty–forty split." Why do far-right women primarily reach men? Caleb Cain explained it directly: "She was beautiful; she was my age.... I think that, for men, those content creators are probably interesting because they're attractive." The women influencers function partly as *honeytraps* — drawing male viewers first with appearance, then retaining them with ideology. They represent a lifestyle aspiration: join the far right, and traditional women like this are waiting for you.

### Mini-Lecture 3: Femininity as Propaganda Vehicle (10 min)

**Chapter 3 (Femininity Not Feminism) and Chapter 4 (The Making of a Tradwife) develop three interlocking vectors:**

**Vector 1: Antifeminism as gateway ideology.** Antifeminism is the shared ideological core across all the influencers Leidig studies — and it is intentionally accessible. Criticizing "social justice warriors" or "third-wave feminism" is not officially far-right. It can feel like common sense. Once inside that gateway, the radicalization pipeline kicks in.

**Vector 2: Religion and science as legitimating frameworks.** Far-right women influencers reach *different audiences* through complementary strategies: pseudoscientific evolutionary psychology arguments (women are biologically suited for domesticity; feminism makes women "unhappy") are deployed alongside theological complementarianism (God ordained distinct roles for men and women). Christianity — Catholicism in particular — functions as a civilizational identity marker as much as a spiritual one. "Christianity" in this discourse consistently means "white Western Christianity."

**Vector 3: Tradwife culture as pipeline.** The "tradwife" community — hashtag #tradlife, subreddits, Pinterest boards — overlaps significantly with the far right without being identical to it. The shared elements: reverence for domesticity, white motherhood as racial reproduction, antifeminism, nostalgia for a mythologized 1950s. Seyward Darby (quoted by Leidig): "Not every tradwife is a white supremacist, but the community's hunger for the distinct boundaries of the past makes it vulnerable to far-right messaging. Tradwives and white nationalists share core objectives (more babies), myths (America's moral decline), and iconography (happy heterosexual families)."

Food is an underappreciated vector. The influencer "Blonde Buttermaker" (Elora) is sponsored by Red Ice TV, a white-nationalist platform, while producing YouTube videos on lacto fermentation and organic butter. Robyn Riley's pregnancy recipes on Instagram Stories — nutritional benefits, superfood caviar — convey commitment to white racial reproduction through the apolitical medium of prenatal nutrition tips.

Leidig's key argument: "perceptions of authenticity and accessibility serve as the most powerful tools of the modern far right." The most effective far-right propaganda doesn't look like propaganda. It looks like a slumber party.

### Discussion Activity: What Is New Here? (20 min)

Small groups (3–4 students), then full-class debrief. Present the following comparisons:

**1. Canalization, old and new.** McVeigh et al. described the 1960s KKK as an organization that restructured preexisting social networks around racial resentment — churches, civic organizations, fraternal groups. Leidig's influencers are doing something similar but through influencer culture infrastructure: YouTube, Instagram, red pill subreddits, Discord servers. Is this a genuinely new mechanism, or canalization in new digital clothes? What changes when the infrastructure is algorithmic rather than geographical?

**2. Who is the audience and what does that tell us?** Leidig finds 85% male audiences for women far-right influencers. She argues these women function partly as "honeytraps" for male viewers. But she also documents how women are recruited — through community belonging, through the "sisterhood" of the trad/far-right world, through red pill vlogs that reframe antifeminism as self-improvement. Is there a tension in the book's argument about who these influencers are actually radicalizing?

**3. Self-conscious propaganda.** Lana Lokteff: "This is propaganda; it's good marketing." Compare to the Proud Boys' self-presentation in Week 3: Gavin McInnes insisted Proud Boys are "a fraternal organization like the Elk's Lodge." Both involve self-aware branding decisions about how to present the organization. But Lokteff's explicit admission that she is producing propaganda is remarkable. What does it mean when a far-right actor frames their own work this way? Does self-aware propaganda work differently from propaganda its producers believe in?

### Closing Bridge (5 min)

Before Session 4B, set up the empirical question: Leidig documents the *mechanism* — how these influencers operate and what they claim to do. But does it *work*? Do audiences actually shift their attitudes? Do they update their beliefs about what society finds acceptable? Can we measure these effects experimentally? The three Part 2 papers provide three very different approaches to answering these questions.

Also pose a methods challenge: How would you falsify Leidig's claim that these women are effective at radicalizing audiences? What evidence would confirm or disconfirm it? (Students should sit with this before encountering the empirical papers.)

### Anticipated Student Moves — Session 4A

**"Aren't these just women making lifestyle content? How is that different from any beauty blogger?"**
That's precisely Leidig's point — and it's the strategy, not an accident. The indistinguishability from ordinary influencer culture is the mechanism. A recipe for fermented butter looks like a recipe; the white-nationalist sponsorship is in the fine print.

**"Why would women join a movement that explicitly subordinates them?"**
This is the central sociological puzzle. Kathleen Blee (a key reference in the book) found that far-right women gain community, moral purpose, and a sense of "worthiness" from movement participation. Leidig adds: they gain income (YouTube monetization, Patreon, Amazon affiliate links), status, and a sense of resistance against "SJW indoctrination." The cost of subordination is presented as worth the reward of belonging to something meaningful.

**"The alt-right is dead. Why are we studying it?"**
Two answers: (1) The influencers Leidig studies are both alt-right (in origin) and far right (in ideology) — their careers didn't end when the alt-right label fell out of use; (2) the mechanisms Leidig documents — algorithmic radicalization pipelines, microcelebrity tactics, red pill vlogs — are durable and have been adopted across the broader far right. The specific movement died; the infrastructure survived.

**"The 85% male audience means these influencers mainly reach men. So aren't they just a tool for male recruitment?"**
Leidig's answer is nuanced: far-right women influencers tailor content differently for men (lifestyle aspiration, "honeytraps") and women (sisterhood, community). Both are genuine targets. But the male audience dominance is revealing — it says something about who the far right is most urgently trying to recruit, and why women's participation in a male-supremacist movement is paradoxically necessary for that recruitment.

## Session 4B: Bots, Disintermediation, and Media Platforming — Empirical Research on Social Media and the Far Right

**Focus:** Three empirical papers with three very different research designs, asking related versions of the same question: Does social media spread far-right ideas, and if so, how? The session should surface both their findings and the methodological choices that enable or constrain those findings.

---

### Opening Frame (8 min)

Open with the composite clip, Segment 1 only — Nick Griffin being introduced alongside the Justice Secretary, a Shadow Cabinet minister, a Liberal Democrat home affairs spokesman, and the Deputy Chairman of the British Museum ([YouTube, 0:16–1:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4iKfrY9l2kY&t=16)). Then:

"In October 2009, the BBC gave Nick Griffin — leader of the British National Party — 60 minutes of prime-time television alongside cabinet ministers and 8 million live viewers. His party had just won two seats in the European Parliament. A government minister had publicly argued the program should not air. Was it a good idea?"

Take a quick show of hands: yes / no / depends. Write the distribution on the board. Don't resolve it.

"This same debate played out again in 2018. Sky News Australia gave Blair Cottrell — a neo-Nazi organizer who admired Hitler — a prime-time interview on immigration. Sky News pulled the video within 24 hours and issued a formal apology. That same year, Sky News UK gave Tommy Robinson — former EDL leader, a figure you've already encountered in the Bolet & Foos paper — a 20-minute prime-time interview. Over 3,000 complaints were filed with the broadcasting regulator. In each case, the question was the same: does giving extreme right figures a media platform help or hurt?"

"Today's three papers are three different tools for thinking about that question. They don't answer it directly — but they tell us things about the information ecosystem that should change how we think about it. We'll come back to the vote at the end."

Preview the papers as accumulating evidence, not a list:

- **Bastos & Mercea**: What was already happening on social media before any interview aired — automated bots producing noise, distrust, and disengagement. This is the baseline information environment Sky News was deciding whether to enter.
- **Gomes-Franco et al.**: What happens when political figures bypass traditional media entirely — the disintermediation alternative that makes platforming decisions even harder to avoid.
- **Bolet & Foos**: The first experimental evidence on what a far-right media interview actually does to audiences — and the finding that complicates the case for critical journalism.

Raise the syllabus question explicitly: Is "social media" one thing, or a bundle of very different mechanisms that happen to use the same infrastructure?

---

### Mini-Lecture 1: Bastos & Mercea (2018) — What Brexit Bots Actually Did (12 min + 2 min check-in)

**The study**: Discovered *accidentally* during research mapping ideological positions to parliamentary constituencies. The researchers noticed that more than 5% of accounts that had been highly active in the Brexit Twitter debate disappeared *after* the June 23 vote. This yielded a dataset of 13,493 bot-like accounts.

**Key finding**: These bots were NOT megaphones. Their influence on the Brexit debate was "relatively minor." This goes against the dominant popular narrative that Russian bots (or domestic bots) swung Brexit.

**Why the "megaphone" model fails here**: The hypodermic needle model of media effects (message → audience attitude change) has long been challenged by the two-step flow of communication (Lazarsfeld et al.). On social media, topology is even more complex — content moves through networks of opinion leaders before reaching general publics. Bots injected content at one point in the network, but their reach into the broader conversation was limited.

**So what do bots do?** Bastos & Mercea argue that the goal of disinformation is not attitude change but *confusion, disorder, and distrust*. The aim is not to convince people of a position but to make them distrust information environments so thoroughly that they disengage. This is voter suppression by epistemological exhaustion.

**The platform accountability gap**: This study was possible because Twitter has public APIs. Facebook does not. The Cambridge Analytica scandal was visible only because of a whistleblower, not because researchers had structural access. This asymmetry in platform transparency is a major constraint on the field.

**Important distinction**: Automated bots (what Brexit had) ≠ supervised human trolls (what the Internet Research Agency used in the 2016 U.S. election, primarily on Facebook). These are fundamentally different mechanisms with different actors, different platforms, and different effects. Conflating "bot problem" and "social media disinformation" as one issue misreads both.

**Methodological challenge**: Bot detection involves precision/recall tradeoffs — this study had approximately a 26% false positive/negative rate. Two notable false positives were @nero (Milo Yiannopoulos) and @steveemmensUKIP. Disclosing these identities raises its own ethical issues — disclosing deleted tweet content and identifying near-bots risks harm to individuals who may have been falsely classified.

**Check-in (2 min):** "Before we move on — does the Bastos finding change your intuition about the Sky News decision? Griffin and Robinson weren't bots. But what does it mean that the information ecosystem they were entering was already saturated with automated content designed to produce distrust and disengagement? Does that make a human interview more consequential, less consequential, or does it change the terms of the question entirely?"

Don't resolve the answers — collect them briefly and move on. The point is to keep the opening question active as evidence accumulates.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Gomes-Franco et al. (2024) — Disintermediation and the Politician-Influencer (10 min)

**The context**: Brazil, February–May 2022, the pre-electoral period leading to Bolsonaro's narrow loss to Lula da Silva. On January 8, 2023 — one week after Lula's inauguration — Bolsonaro supporters stormed the Brazilian Congress, Supreme Court, and Presidential Palace in scenes explicitly compared to January 6, 2021.

**Key concept: disintermediation** — bypassing traditional media gatekeepers to speak directly to citizens. Not new (Roosevelt's fireside chats; direct democracy traditions) but transformed by social media into something unprecedented in intimacy and scale. Bolsonaro's Instagram account grew from 19.5 million to 25.2 million followers during the period studied — approximately 12% of Brazil's population.

**The mechanism**: Bolsonaro's Thursday Instagram Lives functioned as his own TV show, simultaneously rebroadcast by the television channel Jovem Pan. This is a hybrid model: the disintermediation of social media combined with the reach of broadcast television. He used the format to: (1) discredit journalists and media outlets as "rotten gangs" and "liars"; (2) bypass press conferences and media access; (3) construct himself as a *politician-influencer* — a man of the people, not a media-managed politician.

**Seven message categories** identified through discourse analysis: informative, ideological, paternalistic, negationist, authoritarian, aggressive, and discrediting of the media. Note that several of these (paternalistic, authoritarian, aggressive) map directly onto Leidig's far-right women influencers' rhetorical styles — the specific mechanisms of networked intimacy translate across very different political actors.

**The Gramscian frame**: The paper's conclusion borrows from Antonio Gramsci's *Prison Notebooks*: "The old is dying and the new cannot be born; in this interregnum a great variety of morbid symptoms appear." The Brazilian traditional media model has not perished; a fully formed new model has not yet emerged. In the gap, authoritarian figures fill the vacuum as self-declared producers of truth. This is the structural condition that makes Bolsonaro's strategy possible and potentially replicable.

**Connection to Bastos**: Gomes-Franco's Bolsonaro uses direct Instagram addresses — not bots. The mechanism is disintermediation, not automation. The distinction matters: bots produce noise; disintermediation produces a charismatic alternative authority. Both generate disinformation, but through opposite dynamics (impersonal/automated vs. highly personal/direct).

### Mini-Lecture 3: Bolet & Foos (2025) — Does Platforming Normalize the Far Right? (15 min, including predict-then-reveal)

**The experimental design**: Two real-life interviews with extreme right activists — Blair Cottrell (United Patriots Front, Sky News Australia, August 2018) and Tommy Robinson (former EDL leader, Sky News UK, September 2018). Large survey experiments on nationally representative samples (N ≈ 5,000 in each country). Factorial design varying: (1) content (weather report placebo vs. unchallenged interview vs. challenged interview, UK only); (2) platform announced (Sky News vs. YouTube Channel).

**Before presenting findings — predict-then-reveal (3 min):**

Describe the design without revealing the results. Then ask students to predict (hands or written on a slip of paper):

1. "Did exposure to an unchallenged Robinson interview increase personal agreement with extreme right statements?"
2. "Did it increase people's beliefs about how many *others* agree with extreme right statements?"
3. "Did critical interviewing — journalists pushing back — eliminate those effects?"
4. "Did it matter whether viewers thought they were watching Sky News or a YouTube channel?"

Write the class's modal predictions on the board. Hold them there. Students consistently underestimate the normalization effect and overestimate the corrective power of critical journalism. The gap between their predictions and the findings is where the learning happens.

**Two pre-registered hypotheses and mechanisms**:

- **Persuasion hypothesis**: Exposure to an extreme right interview makes viewers more likely to *personally agree* with extreme right statements.
- **Normalization hypothesis**: Exposure makes viewers more likely to *believe that others agree* with extreme right statements (descriptive norms).

These are distinct processes. Persuasion changes what I think. Normalization changes what I think *everyone else* thinks. Normalization can occur even without persuasion — and can lead to behavior change (self-censorship, political disengagement) even if personal attitudes haven't shifted.

**Findings — reveal against the board predictions**:

1. **Unchallenged interviews increase agreement** with extreme right statements by 3–5 percentage points in both countries. Small but substantively significant given that the statements being tested are extreme. Effect sizes are small-to-medium (d = 0.16–0.18 by Cohen's criteria).

2. **Unchallenged interviews increase normalization** — belief that a larger share of society agrees with extreme right views — by 2–6 percentage points in both countries.

3. **Challenged interviews attenuate persuasion** (attitude effects go to zero in the UK experiment) but **do not eliminate normalization** — descriptive norms still shift by ~2 percentage points even with critical journalism.

4. **Platform type (Sky News vs. YouTube) does not matter** — the source cue effect is negligible. Exposure, not the credibility of the platform on which it occurs, drives both effects. An unchallenged interview on YouTube is as persuasive and normalizing as one on Sky News.

5. **Backlash does not occur**: Consistent with recent literature debunking the "backfire effect," exposure to extreme right content does not produce a strong backlash among liberal-leaning respondents. Attitude updating goes in the direction of the information received, across the ideological spectrum.

**The policy implication the paper draws**: Critical interviewing is better than accommodating interviewing — it attenuates persuasion. But it does not stop normalization. De-platforming (preventing the interview from airing at all) remains the most effective tool for limiting both effects, but at the cost of raising platform governance and free speech questions.

**Connection to the Overton window framework** (Camus & Lebourg, Week 1): Bolet & Foos provide a *causal mechanism* for Overton window shift. Normalization effects — changed beliefs about what others find acceptable — accumulate over repeated exposures. The Overton window doesn't shift because elites decide to move it; it shifts because individuals update their descriptive norms based on what they observe in media.

### Decision Point Activity (15 min)

Return to the opening vote — show of hands again: "Should the BBC have given Nick Griffin this platform? Should Sky News have aired the Tommy Robinson interview? Has anyone changed their answer?"

Small groups of 3, 5 minutes. One question only: **"Should Sky News have aired the Robinson interview? If not, what should they have done instead?"** Groups should use at least two of the papers' findings as evidence for their position.

Full-class debrief, 8 minutes. The goal is not consensus but surfacing the genuine trade-off:

- **The free speech / democratic legitimacy argument** (Huhne's position in the Griffin clip): Once a party crosses an electoral threshold, excluding it from broadcast debate is harder to justify than including it. The cure — deciding who gets to speak — may be worse than the disease.
- **The empirical argument for de-platforming** (Bolet/Foos's own conclusion): De-platforming remains the most effective tool for limiting both persuasion and normalization effects. Critical journalism helps with persuasion but not normalization. There is no challenged-interview condition that eliminates the normalization effect entirely.
- **The Gramscian complication** (Gomes-Franco): If traditional media credibility is already collapsing and authoritarian figures are filling the vacuum with direct-to-audience content, refusing to platform them on mainstream media may simply accelerate their migration to alternative channels where they face no challenge at all.
- **The Bastos baseline** (often missed): The information environment was already hostile before Sky News made its decision. The question is not whether to introduce disinformation into a clean ecosystem but whether to add one more vector to an already-compromised one.

The three questions worth leaving unresolved: (1) Does the empirical evidence from Bolet/Foos justify the editorial decision to de-platform, or does it only establish the effect-size of platforming, leaving the normative question open? (2) Is the Gomes-Franco argument — that de-platforming accelerates migration to alt-tech — empirically supported or speculative? (3) Who should make these decisions, and by what process?

For reference, the four-paper mechanism summary (useful for the debrief if students need anchoring):

| Paper | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Leidig | Influencer culture / algorithmic radicalization | Gradual radicalization of individuals |
| Bastos | Automated bots / network topology | Noise, distrust, disengagement |
| Gomes-Franco | Disintermediation / politician-influencer | Alternative authority construction |
| Bolet/Foos | Media platforming | Persuasion + normalization of descriptive norms |

---

### Closing Question (5 min)

Bolet & Foos show that normalization occurs even when viewers disagree with the content — they still update their beliefs about what *others* think. Once people believe that extreme right views are widely held, the social norm against expressing those views erodes. Camus & Lebourg call this the normalization process; Bursztyn et al. (in the Bolet/Foos references) call it the "erosion of social norms."

Close with: "Is it possible to *de-normalize* once normalization has occurred? What would reversal look like, and is there any evidence that it happens?" This is an open question in the literature — and connects directly to Week 5 (Valentim on normalization) and Week 7 (gender and far-right vote share).

#### How to Run the Clip in the Session

**Before the clips:** Pose the anchoring question — "In October 2009, the BBC gave Nick Griffin — leader of the British National Party — 60 minutes of prime-time television alongside cabinet ministers and a live studio audience. Was that a good idea?" Take a quick show of hands: yes / no / depends. Do not resolve it. "We're going to watch about ten minutes of the program, then look at what the evidence says actually happened."

**After Segment 2** (before proceeding to Segment 3): Brief discussion prompt on the metapolitical strategy connection to Leidig (see above). Keep to 2 minutes — this is a mid-clip check-in, not a full discussion.

**After Segment 4:** Return to the opening vote — "Who wants to change their answer?" Then run the predict-then-reveal sequence:

1. **Predict:** "Before I show you the data — did watching that interview make you personally more sympathetic to Griffin's position? Less? Neither?" (Expected: most say less or neither.) "Did watching it change what you think *other people* in Britain believe about immigration?" (This is the harder, more uncomfortable question — students usually pause.)

2. **Reveal:** Show the Bolet & Foos findings. Challenged interviews (like this one) attenuate persuasion to near zero — so Warsi and Straw were probably right that personally persuading viewers toward Griffin's position was not the effect. But normalization — descriptive norms, beliefs about what others think — still shifted by approximately 2 percentage points even under challenged conditions. Straw's "catastrophic week" was correct on persuasion; Huhne's "Christmas present" was closer to correct on normalization.

3. **Real-world check:** The BNP's electoral performance in May 2010 — seven months after this program — was 563,000 votes, 1.9% of the national vote, their all-time peak, before the party collapsed in subsequent elections. Confounded by many factors, but directionally consistent with a short-term normalization effect rather than the backlash Straw predicted.

4. **Close with the policy question:** "Given this, what should the BBC have done? And what should Sky News Australia have done with Blair Cottrell? And what should Twitter/X do with accounts that produce content like this?" This sets up the de-platforming discussion and the free speech tension that Bolet/Foos explicitly raises in their conclusion.

### Methods Notes — Session 4B

This is the most methodologically heterogeneous week in the course. All three papers are empirical but use incompatible research designs:

- **Bastos & Mercea**: Computational social science — natural quasi-experiment (accounts disappeared after Brexit), text-as-data (ideology scoring), network analysis. Advantage: massive scale; identifies patterns invisible to qualitative methods. Limitation: observational (causal identification is imperfect); 26% false positive/negative rate for bot detection.

- **Gomes-Franco et al.**: Qualitative — critical discourse analysis, pragmatic language analysis, visual analysis, and grounded theory, applied to a purposive sample of 10 Instagram Live videos. Advantage: captures the textual and rhetorical mechanisms of disintermediation with granular detail. Limitation: small N, no causal claim, Brazil-specific findings may not generalize.

- **Bolet & Foos**: Survey experiment — pre-registered, randomized assignment, nationally representative samples, factorial design. Advantage: strongest internal validity of the three; can make causal claims about the effect of the treatment (interview exposure) on the outcome. Limitation: short-term attitude measurement (debriefed immediately); external validity depends on assuming the experimental audio translates to the real-world media viewing experience.

Worth raising with students: What can each method tell us that the others cannot? The survey experiment can establish that X causes Y. The qualitative study can identify the rhetorical mechanisms by which X operates. The computational study can analyze X at a scale no qualitative method can reach. These are complementary, not competing.

**Instructor's Research Note — Related Ongoing Work**

A working paper by Holverscheid & Seawright ("Persuasion, Strategy, and the Microdynamics of Right-Wing Mainstreaming in the U.S.") addresses the question this session leaves implicit: if far-right alternative media doesn't shift voter attitudes directly, what does it accomplish at the political level? Using BERTopic topic modeling on transcripts from InfoWars, the Proud Boys, and the Ben Shapiro Show combined with vector autoregression on Congressional floor speeches (2003–2024), the paper finds that far-right podcast discourse Granger-causes Republican Congressional speech patterns — not vice versa — in about 9% of topics (direct mainstreaming) and runs bidirectionally in another 28% (two-way dialogue). The election denial narrative is the exemplary case: the far-right podcast corpus introduced the elite/institution-centric fraud frame before Trump's circle formally adopted it. A pre/post-Trump cohort split shows the far right shifted over time from ideological supplier to full discursive partner in Republican politics. This completes the chain the session traces: Bolet & Foos show what happens when far-right elites access mainstream media audiences; this paper shows the upstream mechanism by which far-right alternative media shapes what those elites say in the first place.

### Anticipated Student Moves — Session 4B

**"So bots don't work — good, I was worried about that."**
Caution: Bastos shows bots don't function as megaphones in attitude change. But Bastos also shows the disinformation goal isn't attitude change — it's distrust and disengagement. The bots may have been effective at their actual goal even while being ineffective at the goal we usually attribute to them.

**"Critical journalism works, right? Problem solved."**
Bolet & Foos show that challenged interviews attenuate persuasion (personal attitudes don't shift) but normalization still occurs at a reduced rate. Critical journalism is better than accommodating journalism — but it is not sufficient to prevent normalization. The paper explicitly notes de-platforming is more effective, which raises the harder question about when it's justified.

**"Isn't this just about Brazil / Australia / UK? What does it tell us about the U.S.?"**
Two responses: (1) Bolet/Foos finds consistent results across two quite different media environments (Australia, with a more conservative-dominated media, and the UK, with a more mixed landscape) — this increases confidence in generalizability. (2) Gomes-Franco explicitly compares Bolsonaro to other disintermediating politicians including Trump and AMLO; the mechanism is general.

**"Platform type doesn't matter in Bolet/Foos — doesn't that contradict what we know about echo chambers?"**
Good tension to surface. Bolet/Foos finds the source cue (Sky News vs. YouTube) doesn't matter much — exposure drives effects, not credibility of the source. This is consistent with Peterson & Allamong (2022), cited in the paper, showing unfamiliar media sources are as effective as familiar ones at shifting opinion. The echo chamber literature focuses on *which* content people encounter, not on *how* they receive a specific piece of content once encountered.
