**Readings:**

**Syllabus framing question:** Is the rise of the radical right a change in what people believe — or a change in what people are willing to say they believe?

## Session 5A: A Norms Theory of Radical-Right Normalization

**Focus:** Valentim's book makes a single, powerful claim: you do not need to assume that voters became more radical-right to explain the rise of radical-right parties. You only need to assume that the social costs of *expressing* preexisting radical-right views declined. This reframes the empirical question — from "why do people hold these views?" to "why do people stop hiding them?" — and connects to the Bolet/Foos normalization mechanism from Week 4B at the level of whole societies and political systems.

---

### Opening Hook (8 min)

Begin with the Portuguese case that opens the book. Portugal was the great European exception: a Western democracy that, through the refugee crisis, the rise of Trump, Bolsonaro, Salvini, Orbán, and the AfD, simply did not produce a successful radical-right party. Scholars wrote papers explaining the exceptionalism. Journalists praised Portuguese political culture. The PNR (the far-right party) never broke 0.2% of the national vote.

And then in 2019, Chega entered parliament with 1.3% of the vote. By 2022 it had 7.2%. By 2024, 18.1% — third largest party in the country.

Nothing significant changed. There was no major economic shock, no migration influx, no institutional reform lowering the parliamentary threshold. What changed was the arrival of a skilled political entrepreneur — André Ventura — who realized that xenophobic, norm-breaching appeals had wide electoral support that nobody had previously tapped.

The question Valentim is asking: where did that 18% come from? Were there really no Portuguese citizens with radical-right views before 2019? Or were those preferences *already there* — just hidden?

Put the core distinction on the board:

> **Did voters change their minds? Or did they stop hiding what they already believed?**

Valentim's answer is the second. And the proof is in how fast Chega grew. Preferences don't change that fast. Social norms can.

### Mini-Lecture 1: The Two-Step Model (15 min)

**The dominant assumption in political behavior research:**

The conventional framework treats the relationship between attitudes and behavior as essentially automatic. Voters develop preferences → they express those preferences in behavior. When we observe a change in behavior, we look for a change in preferences to explain it. The literature on the radical right has followed this template: economic anxiety, cultural backlash, immigration attitudes — all are attempts to identify what *changed inside voters' heads*.

Valentim's challenge: this assumption struggles with the speed and breadth of radical-right electoral growth. Figure 1.1 in the book plots the vote share evolution of Front National (France), Jobbik (Hungary), Swedish Democrats, and Vox (Spain) in their first elections. In all four cases, parties go from near-zero to 10–20% in a small number of elections. The problem: decades of research show that political attitudes and preferences are *remarkably stable* in the long run — including attitudes on topics like immigration and ethnic minorities (Kustov, Laaker, and Reller 2021). The speed required for preference change to explain Figure 1.1 is "of an order of magnitude incompatible with previous behavioral research."

**The alternative: a two-step model.**

Step 1: Individuals develop a preference. (This is what the existing literature explains.)

Step 2: They decide whether to *express* that preference, based on perceived social costs. This second step is what Valentim focuses on — and what previous work has largely ignored.

The mechanism: **social norms**. Norms are behavioral rules individuals follow if they think others follow them, believe they should be followed, and may sanction those who deviate (Bicchieri 2017). If there is a strong social norm *against* expressing a political preference, individuals will often suppress that preference in public settings — even if they hold it privately. This is **preference falsification** (Kuran 1987, 1995): publicly expressing a preference different from one's private preference to avoid social sanctions.

Key implication: behavioral change does not require preference change. It only requires that the *perceived social cost* of expressing a preexisting preference declines. Norms can change very fast. This explains what preference-change models cannot: quick-yet-stable shifts in radical-right behavior.

*Disciplinary bridge (optional, 2 min):* Students in communication or sociology courses will have encountered this behavior under a different name: the **spiral of silence** (Noelle-Neumann 1974). Noelle-Neumann's mechanism is essentially preference falsification — people who perceive their views to be in the minority stay silent to avoid social isolation, which makes those views appear even more marginal, reinforcing the silence. The spiral is Valentim's latency equilibrium described in communication-theory terms. Worth flagging explicitly if students have a comm background, since the two literatures have developed almost entirely in parallel without citing each other.

**Private vs. public behaviors.**

Valentim draws a foundational distinction:

- *Private behaviors*: settings where no potential sanctioner can observe one's action. The voting booth is the central example — nobody sees who you vote for, so social sanctions are impossible.
- *Public behaviors*: settings where at least some potential sanctioners can observe. Survey interviews, political conversations, protests, wearing a party button — all are public to varying degrees.

This distinction generates the book's key empirical opportunity: comparing what people *do* in private (vote) with what they *say* in public (survey response). The gap between the two is a direct measure of preference falsification — and thus of the strength of the social norm.

**The typology of preferences (Table 2.1):**

| | Expressed in voting | Not expressed in voting |
|---|---|---|
| **Expressed publicly** | Open preference | Staged preference |
| **Not expressed publicly** | Covert preference | Silent preference |

The three phases of normalization map onto movement across this table:
- *Latency*: preferences are silent (hidden in both voting and public behavior)
- *Activation*: preferences become covert (expressed in voting but not yet publicly)
- *Surfacing*: preferences become open (expressed in both)

### Mini-Lecture 2: The Three Phases of Normalization (15 min)

**Phase 1: Latency (stable equilibrium)**

Strong anti-radical-right norms → voters falsify preferences → politicians underestimate latent support → skilled politicians don't run for radical-right parties → radical-right parties end up with less skilled leaders → even private radical-right voters don't bother voting for them → preferences remain silent → norm appears even stronger → repeat.

This is a self-reinforcing equilibrium. The Germany case illustrates it: in 2002, CSES data shows that German voters were dramatically less likely to express radical-right views in telephone interviews (public mode) than in self-administered surveys (private mode). The proportion willing to say they felt represented by a radical-right party was about 15% in public of what it was in private. The preference was *there*; the expression of it was suppressed.

The key observable implication of latency is the *reported vote* measure: radical-right parties' declared vote share in post-election surveys is systematically *lower* than their official vote share. For every ten actual radical-right voters, approximately 1.5 fewer are willing to admit it in a survey. Valentim validates this measure across 95 radical-right parties in the CSES: the underreporting is consistent and robust. By contrast, Green parties are *overreported* — there is a social norm *for* environmental support, generating the inverse pattern.

**Phase 2: Activation (unstable equilibrium)**

Two necessary conditions for breaking out of latency: an *exogenous trigger* and a *political entrepreneur*.

The trigger creates a moment of societal commotion — a window when norms become temporarily looser. Islamist terrorist attacks are the book's empirical case (Chapter 6): after attacks, individuals feel momentarily more comfortable expressing outgroup hostility (the norm against prejudice is temporarily suspended). Crucially, this doesn't destroy the norm — it's a temporary peak. But it's sufficient to *update politicians' beliefs* about how much latent radical-right support exists.

This updated belief changes the self-selection calculus. A skilled politician — one with the charisma, organizational ability, and institutional knowledge to build an electoral coalition — now sees an opportunity. They are willing to pay the social cost of running on a radical-right platform because the potential payoff (electoral breakthrough, long-term political relevance) justifies it. Less skilled politicians continue to avoid it. The result: radical-right parties acquire more capable leadership.

The more skilled entrepreneur can now mobilize what was previously silent support. Voters who privately held radical-right views but didn't vote for incompetent radical-right candidates now have a viable option. Their preferences become *covert* — expressed in the voting booth but still hidden in public behavior.

Activation is *unstable*: either the entrepreneur is successful enough to tip the society into the surfacing phase, or the moment passes and the norm reasserts itself.

**Phase 3: Surfacing (stable equilibrium)**

Electoral success erodes the anti-radical-right norm through two mechanisms:

*Voter level*: As the radical-right party grows, its supporters realize there is a critical mass of like-minded others. The perceived social cost of expressing radical-right views declines because the risk of social sanctions falls — there are enough fellow supporters to make ostracism less likely. Preferences shift from covert to open. Reported vote increases: the gap between official and declared vote share narrows.

*Politician level*: Other politicians — including mainstream-party politicians — update their beliefs about the electoral viability of the radical right. In the UK, two Conservative MPs switched to UKIP when it became clear UKIP could win votes. In Germany, after the AfD's 2017 breakthrough, AfD MPs in the Bundestag had significantly higher educational credentials than the prior generation of German radical-right politicians — a direct indicator that more skilled politicians had entered the pool.

The Chega/Portugal case: once Ventura demonstrated the viability of the radical-right platform, the party grew from 1.3% to 18.1% in five years. Nothing changed in the structural conditions. The norm changed.

Surfacing is stable for the same self-reinforcing logic as latency — but running in the opposite direction.

### Mini-Lecture 3: The Supply Side — Political Entrepreneurs and Skill (10 min)

This is the part of the book that most political science courses skip, and the part most likely to generate productive student discussion, because it concerns politicians' strategic behavior rather than voter psychology.

**The skill differential in the latency phase:**

If skilled politicians systematically avoid radical-right platforms during latency, the observable prediction is that radical-right candidates should be *less educated and less experienced* than candidates for other parties in the same period. Valentim tests this in Chapter 5 using data on the educational credentials of party leaders across 95 radical-right parties. The finding is strong and consistent: radical-right leaders have less formal education than other party leaders in the latency phase.

The mechanism is not that the radical right *attracts* less skilled politicians. It is that the latency equilibrium *filters out* the more skilled ones — because skilled politicians have more to lose by attaching themselves to a stigmatized platform, and more to gain from alternatives.

**The trigger and entrepreneur mechanism:**

Chapter 6 shows that after Islamist terrorist attacks, radical-right parties are significantly more likely to acquire new, more skilled leaders — measured by the difference in educational credentials between incoming and outgoing party leaders. The trigger loosens norms; more skilled politicians sense the opportunity; they enter the race.

The three-way interaction in Table 6.3 is the key result: new radical-right leaders with a college degree get substantially higher vote shares than new leaders without one — but only in radical-right parties. For other parties, leader skill makes no significant difference. This means the activist latent demand for radical-right policy was there; it simply had to be mobilized by someone capable of doing so.

**The implication for how we understand electoral politics:**

The standard model assumes parties supply platforms and voters choose between them based on preference proximity. Valentim's model complicates the supply side: parties cannot freely choose their platform because the available pool of politicians is constrained by the social costs of running on a given platform. In a strong-norm environment, radical-right parties get structurally worse candidates — not because the radical right is inherently unappealing as a career option, but because social norms filter out the very people who would make it electorally competitive.

### Discussion Activity: Applying the Three Phases (15 min)

Small groups (3–4 students), 8 minutes. Each group gets one case:

**Case A — The United States, Trump 2016:** Were Trump voters expressing previously silent preferences, or did Trump change the preferences of a previously moderate Republican electorate? What evidence would distinguish these two accounts? What was the trigger and who was the political entrepreneur?

**Case B — Germany, the AfD 2017:** Valentim's empirical case. Before 2017, German voters with radical-right views expressed them in private surveys but not in public. After the AfD breakthrough, reported vote increased. Is this consistent with the Valentim model? What were the triggers? What was the evidence of latency before 2013?

**Case C — Sweden, the Sweden Democrats:** The SD were founded in 1988 and spent nearly two decades in the latency phase — dismissed as neo-Nazis, unable to attract skilled politicians. By 2010 they entered parliament; by 2022 they were the second-largest party. Map this trajectory onto Valentim's three phases. What changed in 2010 and why?

**Case D — France, the Front National / Rassemblement National:** Marine Le Pen took over from her father in 2011 and immediately began "de-demonization" (*dédiabolisation*) — rebranding the party, expelling explicit antisemites, emphasizing economic issues. Is this consistent with Valentim's supply-side mechanism? Is Marine Le Pen a political entrepreneur in Valentim's sense?

Full-class debrief (7 min): Are all four cases consistent with the model? Do any cases strain it? What would count as a disconfirming case — a country where radical-right preferences are demonstrably *absent* rather than merely suppressed?

### Methods Notes — Session 5A

Valentim's book is primarily theoretical but uses multiple empirical strategies to test observable implications:

**Reported vote (Chs. 4, 5, 7):** Compares official vote shares (how many people actually voted for each party) to declared vote shares in post-election surveys (how many say they did). The gap is a measure of preference falsification — and thus of social norm strength. Key validation: the gap is negative for radical-right parties (under-declaring), positive for Green parties (over-declaring), and correlates with party vote share across time. Students may push back that under-reporting of radical-right vote could reflect memory error or survey embarrassment independent of norms; worth addressing whether these confounds are distinguishable from the norms story.

**Interview mode comparison (Ch. 8 — Germany):** Uses CSES data from Germany 2002 with both telephone (public) and self-administered (private) survey modes. Radical-right identification and outgroup hostility are substantially higher in the private mode — direct evidence of preference falsification in the latency phase.

**Islamist attack analysis (Ch. 6):** Uses cross-national panel data with fixed effects to show that attack intensity predicts new radical-right leader entry and higher average skill of those leaders. The key identification assumption is that attacks are quasi-random shocks to the normative environment. Worth noting for students that the mechanism here is not that attacks *create* radical-right preferences — it's that they temporarily loosen norms, revealing preferences that were already present.

**Regression discontinuity design (Ch. 7):** Uses the electoral threshold (typically 5%) as a discontinuity — parties that just barely enter parliament vs. just barely fail to do so. Parties that enter see increases in reported vote, suggesting parliamentary presence (a signal of legitimacy and viability) erodes the stigma of supporting them. This is probably the most causally credible design in the book.

### Anticipated Student Moves — Session 5A

**"Isn't this just saying people were always secretly racist?"**
No — the argument doesn't require radical-right preferences to be a fixed natural endowment. It only requires that, at any given moment, the distribution of private preferences is *more radical-right* than the distribution of publicly expressed preferences, because social norms suppress expression. The preferences themselves can still have causes — economic anxiety, cultural threat, immigration exposure. The Valentim argument is about the gap between the preference and its expression, not about the origin of the preference.

**"If preferences are stable, why do they sometimes decline?"**
Good empirical question. The model predicts that surfacing is a stable equilibrium, but it's not irreversible. If anti-radical-right norms are reinforced — through elite rhetoric, legal sanctions, social taboo restoration — the latency equilibrium could theoretically be restored. The book is less explicit about this direction. The historical evidence from Germany post-1945 could be read as a case of forced norm restoration. More recent evidence on whether norm reinforcement campaigns (Pledge campaigns, anti-hate speech laws) actually reverse normalization is mixed.

**"Does this explain Trump? Or Brexit?"**
Yes, cautiously. Both fit the model: an exogenous trigger (the 2015 refugee crisis and economic anxiety post-2008), a political entrepreneur who sensed latent demand (Trump; Farage), and rapid surfacing of preferences that previous survey research had consistently measured as more widespread than their political expression suggested. The model is explicitly agnostic about *which* preferences were falsified — it requires only that private and public expressions diverge, and that the divergence is norm-driven.

**"So the media exposure effects in Bolet/Foos are doing Valentim's normalization?"**
Exactly right — and this should be pulled to the surface if a student makes the connection. Bolet/Foos shows the micro-mechanism: one interview shifts individual descriptive norms by ~2pp. Valentim shows what happens when enough individual-level norm shifts accumulate at the societal level: the preference suppression equilibrium becomes unstable and tips. The two papers are operating at different levels of analysis but describing the same process.

**"Isn't the reported vote measure just measuring social desirability bias, not norms?"**
This is the most technically sophisticated objection and deserves a careful response. Social desirability bias is one mechanism by which norms produce preference falsification — they are not competing explanations but nested ones. The broader category is preference falsification; social desirability bias is the survey-level manifestation. The key evidence that it's norms specifically (rather than generic embarrassment) is the systematic patterning: the bias is larger for stigmatized parties (radical right), reversed for pro-normative parties (Green), and correlates with external measures of norm strength across countries.

### Connection to the Self-Censorship Literature — Session 5A

Valentim's preference falsification mechanism is the same behavioral phenomenon studied under different names across the communication, social psychology, and political science literatures. Two recent papers are particularly useful for instructors — not as required readings for students, but as background for facilitating discussion and fielding questions about the empirical basis of the norms claim.

**Gibson & Sutherland (2023), "Keeping Your Mouth Shut: Spiraling Self-Censorship in the United States," *Political Science Quarterly* 138(3): 361–376.**

Gibson & Sutherland use a survey item that Samuel Stouffer first asked in 1954 — "Do you or don't you feel as free to speak your mind as you used to?" — replicated in surveys up through 2020. The trend is striking: in 1954, at the height of McCarthyism, only 13% of Americans reported feeling less free than before. By 2020, that figure had reached 46% — a tripling over 66 years. Self-censorship is now the modal experience for a near-majority of the American public.

Two findings connect most directly to Valentim:

*The polarization correlation.* The over-time increase in self-censorship correlates r = 0.91 with ideological affective polarization (and r = 0.86 with partisan affective polarization). As the political environment became more polarized, more people suppressed their views. Gibson & Sutherland interpret this cautiously (correlation, not established causation), but the magnitude is hard to dismiss: the social norm environment — Valentim's key variable — co-moves almost perfectly with affective polarization over the period.

*The conservative asymmetry.* In 2020, conservatives report substantially more self-censorship than liberals: a 37 percentage-point gap in the probability of feeling free to speak one's mind between extreme liberals and extreme conservatives, with conservatives far more likely to self-censor. This is directly consistent with Valentim's argument: if the dominant social norm is anti-radical-right, then the population most likely to falsify preferences is right-leaning voters. The Gibson & Sutherland finding provides macro-level survey evidence for exactly the direction of preference suppression Valentim theorizes.

*The micro-environment driver.* Gibson & Sutherland find that macro-level political intolerance and civil liberties opposition do not predict individual self-censorship — what matters is the micro-environment: fear of social isolation from friends, family, and neighbors. This is precisely the mechanism Valentim's norms theory requires. It is not government repression or formal sanction; it is informal interpersonal social pressure.

**Burnett, Knighton & Wilson (2022), "The Self-Censoring Majority: How Political Identity and Ideology Impacts Willingness to Self-Censor and Fear of Isolation in the United States," *Social Media + Society* 8(3).**

Burnett et al. test the spiral of silence on social media using a 2020 Qualtrics panel (N = 1,047). Their key finding inverts the classical spiral of silence prediction: it is the *majority in the middle* (moderate conservatives, independents, moderate liberals) who self-censor most, while the *hardcore vocal minority* at both extremes (strong conservatives and strong liberals) self-censors least. The margins are the loudest; the center is the quietest.

This creates a productive tension with Valentim's framework. Valentim assumes that radical-right preference-holders are suppressed by an anti-radical-right norm — which implies that the suppressed group is to the right of the median. But Burnett et al. suggest that in the US in 2020, it is moderates — not just right-leaning voters — who are suppressed by the loudness of the ideological extremes on both sides. One reconciliation: Valentim is tracking suppression on a *specific* ideological dimension (radical-right vs. not), while Burnett et al. track general political self-censorship. A moderate conservative who publicly disavows immigration restriction but votes for restriction is exhibiting Valentim's preference falsification even if they don't register as a "strongly self-censoring" person on the Burnett et al. measure.

A second useful finding: Burnett et al. find that *fear of isolation* (FOI) is not the primary driver of self-censorship among political identities — the self-censorship difference between the middle and the extremes is driven by something else (perhaps identity salience, or perceived cost-benefit calculation) rather than fear of social exclusion. This is a mild complication for the spiral of silence as Noelle-Neumann stated it, but does not challenge the preference falsification mechanism per se: people can self-censor for reasons other than FOI (reputational cost, anticipated hostility, perceived futility of expression).

**For classroom use:**

The most productive point to introduce either paper is after the Germany/AfD interview-mode comparison (Phase 1 of the three phases): Valentim's private-vs-public survey mode difference is methodologically identical to what Gibson & Sutherland are measuring at the aggregate level. The connection can be made briefly:

> "Gibson and Sutherland found that by 2020, nearly half of Americans felt less free to speak their minds than they used to — and that this tracks almost exactly with the rise of affective polarization. They also found that conservatives self-censor substantially more than liberals. If Valentim is right about the mechanism, what would we expect to see in a country where the social norm is anti-radical-right? Exactly that pattern."

The Burnett et al. finding about the self-censoring majority is best used as a discussion provocation, particularly for the discussion activity: if the *moderate middle* is now the most silenced group on social media, what does that do to the perceived opinion climate? Does it accelerate normalization (because moderates don't push back), or does it create conditions for a different kind of backlash?

### Connections Forward — Session 5A

- **Session 5B (Bischof & Wagner 2019; Pal 2025):** Bischof & Wagner provides experimental evidence for the legitimization mechanism at the heart of Valentim's surfacing phase — and extends it by showing an unexpected backlash polarization effect on opposing voters that Valentim's model doesn't address. Pal extends the normalization framework beyond the electoral arena entirely, showing that the world's most successful far-right mobilization achieved normalization primarily through civil society organizational diffusion rather than through party politics.

- **Week 6 (Violence):** The surfacing equilibrium has implications for political violence. As radical-right preferences move from silent/covert to open, the social infrastructure for radical-right mobilization strengthens. Accelerationist violence in Valentim's framework is a phenomenon of the late activation or early surfacing phase — a moment when the norm is eroding but not yet fully shifted, and some actors choose physical expression rather than waiting for political channels.

- **Week 7 (Gender):** The Valentim framework applies directly to gender-related radical-right positions — anti-feminist, anti-gender, anti-LGBTQ+ preferences. Anduiza & Rico (2022), on the Week 7 syllabus, document that far-right support is substantially predicted by sexism. Valentim's question is whether these preferences were always present but suppressed. The evidence on the speed of anti-feminist normalization in recent years suggests they were.

- **Week 8 (Electoral Politics):** Valentim's model is explicitly a theory of electoral behavior. The Week 8 readings on party strategy and voter dealignment should be read against his supply-side argument: parties' strategic decisions about whether to adopt radical-right positions are, in Valentim's model, a response to their beliefs about the prevalence of latent radical-right preferences in the electorate — beliefs that are distorted by preference falsification.

## Session 5B: Polarization, Organizational Diffusion, and the Limits of the Electoral Frame

**Focus:** Session 5A established Valentim's account of how normalization happens — through preference falsification, norm erosion, and the supply-side dynamics of political entrepreneurship. Session 5B extends that account in two directions. Bischof & Wagner (2019) test what happens to voter ideology when a radical-right party actually enters parliament — and find not just the legitimization effect Valentim predicts, but an equal and opposite backlash polarization among opposing voters. Pal (2025) then asks a more fundamental question: what if normalization doesn't primarily happen through electoral channels at all? Drawing on the world's largest far-right interorganizational network — India's RSS/Sangh — Pal argues that organizational diffusion through civil society is a more durable and analytically underappreciated mechanism of far-right success.

Together, these papers push students toward two uncomfortable conclusions: that stopping the radical right through electoral defeat may not depolarize a society; and that the European party-focused lens misses the most successful far-right mobilization in history.

---

### Opening Hook (8 min)

Write on the board: **Netherlands, 2002. The Lijst Pim Fortuyn enters parliament for the first time. What happens to Dutch voters?**

Take brief predictions — show of hands: voters become more right-wing? Stay the same? Divide?

Reveal: right-wing party identifiers moved further right *and* left-wing party identifiers moved further left — simultaneously, within weeks of the election, within the same individuals surveyed before and after. The center thinned. Both sides radicalized.

This is the finding from Bischof & Wagner's panel study of 1,574 Dutch voters before and after the 2002 election. Polarization increased by a statistically significant and substantively large amount — measured as the squared distance of each individual voter from the median position.

The puzzle this creates: Valentim tells us that normalization moves radical-right preferences from silent/covert to open. That's the legitimization effect — and it's confirmed here. But the backlash effect on opposing voters is something Valentim's model doesn't predict. If normalization creates counter-mobilization on the left, what does that mean for the overall political system? And does it help explain the polarized political landscape of contemporary democracies?

Bridge to Pal: Bischof & Wagner are studying a *single electoral event* in a European parliamentary system. Their mechanism requires a party to enter parliament for the first time. But what about a far-right movement that has never primarily operated through party politics? The world's largest far-right network — India's Sangh — was achieving normalization for decades before the BJP won a national majority in 2014. What's driving *that* process?

### Mini-Lecture 1: Bischof & Wagner — Legitimization, Backlash, and the Polarizing Power of Radical Party Entry (18 min)

**The core argument:**

The entry of a radical-right party into parliament is a legitimizing event. It signals to every voter in the country that views previously understood as extreme are now sufficiently mainstream to win parliamentary representation. This signal has two effects that run in opposite directions — and together produce ideological polarization across the full political spectrum.

*Legitimization effect*: Voters who sympathize with the radical party had already held radical-right views privately. The party's electoral success updates their perception of the social norm: radical-right views are apparently not as fringe as they thought. They feel freer to declare their positions openly. They move further toward the right on the left-right scale — not because their underlying views changed, but because the social cost of expressing them declined. This is Valentim's surfacing mechanism, tested at the individual level.

*Backlash effect*: Voters on the opposing side of the spectrum react to the perceived normalization of views they regard as threatening or objectionable. The party's success functions as an "out-party cue" — it signals that extreme positions are gaining ground, which motivates opposing voters to publicly reinforce their distance from those positions. Left-wing voters move further left. Minorities and groups whose rights are implicated by radical-right platforms feel the need for more emphatic opposition.

The net result: the variance of voter positions increases. The political center thins. Both sides move away from it.

**Three studies:**

*Study 1 (Netherlands 2002)*: The 2002 Dutch Parliamentary Election Study followed 1,574 voters before and after the election. The LPF's dramatic entry — the party was founded only 3 months before the election — constitutes a near-shock: it was unexpected, attention-grabbing, and unprecedented. A placebo test rules out Pim Fortuyn's assassination (9 days before the election) as the driver: polarization did not increase between respondents interviewed before vs. after the murder, but did increase significantly after the election result. The legitimization effect is cleanest for LPF identifiers and CDA voters (who moved right); the backlash effect is clearest for PvdA and SP identifiers (who moved left).

*Study 2 (Eurobarometer, 17 countries, 1973–2016)*: Moving from a single case to the long run. Countries that experienced radical-right party entry into parliament show significantly higher voter polarization than matched control countries — an effect of approximately half a standard deviation on the left-right scale. Countries using electoral thresholds (where entry is harder and thus more shock-like when it occurs) show even larger effects.

*Study 3 (Synthetic control)*: Uses a better-identified causal strategy — constructing a synthetic counterfactual for each treated country from a weighted combination of untreated countries. Results replicate the cross-national finding.

**The Green party asymmetry:**

A crucial null result: the same analyses find no significant polarizing effect for Green party entry into parliament. Bischof & Wagner propose two explanations. First, radical-right party entry breaks a greater historical taboo — it is associated with historical extremism in a way that Green party entry is not, generating stronger legitimization and backlash signals. Second, radical-right parties are more effective "issue entrepreneurs," succeeding in reorienting political competition around new, more divisive issues (immigration, national identity) rather than adapting to existing issue dimensions.

**Connection to Valentim:**

The legitimization effect is the micro-level confirmation of Valentim's surfacing mechanism: parliamentary presence → updated norm perceptions → covert preferences become open. The reported vote gap narrows. The key extension B&W provide is temporal precision: this process is detectable within weeks of an election, not just over years. Normalization is faster than the gradual preference-change models assume.

The backlash effect is where B&W go beyond Valentim. Valentim's model focuses on the dynamics within the radical-right support base; B&W show that normalization simultaneously mobilizes and radicalizes the opposition. This has implications for understanding why polarized party systems and adversarial democratic cultures tend to follow radical-right breakthroughs rather than precede them.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Pal — Organizational Diffusion and the World's Most Successful Far-Right Mobilization (20 min)

**Reframing the question:**

The mainstream study of the far right begins with parties and elections. This makes sense for European democracies, where party politics is the primary arena of political contestation. But it creates a systematic blind spot: far-right mobilizations that achieve normalization primarily through civil society rather than through electoral competition.

Pal's argument: far-right success should be measured by normalization — the process by which previously unsayable political claims become sayable, legitimate parts of public discourse. Measured this way, the most successful far-right mobilization in history is not the AfD, or the National Front, or MAGA. It is the Indian far right: the network of organizations that emerge from and remain accountable to the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS).

The Sangh — often called the Sangh Parivar (family) — includes: the RSS itself; the BJP (currently the world's largest political party); the VHP (Vishwa Hindu Parishad); the Bajrang Dal; the world's largest student union; the world's largest labor union; India Development and Relief Fund; and roughly 2,831 mapped constituent organizations spanning education, healthcare, media, religious affairs, community outreach, and diaspora mobilization. In electoral terms, the BJP has ruled India since 2014 and has governed 49 state governments. In normalization terms, Hindu nationalist assumptions have so thoroughly penetrated Indian civil society, governance, and the judiciary that "far-right politics in India is no longer contentious politics, but is now the language of governance."

**Organizational diffusion:**

The mechanism Pal identifies as explaining the Sangh's success is *organizational diffusion* — the strategic creation of a large number of proxy civil society organizations through which a central network executive (the RSS) covertly organizes sophisticated divisions of labor. The ties between organizations, not the organizations themselves, are primarily concealed.

The network structure shows:
- 53% of Sangh organizations are tiny, single-location organizations (schools, blood banks, orphanages, cow shelters)
- 48.57% of organizations are service provision organizations
- 48% of all organizations are connected to the network through a single tie — entirely dependent on bridging nodes
- Average path distance from any node to the RSS: 2.092 (meaning virtually every organization is only two steps from the center)
- 74% of ties are informal (undeclared)
- 44% of organizations are legally unregistered

This network structure is designed to produce three effects:

*1. Segmented representation:* Multiple proxy organizations can simultaneously speak to mutually exclusive audiences — Dalit outreach groups, Muslim outreach organizations, environmental activists, textbook revision committees, disaster relief organizations, temple committees, academic societies, industrial collectives — each with tailored messaging that may contradict the others, without undermining the network's underlying material integrity. Far-right mobilizations can tell "all things, at all times, to all people," vastly expanding their potential support base. The central discipline is minimal: "Everything can be negotiated except the hindu rashtra."

*2. Reputational control (the switchboard mechanism):* Mediated network ties — in which peripheral organizations are connected to the center through bridging nodes rather than directly — allow the RSS to strategically acknowledge or deny organizational relationships. When the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's activities become publicly controversial, the RSS can "turn off" its acknowledged connection to the VHP, while the material and financial relationship remains intact. The RSS can claim credit for the charitable activities of Sewa Bharati while distancing itself from the violence of Bajrang Dal militias — because the path from those militias runs through the VHP, which can be switched off. The 74% informal tie rate is what makes this possible: informal ties are inherently deniable.

*3. Leadership accommodation:* The proliferation of organizational positions within a large network provides a supply of titles, fiefdoms, and patronage networks to distribute to potential internal challengers. When Eknath Ranade posed a leadership challenge to Balasaheb Deoras after Golwalkar's death in 1973, the solution was to give Ranade his own semiautonomous network — the Vivekananda Kendra — with over twenty subsidiaries and nationwide reach. The challenge was neutralized without organizational schism. The result: the Sangh has existed for over a century without a major organizational split.

**Why this matters beyond India:**

Pal briefly notes that analogous strategies appear in Nippon Kaigi (Japan), the Thai military's civil society networks, and Ma Ba Tha (Myanmar). The mechanisms of organizational diffusion are not India-specific; they are visible wherever the far right operates primarily through civil society rather than party contestation.

The methodological implication is significant: ideological commitment, which most far-right research uses as its primary analytical lens, is *unevenly distributed* through organizationally diffused networks and weakens at the periphery. Mapping the far right through its organizational ties rather than its ideological commitments will always reveal a larger network than mapping it through ideology alone.

### Discussion Activity: Two Models of Normalization (15 min)

**Prompt for small groups (3–4 students), 8 minutes:**

The two papers in today's reading offer different accounts of how normalization happens: through electoral legitimization events (Bischof & Wagner) and through covert civil society expansion (Pal). Both accounts are about normalization — but they imply very different intervention points.

What civil society organizations make up the American far-right network? Who are the equivalent of the RSS's pracharaks?

*Group B — Western Europe:* The B&W model requires a first-time entry event to generate its effects. Does this imply that polarization and normalization plateau after entry? *Group C — Counter-strategies:* If Bischof & Wagner are right, de-normalizing the radical right through electoral defeat may simply re-suppress preferences without eliminating the backlash polarization among the left. ### Methods Notes — Session 5B

**Bischof & Wagner:**

*Within-person panel design (Study 1):* The key methodological virtue is that respondents are compared to themselves before and after the election. This rules out time-invariant individual-level confounders (personality, education, demographic characteristics). The placebo test — comparing respondents interviewed before and after Fortuyn's assassination (but before the election) — is strong: if the assassination were driving polarization, it would show up here; it doesn't. The main threat is that other aspects of the 2002 campaign (a tumultuous one dominated by the LPF) also drove polarization, but this is hard to disentangle from the party's electoral success itself.

*Two-way fixed effects (Study 2):* Country and decade fixed effects absorb time-invariant country characteristics and common time trends. The comparison is between treated countries (those that experienced radical-right entry) and untreated countries in the same decade. The effect is robust across multiple model specifications. Main caveat: the selection of when a radical-right party enters parliament is not random — it occurs when conditions are favorable to radical-right success, which could be confounded with underlying polarization trends. The synthetic control method in Study 3 addresses this more directly.

**Pal:**

*Archival social network analysis:* Maps interorganizational ties using publicly accessible online sources — internal organizational documents, media reports, financial records, social media pages, far-right publications. The multiplexity matrix (34 types of evidence, three-tier weighting) is a novel methodological contribution, designed to handle the covert nature of the network.

Main limitations: skew toward organizations with online presence (rural and remote organizations underrepresented); the boundary problem (the network could theoretically extend to almost all of Indian civil society); commensurability problem (the BJP and a cow shelter are both "nodes"). Pal is candid about all three. The core defense is theoretical exhaustion — no new types of organizational patterns were being discovered by the time data collection stopped — and the fact that the dataset already exceeds all prior estimates of Sangh network size.

### Anticipated Student Moves — Session 5B

**"Isn't the backlash just preexisting partisanship — people already wanted to move left?"**
The panel design rules this out for time-invariant individual characteristics: the same respondents were measured before and after. The change is what matters, not the level. More importantly, the effect shows up for relatively centrist parties like CDA and D66, not just for the SP and GL — voters who might have stayed near the center moved away from it after the LPF entry. The backlash is not just partisan reinforcement; it's ideological departure from the center driven by the event.

**"Does this mean de-platforming the AfD would repolarize Germany in reverse?"**
A genuinely hard question — Valentim (5A) suggests banning the AfD would convert open preferences back to covert without eliminating them. B&W suggest the legitimization and backlash effects are produced by electoral success, not just existence. If the AfD were banned and lost parliamentary representation, the B&W model predicts at minimum a reduction in the ongoing polarization pressure. But the preferences remain; the backlash identity among left-wing voters may also persist. This is exactly the unresolved question in the DW clip.

**"India isn't far right in the same sense as Germany or France."**
Pal's definition is explicit: nativism (an exclusive ethnic or religious conception of the nation), authoritarianism, and antidemocratic sentiment. The Sangh's commitment to a Hindu nation-state that subordinates Muslims and Christians meets this definition. Students may resist labeling the BJP — the governing party of the world's largest democracy — as "far right." Worth spending time on this, because Pal is doing something methodologically important: evaluating the global far right by the mechanism (organizational diffusion) rather than by the ideological brand.

**"Why hasn't India's civil society opposition stopped the Sangh?"**
This gets at the leadership accommodation and reputational control mechanisms. The Sangh's network makes it very hard to target: violence is attributable to the periphery (Bajrang Dal), not the center (RSS); service provision is widely valued; educational organizations are seemingly innocuous. Opposition to the Sangh requires simultaneously attacking a religious charity that runs blood banks and schools, which is politically costly. The network is structured to make that cost prohibitive.

**"How does Pal's framework relate to Gramscian hegemony (from Gomes-Franco in Week 4)?"**
Directly — Pal uses Gramscian hegemony explicitly, framing normalization as "mechanistic stage in civil society hegemony building." The Sangh's strategy is a concrete instantiation of Gramsci's "war of position" — capturing civil society institutions before capturing state power. The Week 4B Gomes-Franco concept (interregnum, radical-right migration to alt-tech) was the demand-side equivalent; Pal is showing the supply-side organizational equivalent.

This illustrates how organizational diffusion can segment far-right messaging by gender as well as class and community — with women's organizations simultaneously normalizing Hindu nationalist claims and reproducing gender hierarchies.

- **Week 8 (Electoral Politics):** Bischof & Wagner's finding that radical-right party entry polarizes the *entire* electorate — not just radical-right voters — has implications for how mainstream parties respond. If center-left parties' voters are themselves becoming more extreme in response to radical-right normalization, the electoral calculus for those parties shifts toward their activated base rather than toward the median voter. This may partly explain why mainstream-left parties have not reliably "tacked center" in response to radical-right competition.

### Instructor Notes — Session 5B

**Wagner, Schafer & Yavuz (2025) — "Opposition to Government and Back" *Politics & Governance***

Wagner et al. provide a party-level complement to Bischof & Wagner's voter-level findings. Where Bischof & Wagner document that radical-right electoral entry polarizes the electorate, Wagner et al. ask whether parties themselves shift their positions in response to the far right — and if so, in which direction and by how much.

Core findings:

1. *Conservative mainstream parties follow far-right immigration discourse.* Using word embeddings and immigration-related dictionaries across 67 parties in 8 countries, 1996–2024, Wagner et al. find that when far-right parties shift toward more restrictive immigration language, mainstream right parties follow. The effect is asymmetric: mainstream left parties shift less reliably.

2. *Far-right parties minimally moderate on entering government.* This directly speaks to the "taming" hypothesis (see also Bichay in Week 6B): entry into governing coalitions produces some softening of far-right immigration rhetoric, but the moderation is small and often temporary. Far-right parties entering government do not substantively converge toward the mainstream.

3. *Far-right parties radicalize on returning to opposition.* After governing stints end and parties return to opposition, their immigration discourse becomes more restrictive than it was before they entered government. The governing experience does not produce sustained moderation; it produces a temporary plateau followed by radicalization.

4. *Net effect: illiberalization of the party system.* The combined dynamics — mainstream right following far-right discourse shifts, far-right minimally moderating in government, far-right radicalizing in opposition — produce a ratchet effect. Each cycle moves the party system's immigration discourse in a more restrictive direction.

*Connection to Valentim (5A):* Valentim's normalization argument operates at the level of voters — declining social costs allow covert preferences to surface. Wagner et al. operate at the level of parties — mainstream parties revise their positions in response to the electoral signal the far right reveals. These are supply-side and demand-side mechanisms that reinforce each other: as voters surface covert preferences (Valentim), mainstream parties update their supply of positions (Wagner et al.), which in turn reduces social costs further by signaling elite legitimacy.

*Connection to Bischof & Wagner (5B):* Bischof & Wagner find that radical-right party entry into parliament polarizes voters, including moving center-left voters leftward. Wagner et al. find that the same entry moves mainstream right parties rightward in immigration discourse. The combination means that radical-right entry produces divergent movement at both the mass and elite levels — voters and parties simultaneously move away from a common center, which is itself a form of normalization. The immigration debate becomes more polarized and the far right's framing more dominant even before the far right wins an election.

*Methods note:* Word embeddings + dictionary-based scoring on party manifestos and press releases, 67 parties, 8 countries, 1996–2024. The combination of methods is worth noting: word embeddings capture semantic proximity (which words appear in similar contexts to "immigration") while dictionaries provide interpretable scoring (restrictive vs. permissive framing). Used together, they can detect discourse shifts that simple keyword counting misses — for instance, parties adopting far-right *framing* around immigration without literally using far-right *vocabulary*.

*Possible use in session:* If the 5B discussion of Bischof & Wagner generates a student question about whether parties (rather than voters) also respond to radical-right entry, Wagner et al. provides the answer. The finding that mainstream right parties follow far-right immigration discourse is particularly relevant to a discussion of why anti-immigrant policy has become mainstream across the European center-right, independent of formal coalition arrangements with the far right.
