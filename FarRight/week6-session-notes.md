# Week 6 Session Notes: Violence and Governing

**Readings:**
- Part 1 (Session 6A): Koopmans (1996); Piatkowska & Stults (2021); Aarons (2022)
- Part 2 (Session 6B): Bichay (2024); Chueri (2022)

**Syllabus framing question:** When does the far right turn violent — and why does political context seem to matter more than hatred?

---

## Session 6A: Causes and Forms of Far-Right Violence

**Focus:** This session asks why far-right violence occurs. It uses three papers that operate at different levels of analysis and come to conclusions that are, in some respects, genuinely in tension with each other.

Koopmans and Piatkowska explain *reactive* hate violence — violence that responds to changes in political discourse, elite behavior, and triggering events. Their shared answer is that political opportunity structures and legitimization effects matter more than the underlying stock of grievances. Aarons explains a categorically different mode — *proactive*, mythologically-driven accelerationist violence that does not respond to political opportunity but generates its own self-authorizing logic from within an ideological apparatus. The session's central intellectual move is to establish what these frameworks share, where they differ, and whether they can be integrated.

---

### Opening Hook (8 min): The Violence Paradox

Start class with two facts about 1988–1993 Western Europe:

1. France has the Front National winning 12–15% of the national vote. Denmark has the Progress Party pulling comparable numbers. These are among the strongest radical-right parties in Europe.

2. Germany and Great Britain have essentially no viable extreme-right party at the national level.

Ask students: *Which set of countries do you expect had more racist and extreme-right violence during this period?*

Almost certainly most students will say France and Denmark — strong far-right parties presumably means more extremism, more violence.

Koopmans' data say the opposite. Germany had by far the highest *absolute* number of extreme-right violent offenses, and was among the highest on per-capita measures. Switzerland and Great Britain were roughly comparable. France, Denmark, and Norway had *very low* levels of violence. The countries with the strongest extreme-right parties had the least extreme-right violence.

Put this on the board as the day's central puzzle:

> **Strong far-right parties → less violence. Weak far-right parties → more violence. Why?**

This counterintuitive finding is not obvious, not ideologically convenient for anyone, and drives the entire structure of the Koopmans paper. It also matters enormously for policy: if we're worried about radical-right violence, should we be trying to suppress radical-right electoral politics, or does suppression backfire?

### Mini-Lecture 1: Koopmans — Grievances vs. Opportunities (18 min)

**Setup and scope.**

Koopmans (1996) is a social movement scholar applying the field's two dominant explanatory frameworks to racist and extreme-right violence in eight Western European countries, 1988–1993. The paper is old by the standards of contemporary quantitative work, and students may notice the data quality issues Koopmans himself flags at length. What makes it enduring is the clarity of its theoretical argument and the strikingness of its empirical counterintuition.

The paper tests two models against cross-national data:

**The Grievance Model:** Violence occurs where grievances are worst. Large foreign populations, high asylum-seeker influx, economic deprivation, and xenophobic attitudes among the population — these create objective and subjective conditions that find an outlet in racist violence. This is the dominant explanation in political discourse (it's what politicians invoke to explain or excuse violence), and it is roughly what students will assume.

**The Opportunity Model:** Violence occurs where political elites have opened a favorable opportunity structure for extreme-right mobilization. Grievances are necessary but not sufficient — they become politically active only when elites *frame* the immigrant/refugee presence as an urgent social problem. The key variables are elite behavior, discursive context, and whether other channels (particularly electoral parties) exist to absorb the mobilization potential.

**The grievance model fails.**

Walk through the key falsifying cases:

*Foreign population size:* Switzerland (18.4% foreign nationals), Germany (8%), France (6.3%), Netherlands (5%), UK (4.3%), Sweden (5.1%), Denmark (3.1%). Now compare to violence: Switzerland and Germany high; France low; Denmark very low; UK high. The correlation simply doesn't hold. France has a large foreign population and almost no violence. Denmark has a tiny foreign population and almost no violence. UK has a small foreign population and high violence.

*Asylum seeker influx (1988-1993 per capita):* Sweden highest, then Switzerland, then Germany. But Sweden has modest violence; Switzerland and Germany have high violence — so this correlation is partial at best.

*The East Germany anomaly:* At the start of the wave of violence in 1991, the eastern German states had less than 1% foreign residents — a foreign population three times *smaller* than the western states on a relative basis. Yet the wave of violence (Hoyerswerda, Rostock) started in the east. The state of Saxony, site of the first pogrom, had *fewer* foreigners at end of 1991 than at the beginning. The grievance model cannot explain this.

*Attitudes:* Eurobarometer (1992) data on xenophobic attitudes: France scores roughly the same as West Germany on most measures. Denmark is *more* xenophobic than West Germany on some. East Germany is *less* xenophobic than West Germany — yet has far more violence.

Koopmans' summary verdict: "Almost any combination of violence levels, objective conditions, and xenophobic attitudes can empirically be found." The grievance model fails to identify a necessary or sufficient condition for violence.

**The opportunity model and the German case.**

This is where Koopmans makes his most important substantive contribution. The theoretical core:

1. Objective conditions (immigrants, refugees) only come to be perceived as a social problem if political *elites frame them as such*. The framing is not automatic — it is a political choice.
2. Once elites frame the issue as urgent and contested, extreme-right groups exit their political ghetto: they have high media resonance, favorable chances of substantive success, and a general public predisposed to treat their concerns as legitimate.
3. Whether this mobilization potential turns violent depends on whether *alternative channels* exist to express the grievance — above all, whether there is a viable extreme-right party.

The key illustration is the contrast between the *Aussiedler* (ethnic German repatriates from Eastern Europe, whose immigration peaked in 1989-90 at over 300,000/year) and *asylum seekers* (peaked 1992 at similar numbers). Both waves were roughly equal in scale. The Aussiedler question was dealt with quietly, without drama, through an administrative procedural change in July 1990. Nobody invented the concept of the "phony German" or "economic Aussiedler," even though by the same logic one could have. The asylum issue was the opposite: the CDU/CSU in summer 1991 called for restricting the constitutional right to asylum; the SPD attacked them for it; there was an 18-month public crisis in which the asylum/foreigners issue displaced unemployment as the number-one concern in public opinion polls. And extreme-right violence followed the debate, not the refugee numbers.

Koopmans tests this with Granger causality-style time series regression:

- Asylum debate → extreme-right violence: significant (β = 0.33)
- Extreme-right violence → asylum debate: not significant
- Refugee numbers → extreme-right violence: significant but weaker (β = 0.24)

The debate *caused* the violence more than the refugees caused the violence. The process was not spontaneous. It was politically constructed.

**Parties as strategic alternatives — the counterintuitive finding explained.**

The second mechanism: violence and electoral mobilization are *strategic alternatives* in the repertoire of extreme-right movements. Violence is costly — it risks repression and moral sanction. Where a party offers a lower-cost alternative that also has a real chance of influencing policy, activists and sympathizers shift to electoral mobilization. Where no such party exists, violence becomes a more attractive option.

This generates the paradox from the opening hook. France and Denmark had strong extreme-right parties throughout this period — the FN and Progress Party respectively channeled radical-right mobilization potential into the electoral system. Germany and the UK had weak parties, so the mobilization potential had nowhere to go except the streets and the arson attack.

Within-country comparisons confirm this: among German *Länder*, eastern states had weak extreme-right parties AND high violence; Bavaria and Bremen had the strongest extreme-right electoral results AND the lowest violence in the West.

Koopmans' provocation at the end: "the explanation for the fact that countries like France and Denmark have been spared from the wave of violence against foreigners is not that extreme right and racist attitudes find less support among the general public…but, on the contrary, that in these countries the extreme right and the xenophobic attitudes it stands for had already become part of 'politics-as-usual' in the 1980s."

*Pedagogical note on the normative sting:* Students may find this unsettling. Koopmans is not saying this is good. His last sentence makes the point explicit: "The question, of course, is whether ethnic minorities in Western Europe are in the end better off in such a situation of institutionalized racism." The finding creates a genuine dilemma: suppressing electoral far-right politics may *increase* violence while normalized electoral far-right politics reduces violence but normalizes the underlying prejudice. Hold this for the discussion.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Piatkowska & Stults — Brexit and Temporal Clustering (15 min)

**The research question.**

Piatkowska & Stults (2021) extend the Koopmans opportunity-structure argument into the contemporary UK setting. Their core question: do different types of galvanizing events — terrorist attacks vs. a major political referendum — produce different patterns of hate crime? And if so, what does the difference tell us about the mechanisms?

**Data and method.**

UK Home Office daily counts of racially or religiously aggravated offenses, April 2016–August 2017. Monthly counts from January 2013. Method: ARIMA interrupted time-series analysis with 15-day lags (daily) and 2-month lags (monthly) following each triggering event. This design controls for autocorrelation, seasonality, and trends, isolating the effect of specific events.

Events examined: Brexit campaign beginning (April 2016), Brexit vote (June 23, 2016), Westminster Bridge attack (March 2017), Manchester Arena bombing (May 2017), London Bridge attack (June 2017), Finsbury Park Mosque attack (June 2017). Plus three nondomestic events: Gaza conflict (2014), Charlie Hebdo (January 2015), Paris attacks (November 2015).

**Main results.**

*Brexit vs. terrorist attacks:* This is the paper's central finding. Brexit produces a spike that is stronger and longer in duration than any terrorist attack:

- Terrorist attacks: peak 3–4 days after event; return to pre-event levels within 6 days
- Brexit vote: peak 7–8 days after vote; significant positive coefficients persist through 15 days

The Brexit vote's effect on hate crimes is contrary to what Blalock's intergroup conflict theory would predict. Under Blalock's threat model, hate crimes are driven by the majority group's *fear of displacement*. After the Brexit vote, the dominant group *won* — immigration restriction was secured. Threat should have diminished. Hate crimes should have stabilized or declined. They didn't. They went up.

The mechanism Piatkowska & Stults invoke is Perry's (2001) concept of the **enabling political environment** — or what they call "permission to hate." The Brexit vote did not reduce threat; it validated the anti-immigrant worldview. It gave perpetrators a sense of legitimacy and impunity. The "license to hate" effect is not about reactive fear; it's about the political validation of prejudice.

*Severity matters for attacks:* Manchester Arena (22 killed, 250 injured) produced a larger and longer spike than Westminster Bridge (5 killed, 50 injured). The hate-crime response mirrors the severity of the triggering event, consistent with Black's (1983) theory of vicarious retribution — the size of the "crime as social control" response scales with the magnitude of the perceived transgression.

*Domestic vs. nondomestic events:* Domestic terrorist attacks have clearly significant effects; nondomestic attacks have minimal or no effect with one exception: the Charlie Hebdo shooting had a small but significant effect, likely because of its salience around free-speech debates in Western European contexts. Gaza conflict: no effect. Paris attacks: essentially no effect.

*Brexit campaign beginning:* Paradoxically showed negative or null effects on hate crimes during the campaign itself. Only after the *result* — the political validation — did hate crimes spike. This is further evidence against a simple "inflammatory rhetoric" model; the mechanism requires institutional legitimization, not just discourse.

**Connection to Koopmans.**

Piatkowska & Stults explicitly invoke Koopmans (1996): "This aligns with past research on the political opportunity model (Koopmans 1996), which suggests that political opportunity rather than the grievance itself helps to explain hate-motivated violence." Brexit functions as a real-world test of Koopmans' core argument in a different country and a different era: it's not the *stock* of anti-immigrant grievance (which was high throughout the UK in this period) but the *political construction and validation* of that grievance that triggers violence.

The finding about parties and Brexit is worth connecting back to the counterintuitive Koopmans result: UKIP's success in the Brexit campaign channeled anti-immigration sentiment into electoral politics — but then the *victory* of that electoral mobilization produced a legitimization effect that increased hate crime. This suggests that Koopmans' "strategic alternatives" mechanism is more complex than a simple tradeoff: electoral success can, in the right circumstances, produce a legitimization dividend that *increases* violence rather than substituting for it.

### Mini-Lecture 3: Aarons — Accelerationist Violence as Mythological Category (15 min)

**Framing: a different mode of violence.**

Everything Koopmans and Piatkowska have said about far-right violence presupposes a reactive mode: violence responds to changes in political opportunity structure, elite discourse, triggering events. The mechanism is essentially external — political context shapes the cost-benefit calculation for potential perpetrators, or provides legitimizing signals that remove inhibitions.

Aarons is asking about a categorically different kind of far-right violence — one that is not reactive but *proactive*, not triggered by external events but generated internally by an ideological apparatus, not calibrated by political opportunity but by mythological necessity. The Christchurch shooter didn't act because a politician gave him permission. The Pittsburgh Tree of Life shooter didn't respond to a triggering event in the Piatkowska sense. These actors operated from within a self-authorizing symbolic system that has been constructing itself since 1975.

**The Jesian framework: wordless ideas and the mythological machine.**

Aarons draws on the Italian cultural theorist Furio Jesi to analyze how far-right violence is organized around what Jesi called "wordless ideas" — not coherent ideological propositions but capitalized symbols evoking an esoteric truth that *must remain inexpressible*. The symbol gestures toward a Myth that cannot be fully stated; stating it would demystify it and destroy its mobilizing power. This is Spengler's *Blut und Boden*, or the Turner Diaries' vision of a purified white homeland — not an ideology you can debate, but an image you can only inhabit or die for.

Jesi's concept of the **mythological machine**: an apparatus that produces images of Myth and of the Mythic Man (the hero, the martyr) while *withholding* access to the Myth itself. The machine promises consummation — union with the Myth — that is always deferred except in one case: the founding sacrifice. Death in the service of the Myth is the only act that genuinely *confirms* the Myth without betraying it in language. This produces the logic of martyrdom that Aarons calls *religio mortis* — a totalizing funerary mythology in which dying is the supreme political act.

**Three phases of accelerationist genealogy.**

*Phase 1 (1975–1985): The Turner Diaries and the Order.*

William Luther Pierce's *The Turner Diaries* (serialized 1975–78) is the founding text of American accelerationist violence. Its genius, Aarons argues, is that it functions as a **generic myth machine**: it is operationally specific (it describes tactics — how to bomb the FBI building, how to run guerrilla operations) but doctrinally empty (it doesn't explain *why* the world must be purified; it only presents images of the purification). The empty doctrinal center is what makes it applicable across contexts — you can plug any grievance or symbol into its operational frame.

Robert Mathews and his group (the Order) enacted the first phase of the Diaries' operational program in 1983-84: robberies, assassinations, counterfeiting. On December 8, 1984, surrounded by the FBI at Whidbey Island, Mathews died in a fire rather than surrender. Aarons reads this as the deliberate consummation of the sacrificial mythologeme — the first real instance of *religio mortis* in the American accelerationist tradition. David Lane, who observed this, coined the Fourteen Words. December 8 became Martyr's Day in neo-Nazi calendars. The mythological machine now had its foundational sacrifice.

*Phase 2 (1985–1995): Leaderless resistance and the lone-wolf model.*

FBI repression in the mid-1980s destroyed the Order and the organizational infrastructure around Pierce. Louis Beam's "Leaderless Resistance" memo (1992, but circulating from the late 1980s) articulated a strategic response: abandon hierarchical organization; cells of 1-2 individuals; no traceable command structure. Pierce responded on the narrative level with *Hunter* (1989) — a novel about a lone-wolf killer targeting interracial couples, inverting the Diaries' hierarchical organization with a solitary hero who acts without organizational sanction.

Oklahoma City (1995) sealed this phase. Timothy McVeigh carried pages of the Turner Diaries during the attack. Afterward, investigators found that McVeigh had read *Hunter*. He was not acting under organizational direction — he had absorbed the myth machine and acted from its internal logic. He confirmed the Hunter model and sealed it in political memory.

*Phase 3 (post-Oklahoma City to present): The acephalous swarm.*

Post-OKC, Pierce made a fateful decision: the myth machine should be decoupled from any organizational structure. The Turner Myth should propagate as autonomous cultural software, generating violence without requiring social infrastructure. This is what Aarons means by "acephalous virtual contagion" — the myth travels without a body (head), infecting individuals through media and eventually online spaces.

James Mason's *Siege* (a collection of neo-Nazi newsletters from the 1980s, republished and circulated in the 2000s and 2010s) completed the Phase 3 logic: a pedagogy of the *useless* task — actions that have no prospect of achieving any stated goal, that are nihilistically designed to end in the actor's death or capture. The point is not to win; the point is to perform the sacrificial act that confirms the Myth.

Today, the primary audience is not an organization but an **online swarm** — anonymous imageboard communities (8chan/8kun, Telegram channels) that consume and amplify violence as entertainment and ideology simultaneously. The Christchurch shooter livestreamed; Pittsburgh, Poway, and Buffalo shooters posted manifestos that cited each other and referenced the Turner Myth symbol-set without necessarily having read the Diaries directly. The machine now reproduces itself through citation of citations.

**Against Belew and Berardi.**

Aarons positions his argument against two prominent alternatives:

*Against Kathleen Belew (Bring the War Home, 2018):* Belew argues that post-Vietnam white power violence is best understood as a *coherent social movement*, unified by ideology and organizational continuity. Aarons thinks the Phase 3 evidence refutes this: there is no organizational continuity, no shared ideology in any propositional sense, no unified command. What there is is a mythological machine — a mechanism for producing violence-authorizing symbols that doesn't require any of the things a social movement requires.

*Against Franco "Bifo" Berardi:* Berardi argues that mass shootings are a form of nihilistic late-capitalist spasm — alienated individuals with no political content whatsoever, expressing only the death drive of a society that can't reproduce meaning. Aarons thinks this misses the *specificity* of the symbolic content. These are not arbitrary violence; they are organized around recognizable symbols (the Fourteen Words, the Turner myth imagery, specific tactical references) that connect to a tradition. The nihilism is itself a content — it is the doctrine that doctrine is useless, which is itself a doctrine that the mythological machine teaches.

Aarons' category: **mythic violence** — not coherent social movement, not meaningless spasm, but violence organized by and through a self-reproducing mythological apparatus that operates without organization, without leadership, and without fixed doctrine.

**Connection to Koopmans and Piatkowska.**

The most important connection: Koopmans argues that where electoral channels are available and used, violence is lower. His strategic alternatives hypothesis treats extreme-right actors as choosing between violence and electoral mobilization based on relative cost-benefit. Valentim's surfacing phase (Session 5A) creates the electoral channel — when it becomes socially acceptable to express radical-right preferences openly, parties can form and electoral mobilization can absorb the potential.

Aarons' accelerationist actors *reject* this logic entirely. For them, electoral politics is not just an alternative to violence — it is a betrayal of the Myth. Pierce explicitly theorized this: democratic politics is a corrupting system that co-opts and neutralizes genuine revolution. The mythological machine is designed to produce actors for whom Koopmans' strategic calculation is ideologically inadmissible. The surfacing phase, in this analysis, creates not just voters and skilled politicians but also, at the extreme tail of the distribution, a small cohort of actors who experience the normalization of the far right as a provocation rather than a release — as evidence that the system is absorbing what should be destroying it.

This means the Valentim and Koopmans frameworks together generate an important implication: normalization produces both the mass electoral mobilization *and* the small accelerationist fringe as joint products of the same process. Whether the violence that results is *net less* (because the electoral absorption effect dominates) or *net more* (because of legitimization effects) is an empirical question — and the answer likely varies by context.

### Discussion Activity (15 min): Applying the Frameworks

Divide into four groups. Each group gets a case. Groups should identify which framework best explains the case, and where the frameworks conflict or fail.

**Group 1 — Germany, 2015–2017:**
After the 2015 refugee crisis, there was a major spike in anti-refugee violence (arson attacks on refugee centers, assaults). Simultaneously, the AfD grew from 4.7% (2013) to 12.6% (2017). Koopmans would predict: as the AfD absorbed radical-right mobilization potential, violence should have declined. Did it? What does the Piatkowska framework add about the refugee crisis as a "triggering event"? Is there anything Aarons' framework explains that the others don't?

**Group 2 — Brexit UK, 2016–2019:**
Piatkowska shows a 15-day spike in hate crimes after the Brexit vote. But what happened in the subsequent years? If the enabling political environment persists (hardline government rhetoric on immigration, prominent anti-immigrant discourse), does Koopmans' prediction about "politics-as-usual" reducing violence hold? Or does a sustained legitimization environment produce sustained elevated violence?

**Group 3 — Pittsburgh / Poway / Buffalo (2018–2022):**
Apply Aarons' mythological machine framework. Each shooter posted a manifesto; each cited previous shooters and/or Turner Myth symbols; each framed their action in sacrificial rather than political terms. Can Koopmans' opportunity model explain why these attacks occurred *when* they did? Does Piatkowska's triggering-event logic apply? Or does Aarons' framework require a completely separate causal account?

**Group 4 — Charlottesville (2017):**
The Unite the Right rally involved both electoral-politics-oriented activists (some connected to the Trump campaign) and explicitly accelerationist figures (some carrying Siege iconography). James Fields' car attack: reactive (triggered by counter-protest events) or mythologically scripted? How do you distinguish empirically between a Koopmans "opportunity"-enabled reactive violent actor and an Aarons "mythic" proactive one?

After 10 minutes, bring groups back. Focus the debrief on: *Do these frameworks produce different policy predictions? If the mechanism differs, the intervention must differ too.*

### Closing Question — Bridge to 6B (4 min)

End with Koopmans' most uncomfortable sentence: "The question, of course, is whether ethnic minorities in Western Europe are in the end better off in such a situation of institutionalized racism."

His data show that where the far right is normalized as electoral politics, there is less street violence. But normalization means ethnic minorities live in a political environment where hostility toward them is routine, legitimate, and reflected in policy. Violence is not the only harm.

For next session: Koopmans' data show that normalized far-right politics reduces street violence by channeling radical-right mobilization into electoral competition. But normalization has an endpoint: when parties grow strong enough through electoral success, they become candidates for governing coalitions. What happens when the far right actually enters government — and why do mainstream parties let them in? That is the question Session 6B takes up.

### Methods Notes

**Koopmans:**
- Comparative-historical / cross-national. Eight countries, 1988-1993. Data quality issues are genuine and Koopmans is admirably candid about them — good teaching opportunity on how scholars handle data limitations while still drawing valid inferences.
- The time-series regression for Germany is Granger-style (testing causal direction between the asylum debate and violence). Standard errors controlled for first-order autocorrelation; variables log-transformed and differenced for stationarity.
- Students may push on: why should we trust cross-national comparisons when the underlying data definitions differ so much? Answer: Koopmans works across multiple indicators at different levels of inclusivity; consistent patterns across all of them give us confidence even if the precise numbers are unreliable.

**Piatkowska & Stults:**
- ARIMA interrupted time-series. Daily and monthly data. The paper is technically demanding; students with no time-series background should focus on the intuition: after-vs-before comparisons for each event, controlling for trends, seasonality, and autocorrelation.
- Key methodological choice: using 15-day lags rather than King & Sutton's (2013) 10-day lags, justified by the Brexit effect extending further than terrorist-attack effects. This is an empirically-driven specification choice — worth flagging as an example of responsiveness to data.
- The null effect for the Brexit campaign beginning is initially puzzling (and one model shows some sensitivity to lag specification) — Koopmans' framework provides the explanation: discourse alone doesn't create opportunity; institutional validation does.

**Aarons:**
- Genealogical/interpretive. Draws on Jesi's cultural theory alongside historical reconstruction of the American accelerationist tradition. Evidence is textual (close reading of the Turner Diaries, Hunter, Siege) and historical (primary sources on the Order, the Oklahoma City investigation, manifestos of Phase 3 shooters). This is not a quantitative causal claim but a genealogical argument about the transmission of a symbolic apparatus.
- Students trained in social-scientific methods may ask: how do you *test* the mythological machine hypothesis? How would we know if Aarons is wrong? Good epistemological discussion — Aarons' framework is more falsifiable than it might appear: if Phase 3 shooters' manifestos and symbolic content are random with respect to the Turner tradition, the thesis fails. The citations and imagery are, in fact, consistent and traceable.

### Anticipated Student Moves

**"If radical-right parties reduce violence, should we allow them to exist?"**
This is the normative flip side of the Koopmans finding and it's worth engaging directly. Koopmans himself doesn't endorse this conclusion — his last sentence registers precisely the problem. The empirical finding doesn't license a policy preference; it describes a tragic dilemma. Democratic societies may face a choice between two types of harm: physical violence against minorities (when parties are suppressed) vs. institutionalized discrimination (when parties are normalized). Neither is acceptable, which is part of why the question of democratic response to the far right is genuinely hard.

**"The Aarons framework is unfalsifiable."**
See the Methods Notes above. It's more testable than it looks. The specific claim is that Phase 3 shooters' symbolic vocabulary is non-random and traceable to the Turner Diaries tradition. This can be checked. What can't be tested in the standard sense is the Jesian framework itself — but that's true of most genealogical/interpretive frameworks, and it doesn't make the empirical genealogy wrong.

**"Why does the Brexit campaign show no effect but the Brexit vote does?"**
This is an excellent question and the answer is theoretically important. Rhetoric alone doesn't create permission to hate — political validation does. The campaign was contestable and contested; the result was a democratic verdict. Once the referendum voted to leave, the anti-immigrant position became not just one view among many but the expressed preference of the British democratic majority. That institutional legitimation — that *democratic verdict* in favor of the anti-immigrant worldview — is what converts discourse into enabling political environment.

**"These frameworks all seem to assume the perpetrators are rational."**
Koopmans explicitly assumes bounded rationality (strategic alternatives as rational choices). Piatkowska's retribution model (Black 1983) treats vicarious retribution as a quasi-rational response to perceived transgression. But Aarons is precisely about the limits of this assumption: his Phase 3 actors are not making strategic calculations. They are operating from within a mythological frame that is, by design, impervious to cost-benefit reasoning. This is why Aarons argues for a third analytical category distinct from social movement theory's rationalist assumptions.

## Session 6B: The Far Right in Government

**Focus:** Session 6A asked when and why far-right movements produce violence. This session takes the normalization logic one step further: the same political strength that, per Koopmans, reduces street violence by channeling mobilization into electoral politics eventually becomes strong enough to produce coalition entry. Two papers. Bichay asks why mainstream parties choose to include the far right in governing coalitions; Chueri asks what the far right actually does with governing power, specifically in welfare policy.

---

### Opening Hook (8 min): The Austrian Puzzle

January 2000. Wolfgang Schüssel's ÖVP forms a coalition government with Jörg Haider's FPÖ — giving the far right its first entry into Austrian government. The European Union responds with unprecedented diplomatic sanctions: fourteen EU member states recall their ambassadors. An EU member state's domestic electoral outcome has never before triggered a collective diplomatic response from partner states.

Here is the puzzle. Schüssel had another option. He could have formed a grand coalition with the Social Democrats (SPÖ) — the standard Austrian postwar formula. The SPÖ had more seats than the FPÖ. The EU would not have sanctioned a grand coalition. His own party had governed with the SPÖ for decades.

Ask students: *What does Schüssel get from governing with the FPÖ that he couldn't get from governing with the SPÖ?*

Take 3–4 answers before providing the Bichay frame. Students will likely suggest ideology (ÖVP and FPÖ are both right of center), electoral strategy (governing together might split the right-wing vote more favorably), or political opportunity (the FPÖ's surge made the status quo untenable). All of these are partially right — and Bichay's argument integrates them under a single logic.

The key claim students should arrive at, or be pushed toward: Schüssel may have calculated that governing *with* the FPÖ was better than governing *against* it. When your main electoral competitor is the FPÖ rather than the SPÖ, putting the FPÖ inside the governing coalition — making them responsible for the compromises of governing — converts them from a free-riding opposition critic into a governing partner who must defend difficult decisions and lose protest credibility. That is Bichay's co-optation logic.

### Mini-Lecture 1: Bichay — Why Mainstream Parties Co-opt the Far Right (15 min)

**The concept: co-optation from authoritarian politics.**

Bichay borrows the concept from the comparative politics literature on authoritarian regimes (Svolik 2012; Gandhi 2008). In authoritarian settings, leaders include potential coup-makers in the ruling coalition not because they share their agenda but to divide and pacify them: inclusion converts opponents into partners who share responsibility for difficult decisions. The logic: inclusion is cheaper than repression when the opponent is strong enough to be dangerous.

Applied to European democracies: when a far-right party is strong enough to be an electoral threat, mainstream right parties face a structural choice. Compete on immigration and cultural issues — requiring policy concessions and risking centrist voters. Or include the far-right in a coalition — converting them from opposition critics into governing partners who must take responsibility for the economy, accept coalition compromises, and lose protest credibility. This is the "taming" hypothesis.

**Three empirical tests.**

*Study 1 — Macro-level (GDP + immigration inflows):*
Multilevel probit on 255 coalition formation observations across 30 European countries, 1989–2018. Two significant predictors of far-right coalition inclusion: economic deterioration (lower GDP growth → higher probability of inclusion) and high immigration inflows (asylum seeker rate → higher probability). Poor economies raise far-right salience; high immigration gives the far-right its core issue credibility. Both make the threat serious enough to require co-optation.

*Study 2 — Survey-level (ESS immigration hostility):*
As public hostility to immigration rises within a country, the probability of far-right inclusion in government increases. The mainstream right is responsive to the public demand signal — not just to the organizational threat of the far-right party itself. This matters: co-optation is not simply a cynical elite maneuver but a response to genuine shifts in what voters are demanding.

*Study 3 — Spatial (neighboring far-right seat share):*
When neighboring countries have stronger far-right parties, the domestic probability of coalition inclusion increases. Far-right success is contagious: observing FPÖ or FN governing next door signals that the threat is serious and that co-optation is a legitimate response. This means far-right electoral success in one country creates inclusion pressure in others even absent equivalent domestic conditions.

**The taming finding — and its limits.**

Bichay finds some evidence that coalition inclusion reduces far-right criticism of the government, consistent with the taming hypothesis: once in government, they must defend policy rather than attack it. But the long-run electoral record is mixed. The FPÖ lost votes after its first governing stint (2000–2005) — and then recovered so thoroughly that it won the Austrian national election outright in 2024. The mainstream right's co-optation gambit may produce short-run taming at the cost of long-run legitimation.

**Connection to 6A.**

The Koopmans finding from Session 6A says that electoral strength reduces violence by providing a low-cost alternative to mobilization. Bichay shows the next stage: once parties reach that level of electoral strength, they become co-optation targets. The mechanism Koopmans credits with reducing violence also, over time, generates the conditions for governing inclusion. The same process that pacifies the street eventually populates the cabinet.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Chueri — Welfare Dualism in Government (15 min)

**What the far right actually does.**

Bichay explains how the far right gets into government. Chueri asks what it does once it's there. The received wisdom has been uncertain: some argue far-right parties moderate in government, trimming ideological excess under coalition accountability constraints; others argue they radicalize their mainstream partners. Chueri offers a more specific answer: far-right parties pursue a coherent welfare state vision — not moderate, not simply radical, but *dualistic*.

**The dualistic welfare paradigm.**

The central distinction is between "deserving" and "undeserving" groups. Far-right parties are simultaneously protectionist and neoliberal — but toward different populations.

*Protectionist paradigm — for "deserving" groups (native workers, long-term employees, pensioners, families with children):*
- Strong income replacement and pension protections
- Family benefits and tax bonuses
- "Selective universalism" — benefits that appear universal but in practice cover the national working-class core
- Active opposition to retrenchment of earned entitlements

*Neoliberal/activation paradigm — for "undeserving" groups (immigrants, long-term unemployed, those outside the labor-market norm):*
- Activation requirements and workfare
- Strict entitlement conditions, waiting periods
- Reduced benefit rates for recently arrived immigrants
- Commodification — welfare conditioned on market participation

The ideological work is in the classification, not the rules themselves. Eligibility criteria are mapped onto national/immigrant and insider/outsider distinctions that mirror far-right ideology, but they are presented as administrative rationality: "earned" vs. "unearned," "contributing" vs. "dependent," "committed" vs. "transient." The two paradigms are not contradictory — they are two faces of the same ethnic-identitarian welfare logic applied to different populations.

**Denmark case (DF as confidence-and-supply partner, 2001–2011).**

DF was not formally in government but held balance-of-power leverage. Key outcomes consistent with the dualistic logic:
- *Preserved* early retirement (efterløn) when the mainstream right wanted to reform it — protecting a major earned entitlement for DF's blue-collar constituency
- *Vetoed* pension cuts for existing recipients
- *Accepted* cuts to unemployment insurance duration and adult education subsidies (primarily benefiting labor-market insiders who already have jobs; less costly for the DF base)
- *Pushed through* the "start help" — a reduced benefit rate for immigrants in the first seven years of residence; subsequently, policies reducing immigrant benefits below regular minimum social assistance

The pattern is consistent: protection for Danish workers who have contributed to the system, restriction for immigrants and labor-market outsiders.

**Austria case (FPÖ in formal coalition, 2000–2005 and 2017–2019).**

FPÖ held ministerial posts. Outcomes:
- *Expanded* the minimum pension for long-term contributors
- *Introduced* relief packages for low-income workers
- *Created* the Familienbonus Plus — a significant tax benefit for employed parents
- *Protected* the 13th and 14th month salary bonuses
- *Imposed* immigrant waiting periods for family-reunification benefits and minimum income programs
- *Reduced* the minimum income for non-citizens
- *Cut* AMS (labor market training) access for long-term unemployed

Again: protectionist for FPÖ's Austrian-born worker, pensioner, and family constituency; neoliberal and restrictive for immigrants and labor-market outsiders.

**Methods: process-tracing across two cases.**

The comparative design tests whether the dualistic pattern holds across very different institutional settings — DF operating from outside coalition (minority support) vs. FPÖ operating from inside (formal coalition with ministerial posts); Denmark's more open economy vs. Austria's more domestically oriented one. The consistent pattern across these contrasts is what drives Chueri's confidence that the dualistic paradigm is a general feature of PRRP governing behavior, not a Denmark- or Austria-specific artifact.

The counterfactual burden: to establish far-right *influence*, Chueri must show what would have happened without DF or FPÖ. This is addressed through legislative history, elite interview evidence, and party document analysis — particularly instances where the mainstream coalition partner publicly preferred a different outcome before the far-right partner intervened.

### Discussion Activity (12 min): Coalition Negotiation Simulation

*Format note:* Sessions 5A, 5B, and 6A have all used four-group case discussion. This session introduces a different format — role-play simulation — that makes the strategic interaction at the heart of both readings concrete and student-generated.

Divide into three groups:

**Group 1 — Mainstream Right Party (ÖVP analog)**

You have won a parliamentary plurality. The far-right party finished second; the Social Democrats finished third. Your objectives: economic liberalization — labor market reform, pension reform, budget consolidation. Your constraint: you need either the far-right or the Social Democrats for a majority; the far-right is numerically closer and ideologically nearer. What do you offer to bring the far-right into coalition? What do you demand in return? What does Bichay's co-optation logic say your strategy should be?

**Group 2 — Far-Right Party (FPÖ analog)**

You have finished second. Both potential coalition partners need your votes. Your objectives: welfare protections for your core constituency (workers, pensioners, families), immigration restrictions in the welfare state. Your constraint: entering government exposes you to governing compromises that can cost you protest credibility and opposition voters. What do you demand from the coalition agreement? What are your red lines? What does Chueri predict you will actually achieve?

**Group 3 — Bond Markets and Financial Press**

You manage sovereign debt portfolios and cover the European economy. Your knowledge base: governments that include far-right coalition partners typically face higher borrowing costs than comparable center-right governments without far-right partners. This premium operates through two channels: policy uncertainty (welfare chauvinism and trade protectionism signal weaker fiscal discipline) and governance instability (far-right partners can collapse coalitions when blocked — PVV did exactly this in the Netherlands in 2012). What signals in the negotiation would reassure you? What signals would trigger spread widening?

*Debrief (4 min):* Can Groups 1 and 2 reach an agreement that Group 3 finds fiscally credible? What does the tension between Bichay's co-optation logic and Chueri's welfare dualism demands tell us about why governing with the far right is harder than getting into government with them?

### Methods Notes

**Bichay:**

*Multilevel probit with country-level random effects:* 255 coalition formation observations, 30 countries, 1989–2018. Random effects absorb time-invariant country characteristics; year fixed effects absorb common temporal trends. Three separate empirical models guard against overfitting to any single measurement strategy.

Key measurement decision: what counts as "coalition inclusion"? Bichay uses formal government participation — supply-and-confidence arrangements don't qualify, even when they confer significant policy leverage. This is a conservative and consequential choice: Chueri's Denmark case, the clearest evidence of far-right welfare policy influence, happens entirely outside the threshold Bichay uses. Students should notice this measurement tension directly — it is not a flaw so much as a genuine definitional issue about when the far right is "in government" that the two papers resolve differently.

**Chueri:**

*Process-tracing in two cases:* Process-tracing reconstructs the causal pathway from proposed cause (far-right party presence) to proposed outcome (welfare dualism) through documented intermediate steps — legislative history, elite interviews, party manifestos, ministry records, press coverage. The strength: it can show *how* the outcome was produced, not just whether it correlates with proposed causes. The limitation: it cannot easily establish how general the finding is beyond the chosen cases.

Case selection: Denmark and Austria were chosen for maximum variation on the institutional dimension (supply-and-confidence vs. formal coalition) while holding the key independent variable (far-right governing involvement) roughly constant. If the dualistic pattern appears under both configurations, it is probably not a feature of any single institutional form.

The counterfactual burden is real. Establishing that DF caused the preservation of efterløn requires showing that the mainstream right would have reformed it absent DF pressure. Chueri addresses this through documented preference divergence — instances where the Liberal-Conservative government publicly preferred a different outcome before DF intervened. Ask students: is this sufficient? What additional evidence would make the causal claim stronger?

### Anticipated Student Moves

**"Don't voters hold far-right parties accountable when they govern?"**

Bichay addresses this partially: FPÖ did lose votes after its first coalition stint (2000–2005), and DF lost votes after its decade of supply-and-confidence influence ended with a formal coalition attempt in 2015. This is consistent with the taming hypothesis working short-term. But both parties recovered and eventually exceeded their pre-governing heights. The accountability mechanism produces a temporary electoral cost that the party absorbs and recovers from — especially if the governing period is followed by return to opposition where they can again criticize mainstream parties. The co-optation gambit may be rational for one term but self-defeating across multiple cycles.

**"Is welfare chauvinism really different from mainstream welfare retrenchment?"**

Sharp question worth engaging directly. Chueri's finding is specifically about the *asymmetric* pattern: far-right parties simultaneously expand protectionist welfare for their core constituency while restricting welfare for outsiders. This is different from mainstream right retrenchment, which typically cuts across the board on fiscal grounds. The dualistic paradigm is distinctive because it distributes gains and losses according to an ethnic-identitarian logic — "earned" vs. "unearned" maps onto national/immigrant — rather than a fiscal or efficiency logic. Whether voters experience the distinction depends on which side of the dualism they're on.

**"Why would the mainstream right agree to the 'deserving' spending side of the dualism? Don't they want fiscal consolidation?"**

Yes — and this is where coalition negotiation gets costly for the mainstream right. The ÖVP wanted pension reform in 2017; the FPÖ vetoed it. Schüssel got FPÖ support for labor market liberalization; the FPÖ got welfare protections for Austrian workers and immigration restrictions. The mainstream right gets the pro-business agenda; the far right gets the protectionist-for-insiders agenda. The fiscal cost is real and shows up in bond market pricing — see the Johnston instructor note below.

**"These cases are from before 2020. Has anything changed?"**

Fair push on external validity. Since Chueri's data period, the Italian coalition led by Fratelli d'Italia (from 2022) and the Finnish coalition including the Finns Party (from 2023) provide early out-of-sample tests. Preliminary evidence from Italy suggests similar welfare dualism: significant expansion of family benefits for Italian citizens alongside highly restrictive immigration and asylum policies. The Italian case also tests whether the pattern holds when the far right *leads* the government rather than acting as junior partner. This is an active empirical frontier.

### Instructor Notes

**Johnston (2024) — "So Right It's Wrong?" *Comparative Political Studies***

Johnston provides the financial-market complement to Chueri's welfare findings. Where Chueri documents what the far right does in government, Johnston documents what markets think of it.

Core finding: right-of-center governments lose their typical bond spread advantage (relative to US Treasuries) by over 100 basis points when they govern with far-right partners. Two channels: the *policy channel* (welfare chauvinism and trade protectionism signal weaker fiscal discipline to investors) and the *governance channel* (far-right coalition partners create instability — they can collapse coalitions when they don't get what they want).

The Netherlands/Sweden comparison is the pedagogical centerpiece. Rutte's first cabinet (2010–2012) governed with PVV external support. PVV refused to support pension reform in April 2012, triggering cabinet collapse and early elections; Dutch bond spreads rose. Reinfeldt's second cabinet (2010–2014) governed without Sweden Democrat support through the same period; Swedish spreads remained stable. The natural experiment holds many factors constant (both center-right governments, same European economic environment) while varying far-right involvement.

Johnston's ECM panel analysis covers 23 OECD advanced market economies, 2000–2019. ECMs handle bond spread data well because spreads have both short-run news-driven components and long-run fiscal equilibrium components; the error-correction structure captures both.

*Use in class:* The coalition negotiation simulation's Group 3 (financial analysts) operationalizes Johnston's finding without requiring students to have read it. During the debrief, you can note: Johnston documents that the fiscal concerns Group 3 expressed are priced into sovereign debt at a premium exceeding 100 basis points. This creates a genuine puzzle for Bichay's rational co-optation logic — the mainstream right may be paying a substantial fiscal premium for what turns out to be a short-run electoral benefit.

---

**Bedock et al. (2022) — "A Policy Like No Other?" *Party Politics***

Bedock et al. address the far-right's electoral platform claim that it uniquely represents "the people" and would enhance direct democracy — citizen initiatives, referendums, citizen assemblies. The finding is a clean negative: far-right parties in government in Austria (FPÖ), Netherlands (PVV), France (RN), and Germany (AfD) show essentially no progress on direct democracy reform relative to their manifestos. The populist-democratic program does not translate into governing outcomes.

The companion finding is more counterintuitive and arguably more important: mainstream parties turn *away* from direct democracy when far-right parties embrace it. Issue ownership dynamics mean mainstream parties cede the issue rather than compete on it — leaving the far right with rhetorical ownership of direct democracy while no one actually implements it.

*Use in class:* This finding creates a useful analytical contrast with Chueri. Chueri shows the far right CAN translate its core ethnic-identitarian program (welfare dualism) into governing outcomes. Bedock shows the far right CANNOT translate its populist-democratic claims into outcomes. The asymmetry is instructive: the governing far right advances its ethnic-identitarian agenda but abandons its populist democracy agenda when the two conflict. For voters motivated primarily by anti-establishment sentiment, this is a betrayal. For voters motivated primarily by ethnic-identitarian concerns, it is a success. The governing record sorts out which motivation is actually doing the electoral work.

### Connections Forward

Week 7 examines democratic responses to the far right — counter-movements, institutional resilience, party system adaptation. The governing findings from this session provide the problem framing: the far right has achieved real welfare policy change through coalition entry (Chueri) but at the cost of market credibility (Johnston) and without delivering the populist democratic reform program most of its voters expected (Bedock).

The groups bearing the cost of welfare dualism — immigrants, long-term unemployed — are precisely those least well-represented by existing democratic institutions. Counter-movements and institutional champions for these groups are the problem Week 7B takes up.

The arc of the course to this point: Weeks 2–5 established what the far right believes and how those beliefs become expressible (ideology, networks, normalization). Week 6A examined what far-right movements do with violence; 6B examined what they do with governing power. Weeks 7–9 examine responses, resilience, and the long-term consequences for democratic systems.
