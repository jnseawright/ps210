# Week 7 Session Notes: Gender and the Far Right

**Readings:**
- Part 1 (Session 7A): Anduiza & Rico (2022); Blee (2012)
- Part 2 (Session 7B): Corredor (2019); Brady (2026)

**Syllabus framing question:** How does gender work in and around far-right politics — as a motive, as an ideology, as a structural dimension of movement life?

---

## Session 7A: Gender, Sexism, and the Far Right

**Focus:** This session examines gender from two directions simultaneously. Anduiza & Rico ask why individuals vote for far-right parties, finding that sexist attitudes — specifically modern sexism — are among the most powerful demand-side predictors of far-right electoral support. Blee asks how scholars have studied gender *within* far-right movements, and argues that a series of flawed conceptual templates have constrained what we can see. Together, the papers establish that gender is not a peripheral variable in far-right politics — it is structurally central — while disagreeing (productively) about the mechanism by which it operates.

A key pedagogical challenge here is that students may enter with one of two symmetrical misconceptions: that the far right is simply a male phenomenon (and women are incidental or merely compliant), or that gender attitudes are just one attitudinal factor among many. Both papers push hard against the margins of those assumptions.

---

### Opening Hook (8 min): Who Was in the Klan?

Before doing any reading introduction, put this on the board:

> **In the largest far-right movement in 20th-century US history, approximately what share of members were women?**
>
> A) Under 5%  
> B) 10–15%  
> C) 25–30%  
> D) Over 40%

Give students 30 seconds to commit to an answer, then reveal: at its mid-1920s peak, women accounted for at least 500,000 of the Ku Klux Klan's membership — roughly a third to a half of the eligible membership (since the full Klan was restricted to white, native-born Protestants). The women were organized into their own institutional structures with distinct hierarchies, tactics, and goals.

Most students will anchor on A or B. This is not ignorance — it reflects exactly the scholarly template Blee argues has constrained the field: the assumption that the far right is male.

Now extend this to the present:

> **What share of voters for far-right parties in Europe are women?**

Students typically assume the far-right vote is predominantly male. Correct — on average, there is a "gender gap" in far-right voting, with men overrepresented. But Anduiza & Rico's point is that this aggregate gap has distracted attention from the question of *why* any women (and men) vote for far-right parties — a question to which gender attitudes turn out to be central.

These two questions frame the session's two registers: gender inside movements (Blee), and gender attitudes as driver of far-right support (Anduiza & Rico).

### Mini-Lecture 1: Blee — Templates and the Invisible Women of the Far Right (18 min)

**The article's central claim.**

Blee (2012) is a methodological and historiographical intervention, not a conventional empirical paper. Its argument: scholarship on the US far right has been governed by a series of conceptual templates that have constrained what scholars can see, ask, and conclude about gender. The article does not primarily advance new empirical findings; it identifies the assumptions embedded in existing research and argues for a more productive set of questions going forward. Blee draws on her own two case studies — the 1920s KKK and contemporary organized racism — as illustrations.

Students may initially struggle with the article's form. It helps to frame this upfront: Blee is doing to the study of the far right what Koopmans did to the "grievance model" — systematically identifying a set of assumptions that seem obvious but turn out, on inspection, to be obstacles to understanding.

**Two earlier templates that made women invisible.**

*Template 1: The rightist as male.* Far-right politics was treated as an exclusively or overwhelmingly male domain — not because scholars checked this empirically, but because the dominant paradigms (totalitarianism theory, political religions theory, studies of authoritarianism) framed rightist politics around the exercise of power in public space, and assumed its actors were men. Women were "invisible not because [scholars] looked and didn't find them, but because the template precluded the question."

The 1920s KKK illustrates what this template cost us. Half a million women were in the Klan, with their own organizations, hierarchies, and tactics. The Women's Klan's most powerful weapon was not physical violence but "poison squads of whispering women" — systematic rumor campaigns that destroyed the livelihoods of Jewish merchants (rumors of spoiled meat), drove Black men from communities (rumors of sexual assault), and expelled Catholic teachers from public schools (rumors of Papal conspiracy). This was coordinated racial terror — but because it was private and non-physical, it was invisible through the lens that defined Klan politics as a masculine enterprise.

*Template 2: Nazi Germany as the prototype of far-right politics.* Early scholarship took Weimar/Nazi Germany as the defining case: extreme-right movements emerge from macro-structural crises (economic collapse, national defeat), grow explosively through mass mobilization, and are explained by psycho-social factors (authoritarianism, status anxiety, frustrated aggression). This template made the participation of women not just invisible but *inexplicable*. If far-right mobilization is explained by men's anxiety about eroding power in family, economy, and polity — men's *rational self-interest* in racial and economic hierarchy — then why would women join? The template couldn't process the question.

Both templates were disrupted by the accumulation of feminist scholarship through the 1980s–90s, but they were replaced by new templates that, Blee argues, create their own distortions.

**Three current templates and their problems.**

*Template 3: Historical continuity.* The move toward treating the far right as a "social movement" (rational, strategic, continuous) had productive effects — it opened questions about recruitment, organization, and ideology that psychological frameworks couldn't ask. But it also led scholars to treat historically discontinuous phenomena as instances of an underlying case. The KKK had four distinct waves of activity (Reconstruction, 1920s, 1960s, 1980s) with almost no overlap in membership, leaders, targets, or ideology. Women were in the 1920s Klan; excluded from the Reconstruction Klan; largely absent from the 1960s Klan; re-recruited in the 1980s, but for different reasons. Treating this as a single movement makes gender patterns look more coherent than they are, and obscures the ways in which gender issues are historically and contextually contingent.

*Template 4: Belief-driven activism.* The conventional assumption: people join racist movements because they hold racist beliefs. Joining is the behavioral expression of a pre-formed ideology. Blee's interview data suggest this is often not true. She presents two cases — Sean and Sandy — whose trajectories ran the reverse: action first, ideology second. Sean was recruited into skinhead violence before he understood or adopted white supremacist ideology; it was prison and the social world of racist groups that transformed his inchoate racial animus into an ideological system. Sandy joined skinhead street fights because she liked to fight and felt alive doing it; white power ideology came later, through immersion in the group's social world.

This inversion has specific gender implications. Women in contemporary organized racism are often recruited through ordinary social ties — conversations at playgrounds, environmental group meetings, grocery stores — before they hold strong ideological commitments. They are recruited as *people*, then learn to be racists. This means that the template of "she joined because she believed" misses the mechanism by which gender-differentiated recruitment works, and systematically underestimates the organizational sophistication with which racist groups have pursued women members.

*Template 5: Gender as a category of analysis.* Blee's most nuanced argument. The assumption that "gender always matters" has become almost axiomatic in feminist scholarship. But this template, applied to the far right, can produce its own distortions. Studies that look *for* gender find it — but in predictable places, emphasizing the hyper-masculine character of racist movements (guns, bravado, aggression) while missing the ways in which gender dynamics may be more complex, unstable, or context-dependent than assumed. The example: the "14 words" of white supremacy ("We must secure the existence of our people and a future for white children") is taught differently to men (individual racial heroism, culmination of white manhood) and women (defensive posture, suspension of normal gender rules — women are called to extraordinary action precisely because the racial emergency makes ordinary gender roles temporarily irrelevant). If we assume that gender always matters in a particular direction, we may miss the interesting fissures.

**Blee's four productive questions (brief).**

Blee ends by posing the agenda for future scholarship:
1. When is gender most salient, meaningful, and effectual in rightist movements — and when is it less so?
2. To whom does gender matter in the far right? (Not just members — external audiences, competing leaders, prospective recruits)
3. What does gender *do* — what work does it accomplish — in rightist movements?
4. What is the mechanism by which gender matters, or does not?

These are worth noting because they frame exactly the kind of questions Anduiza & Rico are trying to answer at the electoral level — even though the two papers operate at very different levels of analysis.

*Instructor note — objects, agents, and current research (2–3 min):* These questions are live in the contemporary literature, and this is a good moment to briefly illustrate the objects/agents tension with a concrete case from ongoing research.

Research I've been conducting with a colleague uses transcribed audio from several thousand InfoWars podcast episodes (2008–2024) and voice-based gender classification to track what women say on male-hosted alt-right media. One case from that dataset is worth describing in class:

On November 28, 2008, a mainstream broadcast journalist named Amanda Guerra appears on the Alex Jones Show — not as a guest, but involuntarily. Her local television reporting on children's deaths in the Texas foster care system is played as a clip without her knowledge or permission. Jones reframes her conventional journalism inside a conspiracy narrative about government child abduction. He could have used just the footage of the state administrator's callous quote ("Children will die. Accidents will happen. Period."). He includes Guerra specifically — because a woman's voice reporting on children's safety carries a kind of feminine authority that the administrator's clip alone does not.

This is the pure "gendered body" pole of Blee's spectrum: Guerra has no agency here at all. Her gender is being *used* by the male-run platform to legitimize an argument she has no part in making. Hand-coding of a random sample of 50 InfoWars episodes finds that roughly 20% of women's speech acts involve involuntary clips of this kind — the gendered bodies, not the agents.

The other end of the spectrum — strategic agents who use the movement's gender norms to build careers — is the subject of our closing question today.

*Pedagogical note:* Students who have encountered gender studies may bristle at Template 5's critique — it can seem like Blee is arguing that gender doesn't matter, which is not her claim. Her claim is more specific: assuming gender always matters in the same way, with similar salience across all contexts, can prevent us from noticing when and how it actually operates. This is a research design critique, not a substantive claim about whether gender matters.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Anduiza & Rico — Modern Sexism as a Predisposition (15 min)

**The conceptual move: from gender gap to gender attitude.**

Most research on gender and the far-right vote has examined the *gender gap* — the finding that men are somewhat more likely than women to vote for far-right parties. Anduiza & Rico argue this is the wrong dependent variable for understanding why people vote for the far right. The question should be: what *attitudes* drive far-right voting, and are gender-related attitudes among them?

The comparative literature has largely ignored gender attitudes in favor of the usual suspects: left-right ideology, nativism/anti-immigrant attitudes, EU skepticism, authoritarianism, populist attitudes. Anduiza & Rico call this a "blind spot" and argue it is partially a measurement problem — most survey batteries use thin measures of gender attitudes that don't capture what contemporary far-right discourse is actually mobilizing.

**Modern sexism: what it measures and why it fits.**

The conceptual core is the "modern sexism" construct (Swim et al. 1995), which Anduiza & Rico argue fits contemporary far-right discourse better than traditional measures. Modern sexism has three components:

1. *Denial of discrimination against women* — the belief that sex-based discrimination is not a real or significant problem in contemporary society
2. *Rejection/delegitimization of feminist complaints* — protests, mobilization, and outrage about discrimination are unjustified because there is no discrimination to correct
3. *Opposition to corrective measures* — policies and institutions designed to address gender inequality are unfair, discriminatory against men, or ideologically contaminated

This maps precisely onto what Vox (and other far-right parties) actually say. Vox denies that discrimination exists ("Spain is a country where men and women are equal before the law"), attacks feminists as "feminazis" and "totalitarian indoctrinators," and campaigns vigorously against the 2004 law against gender violence on the grounds that it "discriminates against men." Modern sexism is not measuring an abstract value; it is measuring an attitude that far-right parties are directly and explicitly mobilizing.

**The Spanish case and why it is a hard test.**

This matters because Spain is a *hard case* for the sexism hypothesis. Existing accounts of Vox's rise emphasize the Catalan territorial conflict and immigration attitudes. Antifeminism is considered a feature of Vox's discourse but not its electoral raison d'être. Individual-level explanations of Vox voting have not included gender. If sexism is significant in Spain, it is probably significant in easier cases too.

Spain also offers a unique natural experiment: between 2017 and 2020, the country experienced a massive feminist mobilization (2018 Women's Day marches, historical proportions, spurred by a high-profile gang rape case), *followed* by the emergence of Vox. The two events are temporally distinct, allowing the researchers to ask: did sexist attitudes predict Vox support *before* Vox became visible? Did *changes* in sexist attitudes matter?

**Main finding: sexism is the second most powerful predictor of Vox voting.**

Using the full four-wave Spanish Political Attitudes panel (2017–2020, N ≈ 1,600–2,000), Anduiza & Rico estimate logistic regression models of intended Vox vote. Modern sexism (measured as a full-battery composite) is significant and substantively large across all specifications:

- Cross-sectional (2019): Individuals at the 95th percentile of sexism are **8.6 percentage points** more likely to intend to vote for Vox than those at the 5th percentile, holding all else constant
- Cross-sectional (2020): **9.9 percentage points**
- The only variable with a larger coefficient is left-right ideological identification
- Nativism, populism, authoritarianism, income — all smaller effects than modern sexism

Lagged models (Table 2) confirm causal precedence: sexism *measured in a prior wave* predicts switching to Vox in subsequent waves. Those in the 95th percentile of sexism in 2018 were 5.7 pp more likely to switch to Vox in 2019 than those at the 5th percentile.

Placebo test: prior sexism does not predict switching to the People's Party (PP), the mainstream conservative party. The effect is specific to the far right — parties that display discourse reflecting modern sexism.

### Mini-Lecture 3: Anduiza & Rico — The Individual Dynamics of Backlash (12 min)

**The second contribution: sexism as a changing attitude.**

The standard assumption in the voting behavior literature is that attitudes are stable predispositions — people have them, and they drive behavior. Anduiza & Rico challenge this for modern sexism. Using their panel, they track *individual-level changes* in sexism across waves, and ask whether those changes also affect far-right voting.

First, the aggregate trajectory: average sexism in Spain *declined* from 2017 to 2018 (the feminist wave year) — from 0.36 to 0.34 on a 0–1 scale. But it then increased to 0.37 in June 2019 and 0.41 in 2020. These changes are statistically significant. More importantly, the aggregate masks enormous individual heterogeneity: many individuals moved in the opposite direction of the overall trend.

**Backlash vs. normalization attitudinal change.**

This is the paper's key theoretical contribution. Anduiza & Rico distinguish two types of attitude change:

*Backlash attitudinal change:* Increases in sexism occurring during a period of heightened feminist mobilization, when overall sexism is declining. The individual is swimming against the aggregate tide. If these increases predict far-right voting, it suggests that feminist momentum itself generates counter-mobilization at the individual level.

*Normalization attitudinal change:* Increases in sexism occurring during a period of far-right visibility (when Vox is in parliament, in the media, making sexist appeals). The individual is responding to cues from the far-right party itself. If these increases predict far-right voting, it suggests a feedback loop: Vox's rhetoric increases sexism → increased sexism increases Vox support.

**The asymmetric result.**

The key finding (Table 3, Models 1–4):

- Individuals who *increased* their sexism between 2017 and 2018 (backlash period, during feminist wave) were significantly more likely to switch to Vox in 2019 — the predicted probability of supporting Vox is 9.3% for those who increased by 0.20 points, compared to 4.8% for those who maintained their level
- Individuals who increased sexism between 2018 and 2019 (normalization period, during Vox's rise) did *not* significantly predict switching to Vox in 2020
- The effect is asymmetric: *decreases* in sexism did not significantly predict away from Vox (only increases matter)

The implication is politically striking: it was the feminist wave itself — the 2018 Women's Day mobilizations, the largest in Spanish history — that produced the backlash attitudinal change that fueled Vox's 2019 breakthrough. Not Vox's own rhetoric. The feminist movement's success in making gender inequality visible appears to have triggered counter-movement at the individual level among those already disposed toward denial.

*Note on the limits of observational evidence:* Anduiza & Rico are careful not to claim they have proven a causal chain. The timing is consistent with the backlash interpretation. But they cannot definitively rule out that some third factor (e.g., territorial attitudes, disillusionment with mainstream parties) drove both attitude change and vote switching. This is worth acknowledging for students who are methodologically attentive.

**Parallel to Koopmans and Piatkowska.**

The dynamic Anduiza & Rico document has structural resonance with two things we've already studied:

- *Koopmans:* Political framing by elites activates grievances that were previously latent. Here, feminist political action activates a latent opposition that was previously un-articulated — the mechanism runs in the opposite direction, but the structure (political activation of previously dormant potential) is similar.
- *Piatkowska:* The Brexit vote triggered a spike in hate crimes that peaked 7–8 days later, consistent with "permission to hate." Here, feminist mobilization may have produced a "permission to resist" — an enabling environment that makes it socially more acceptable to hold and express sexist attitudes in political opposition to the feminist movement.

### Discussion Activity (15 min): Cross-Level Mapping

**Format:** Structured two-part exercise — individual work (5 min) then pair/trio discussion (10 min). This is a different format from the case discussions (5A, 5B, 6A) and the jigsaw (6B), using structured analytical mapping to force students to specify the level of analysis at which each paper operates and to identify genuine points of contact.

**Part 1 — Individual work (5 min):**

Each student maps the two papers on a 2×2 grid (draw on the board):

|   | Voters/Supporters | Movement Members/Activists |
|---|---|---|
| **Gender attitudes drive behavior** | | |
| **Group/structural dynamics shape gender** | | |

Which cell does each paper occupy? Can either paper fit in multiple cells? If there's a cell that neither paper addresses, what would research in that cell look like?

**Part 2 — Pair/trio discussion (10 min):**

*Instructor note: There are four questions below. Choose 2–3 based on time and how well Part 1 generated insight. Q4 requires students to recall Valentim — briefly remind the class before discussion begins: "Valentim's argument was that radical-right voters already held those preferences privately; what changed wasn't their views but the social cost of expressing them. Normalization, in his model, means the gap between private preference and public expression closes."*

1. Blee critiques the "belief-driven activism" template — the idea that beliefs cause joining. Anduiza & Rico treat sexism as a predisposition that *causes* voting for the far right. Are these in tension? How would Blee evaluate Anduiza & Rico's causal model? How might Anduiza & Rico respond?

2. Anduiza & Rico find that feminist mobilization appears to have *generated* backlash sexism that fueled Vox's rise. If you were a feminist activist in Spain in 2019, what would you make of this finding? Does it counsel a different strategy, or does it suggest the feminist movement was facing a no-win situation?

3. Blee ends with four questions for future research (when, to whom, what for, by what mechanism). Which of her questions does Anduiza & Rico's paper make progress on — and which does it leave untouched?

4. Valentim argued that radical-right preferences were *already there* before Vox emerged — suppressed by social norms, not absent. Anduiza & Rico find that some people's sexist attitudes *actually increased* during the feminist wave, before Vox appeared. Are these claims complementary, in tension, or talking past each other? What evidence would you need to adjudicate between them?

**What to listen for in debrief:**

- Students may say Blee and Anduiza are simply about different things (activists vs. voters) — accept this but push: does the distinction fully dissolve the tension? Blee's critique of belief-driven activism would presumably apply to any simple "attitude causes behavior" model.
- The strategic dilemma in Q2 is genuinely hard. Don't resolve it. The relevant parallel is the Koopmans normative sting from Week 6A: does institutional legitimization of prejudice reduce violence while normalizing the underlying attitude?
- On Q3: Anduiza & Rico make real progress on "what mechanism" (backlash attitudinal change) and "when" (during feminist mobilization). They say little about "to whom" in Blee's sense (what audiences are being addressed by far-right gender discourse?) and nothing about supply-side "what does gender do" within movement structure.
- On Q4: See the expanded Valentim entry in Connections Forward below for a full map of the complementary/tension/orthogonal dimensions. The short version for debrief: Valentim and Anduiza & Rico agree on the *existence* of latent sexist predispositions, but disagree about whether those predispositions can change. For Valentim, the key variable is the social cost of expression; for Anduiza & Rico, the attitudes themselves move — at least for some people, under some conditions. The most interesting tension is directional: Valentim predicts that far-right party entry triggers norm shifts → attitude expression. Anduiza & Rico find that attitude change (backlash) preceded Vox's entry and caused it. They locate the initiating cause differently.

### Closing Question (6 min): Bridge to 7B

**Setup:** Show a 90-second clip from the PBS NewsHour piece on Laura Loomer (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Oow_xORyH9g). No framing needed before playing — let students watch cold. The two moments worth including in any edited clip:

1. The journalist's comment (~3:30–3:44): *"She really wanted to get the attention of Trump and his base by basically going one step further than anyone else in terms of making extreme claims. Making up conspiracies. And posting openly racist and Islamophobic content online simply in order to get clicks, likes, and follows."*

2. Loomer's own closing quote (~5:59–6:05): *"I am not working for President Trump. I'm not getting paid... And yet I feel like every single day it is a full-time job just to make sure the president is protected and he is receiving the information he needs to receive."*

**Closing prompt:**

> We've been talking about frameworks for understanding women's relationship to far-right politics. Using Blee and Anduiza & Rico: what's your best account of how Laura Loomer became who she is? What does each paper contribute to that account? What does each paper miss?

There is no clean answer. The goal is application under pressure — students will find that the frameworks partially fit and partially don't, which is the honest state of the literature.

**What to listen for:** Blee's "belief-driven activism" template is directly tested by the journalist's framing that Loomer escalated for strategic reasons (clicks, follows) before — or at least alongside — ideological conviction. That's not quite the Sean/Sandy pattern (social immersion → ideology) but it's not ideology-first either. Anduiza & Rico's framework is about voters, not activists, so students should notice it doesn't translate cleanly to Loomer's case. Her informal power (no job, not paid, not elected, but successfully influencing who gets hired and fired in a presidential administration) connects to Blee's point about women's far-right power operating outside formal institutions. This also connects to a pattern from the InfoWars research mentioned in ML1: systematic analysis of those podcasts finds women are about twice as likely as men to discuss parenting, children, and anti-trans themes — topics that are feminine-coded within the alt-right but also core ideological terrain. The data suggest a "leveraging constraints" dynamic: women use gendered topics to build credibility in a male-dominated space, then parlay that credential into broader influence. Loomer's career trajectory — InfoWars correspondent → congressional candidate → Trump insider influencing NSC personnel — fits this pattern closely.

### Methods Notes

**Anduiza & Rico:**

Panel data design is the paper's methodological core — it's what allows them to make progress on causality beyond cross-sectional correlation. Four waves (2017, 2018, 2019, 2020), online sample with demographic quotas, N ≈ 1,600–2,000 in vote models. Key methodological moves:

- *Cross-sectional vs. lagged specifications:* They estimate contemporaneous effects (Table 1 — sexism at time t predicts vote at time t) and lagged effects (Table 2 — sexism at t-1 predicts change in vote at t). The lagged specifications are more convincing because they establish temporal precedence.
- *Change-in-change specification (Table 3):* Most innovative. They estimate how *changes* in sexism between t-2 and t-1 predict vote at t, controlling for prior levels. This allows them to isolate the effect of backlash (2017–2018 change) vs. normalization (2018–2019 change).
- *Asymmetry test (Models 3–4):* They split the change measure into separate increase and decrease components, testing whether the effect is symmetric. It is not — increases matter, decreases don't.
- *Placebo test:* Replicating models on PP (mainstream conservative) vote confirms that the sexism effect is specific to the far right, not a general feature of conservative voting.

Limitation worth raising: online panel with quotas is not a probability sample, though the quotas are designed to balance demographics. More importantly, Vox supporters and non-supporters may drop out of the panel differentially, potentially biasing estimates of change over time.

**Blee:**

Qualitative methods across two cases:
- *1920s KKK:* Archival research (national Women's Klan organizations; Indiana state chapters) plus retrospective interviews with elderly former Klanswomen in Indiana
- *Contemporary organized racism:* Extensive life history interviews with a sample of women racist activists, purposively sampled across group type, age, position, and region. Supplementary interviews added to address emergent questions.

The article itself is not primarily a data paper — it's an interpretive/historiographical essay. The appropriate question is not whether the argument is statistically significant but whether the templates Blee identifies are real, whether her counter-examples are persuasive, and whether the four questions at the end are the right ones to ask.

### Anticipated Student Moves

**"Doesn't Anduiza & Rico just show that sexist people vote for sexist parties? Isn't that obvious?"**

This "it's obvious" response is actually worth taking seriously as a methodological point — there's always a risk that political attitudes and vote intentions share method variance, especially with contemporaneous measures. But two things undercut the "obvious" reading. First, the placebo test: sexism doesn't predict PP voting, even though the PP is also to the right of center. Modern sexism as Anduiza & Rico measure it is tracking something that maps specifically onto the far-right niche. Second, and more importantly: the lagged and change-in-change specifications show that sexism measured *prior to Vox's visibility* predicted later switching to Vox. If the relationship were entirely driven by partisan consistency, you'd expect sexism changes during Vox's rise to be most predictive — but it's the pre-Vox backlash period that matters.

**"If feminist mobilization caused backlash, does that mean feminism is counterproductive?"**

This is the strategic/normative question that follows naturally from Q2 in the discussion activity. Redirect it rather than resolving it. Several considerations complicate the simple "backlash → don't mobilize" conclusion: (a) we don't know the counterfactual — Vox might have emerged anyway, or with a different grievance structure; (b) the feminist wave also had effects on non-backlashing individuals, potentially making sexism less acceptable in the mainstream; (c) Anduiza & Rico show that overall sexism *decreased* during the feminist wave — the backlash was a minority response within a broader progressive trend. The question is whether a smaller, faster far-right party is worse or better than a slower, larger one that would have emerged anyway.

**"Blee seems to be arguing that women join far-right movements for non-ideological reasons — doesn't that excuse them?"**

This reflects the Template 5 problem Blee herself identifies: we tend to treat women's far-right participation as less agentive, more socially determined. The Tristian Frye case (the prosecutor who recommended a reduced sentence for a woman who kicked a homeless man to death) illustrates how this plays out in practice. Blee's actual claim is not that ideology is irrelevant but that it sometimes comes *after* affiliation rather than before. Sean learned white supremacy ideology *in* the movement; Sandy became ideologically committed *through* street fighting. Being recruited through social ties doesn't make the subsequent commitment less real or the actions less culpable. But it does change the intervention logic — counter-messaging to "correct" ideology may miss the actual recruitment pathway.

**"Spain is one country. How do we know modern sexism drives far-right voting elsewhere?"**

Legitimate external validity concern. Anduiza & Rico acknowledge Spain is a *hard case* — if sexism matters there, where the Catalan conflict and immigration are the standard explanations, it probably matters in easier cases too. But they also note the measurement problem: most comparative surveys use thin gender attitude items that don't capture modern sexism. The absence of evidence in comparative literature may partly reflect inadequate measurement. A useful research direction is whether the modern sexism battery deployed in Spain would show similar results in Germany, France, or Sweden.

**"Wasn't the 1920s KKK different from the 'far right' — wasn't it mainstream?"**

Good question that pushes on definitional issues from earlier in the course. The 1920s KKK was genuinely mass — 3–5 million members — and had mainstream political power in several states. But it promoted white supremacy, anti-Catholic bigotry, anti-Semitism, and violence (physical for men; reputational for women). ## Session 7B: Gender Ideology, Natalism, and the Far Right

**Focus:** Session 7A established gender as a driver of individual far-right support (Anduiza & Rico) and as a structural dimension of movement composition (Blee). This session zooms out to ask what the far right is actually *doing* with gender at the level of organized politics and state power. ### Opening Hook (8 min): Two Moments, One Logic

Put these two moments on the board side by side, without comment:

**October 2, 2016 — Bogotá, Colombia:** After 52 years of civil war, Colombia's peace agreement with the FARC guerrillas is submitted to a public referendum. Evangelical and Catholic churches have organized mass opposition, distributing flyers claiming the agreement is a vehicle for "gender ideology." Their specific objection: the agreement contains language about equal rights for LGBTQ+ Colombians and women's rights in the post-conflict context. The "No" side wins by less than half a percentage point. The peace agreement is defeated.

**March 27, 2025 — Washington, D.C.:** At a White House Women's History Month event, President Trump declares himself the "fertilization president," announces an executive order expanding IVF access, and describes the previous administration's recognition of trans identity as "a Marxist war on women." He signs an order directing all federal agencies to recognize only two biological sexes, titled "Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism, and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government."

Ask students: What connects these two moments — one in Colombia, one in the US, separated by nine years? What is the shared conceptual vocabulary? Who are the actors?

The answer Corredor provides is: they are both instances of the same transnational antigender countermovement, deploying the same rhetorical infrastructure — "gender ideology" as a catch-all villain — in different national contexts. Brady's paper shows what the US case looks like when you follow it through to policy. The session's task is to understand both the global architecture (Corredor) and the local implementation (Brady).

### Mini-Lecture 1: Corredor — The Antigender Countermovement (20 min)

**The article's intervention.**

Corredor (2019), published in *Signs*, argues that antigender campaigns have been misread as isolated, spontaneous, or purely local phenomena. The existing scholarship treats them country by country — Poland, Croatia, France, Colombia, Hungary — as if each were independent. Corredor's claim is that this misses the essential feature: antigenderism is a *coordinated transnational countermovement* responding to a perceived common threat. The same vocabulary, the same rhetorical moves, and in many cases the same organizational infrastructure appear across geopolitically diverse settings, because they were developed and diffused deliberately.

This matters analytically because countermovements are not the mirror image of the movements they oppose. They have their own logic, strategies, and internal dynamics. To understand feminist and LGBTQ+ politics, you have to understand their organized opposition — just as understanding the far right requires understanding what it is reacting against.

**What is "gender ideology"?**

The term is not neutral description — it is a rhetorical weapon, and Corredor traces its manufacture precisely. The Vatican created it in the early 2000s in response to feminist conceptual gains at UN World Conferences. The core claim: feminist and queer theory has invented an "ideology of gender" that denies biological sex, destroys the natural family, promotes homosexuality and abortion, and threatens children's healthy development. In a 2001 statement, Pope John Paul II warned against "misleading concepts concerning sexuality and the dignity and mission of the woman" driven by "specific ideologies on 'gender.'" The Pontifical Council for the Family's 2003 *Lexicon* systematized this, branding feminist gender theory as "neo-Marxist" — a term with explicit political valence particularly in post-Communist Eastern Europe.

Corredor's definition of gender ideology as a counterstrategy: it aims to (1) refute claims about the hierarchical construction of the gendered and heterosexual order; (2) essentialize and delegitimize feminist and queer theories; (3) frustrate gender mainstreaming in policy; (4) thwart gender and LGBTQ+ equality legislation; and (5) reaffirm heteropatriarchal conceptions of sex and family. It is, in other words, not a description of what feminists believe but a weapon designed to defeat them.

**Movement-countermovement theory: the four conditions.**

Corredor applies standard social movement theory to explain when and why countermovements emerge. Four conditions must be met:

1. A social movement challenging existing power arrangements
2. A privileged collective that perceives its interests threatened
3. The existing movement shows signs of actual or potential policy success
4. Opponents have political allies and resources to mobilize

On all four dimensions, the feminist/LGBTQ+ movement triggered a countermovement response at the UN level in the 1990s. The sequence matters: antigenderism was *reactive*, not pre-existing. It emerged specifically in response to feminist policy gains, not to abstract feminist ideas. This is why Corredor insists on countermovement framing: the antigender movement cannot be understood except in relation to the feminist successes it was designed to reverse.

**The historical trigger: UN World Conferences, 1994–1995.**

Walk students through the key inflection points:

*Cairo 1994 (International Conference on Population and Development):* Feminists secured language about "ensuring women's ability to control their own fertility" as a "cornerstone of population and development." The Vatican and Muslim leadership filed formal objections. First signs of coordinated opposition — not yet synchronized, but beginning to work in concert.

*Beijing 1995 (Fourth World Conference on Women):* The feminist high-water mark. 17,000 in the official conference, 30,000 at the NGO forum. The draft platform proposed defining gender as "socially determined" rather than biological — a fundamental conceptual shift that would have given feminist understandings of gender the authority of UN policy. The Vatican led the charge against this language, rallying a coalition of Catholic and Muslim countries. In the final hours, all references to sexual orientation were removed from the platform and the proposed feminist definition of gender was blocked. The final document stated that gender "was intended to be interpreted and understood as it was in ordinary, generally accepted usage" — a explicit defeat for the feminist redefinition.

The Vatican's response in the aftermath was to *accelerate* its counter-offensive, developing gender ideology as a robust rhetorical system and expanding its coalition beyond explicitly Catholic actors to include Evangelical Protestants, conservative Muslims, right-wing politicians, and conservative think tanks.

**Three leading counterstrategies — each exploiting divisions within feminism.**

This is Corredor's most analytically original contribution: the antigender countermovement's rhetorical strategies are specifically designed to exploit *pre-existing internal divisions* within feminist and LGBTQ+ movements.

*Strategy 1 — Equality/sameness/difference:* The Vatican distinguished "equity feminism" (acceptable: seeks legal and moral equality, respects biological sex difference and the family) from "gender feminism" (pernicious: neo-Marxist, advocates constructivist gender, destabilizes family and motherhood). This exploits a genuine and long-standing feminist debate about whether equality means sameness with men or recognition of difference. By bifurcating feminism, the Church positions itself as the genuine champion of women's equality while delegitimizing the strands of feminism that most threaten it. It can simultaneously claim to support women's rights (antipoverty, anti-violence) while opposing abortion, same-sex marriage, and gender mainstreaming.

*Strategy 2 — Colonization and imperialism:* Framing gender equality as Western cultural imperialism — an imposition of liberal Northern values on the Global South. This strategy drew strength from genuine and well-grounded critiques within transnational feminism about Western dominance, the marginalization of Global South perspectives, and the hegemony of European and US feminist frameworks. When Nigerian author Obianuju Ekeocha titled her book *Target Africa: Ideological Neocolonialism of the Twenty-First Century* and described international donors as pushing "radical feminism, population control, sexualisation of children, and homosexuality," she was exploiting real resentments about postcolonial power dynamics. The Colombia case from the hook is a direct instance: characterizing LGBTQ+ protections in a peace agreement as foreign ideological imposition resonated with existing anti-Western sentiment.

*Strategy 3 — Communism, totalitarianism, and terrorism:* Polish Bishop Tadeusz Pieronek: "Gender ideology is worse than Communism and Nazism put together." Guinean Cardinal Robert Sarah: "We find ourselves between gender ideology and ISIS." The Vatican's *Lexicon* explicitly labeled feminist gender theory "neo-Marxist." By linking feminist/LGBTQ+ goals to the greatest political catastrophes of the 20th century, the countermovement activates fears with deep historical resonance, particularly in countries that experienced totalitarian rule. It renders the feminist agenda not merely mistaken but existentially threatening. Trump's framing — "ending the Marxist war on women," "Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism" — is a direct inheritance of this Vatican-developed rhetorical strategy.

**The heterogeneous coalition.**

The global Right in Corredor's account is explicitly not just the far right in the narrow electoral sense. It includes the Catholic Church, Evangelical Protestants, conservative Muslims, right-wing politicians, and conservative think tanks — diverse actors who would otherwise disagree sharply on many issues but are united by opposition to feminist and LGBTQ+ policy. "Gender ideology" functions as a *meta-frame* that enables coalition-building across these divisions. This has important implications: it means the antigender countermovement has resources, organizational infrastructure, and political allies that exceed any single party or movement. It is not simply a creature of far-right electoral politics; it is a much broader mobilization with the far right as one important component.

*Pedagogical note:* Students may push back on whether the Catholic Church or mainstream Evangelical organizations should be grouped with "the far right." This is a productive tension. Corredor's answer is not that all antigender actors *are* far right, but that they constitute a countermovement whose interests converge with far-right politics in this specific domain. The alliance is issue-specific, not total.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Brady — Natalism, Race, and the Mother Citizen (17 min)

**The article's argument in one sentence.**

Brady (2026), published in *Social Identities*, argues that contemporary US pronatalist discourse and Trump administration policy construct an ideal "mother citizen" — implicitly white, heteronormative, fertile, selfless — in binary opposition to a series of "outsider/others" who are treated as threats to the nation's demographic future. Pronatalism and antinatalism are not contradictory in the Trump administration; they are the same neo-mercantilist project aimed at different populations.

**The Derridean binary.**

Brady's theoretical frame draws on poststructuralist sign theory. Following Derrida, every sign is constituted through its binary opposite — the "good mother" citizen can only exist if there is a "bad mother" or "non-mother" other. The binary is never neutral; one side is always privileged. Brady maps the specific others being constructed in Trump-era natalist discourse:

- The voluntarily childless woman ("childless cat ladies" — JD Vance's phrase)
- The Latina/migrant mother (targeted by deportations, denial of birthright citizenship)
- The trans person (erased by the "two sexes only" executive order)
- The disabled/neurodivergent person (RFK Jr.'s characterization of autistic children as a burden who "will never pay taxes, hold a job, or go on a date")
- The queer parent (excluded by the IVF executive order's emphasis on "mothers and fathers," "loving couples")

Each of these is constructed in binary opposition to the ideal white, heteronormative, able-bodied mother citizen. The logic is not additive but structural: the ideal citizen is *constituted through* the denigration of these others.

**Neo-mercantilist natalism and its historical lineages.**

Brady uses historian Richard Togman's concept of "neo-mercantilist natalism" — a state approach that responds to perceived threats from both "foreign/enemy states" and "foreign/enemy peoples." Unlike mercantilist natalism (concerned with national power through population size) or generic pronatalism (concerned with fertility rates in general), neo-mercantilist natalism explicitly targets *which* populations should grow and which should shrink. The "fertility crisis" is a crisis of the *right kind* of people not having enough babies.

Brady traces two historical parallels the Trump administration recapitulates:

*Early 20th-century US eugenics:* Figures at Stanford (David Starr Jordan, Lewis Terman) and throughout US universities promoted scientific racism and the idea that "genius" was heritable and more common among whites. This produced forced sterilization laws disproportionately applied to Latina women (59% higher rates in California's eugenic sterilization program), Black women, Indigenous people, and disabled people. Margaret Sanger's birth control advocacy explicitly merged with eugenics. The UN Genocide Convention specifically lists "imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group" as an act of genocide — Brady cites William L. Patterson's 1951 petition to the UN *We Charge Genocide* which made exactly this argument about US treatment of Black reproduction.

*Nazi Germany:* The Mother's Cross (gold for 8+ children, silver for 6-7, bronze for 4-5) awarded only to women who were "genetically fit," born within wedlock, and maintained clean homes. Women divided into "mothers of the race" and "sources of racial degeneration." Brady shows a Nazi propaganda image (Figure 2) of the ideal Aryan mother — blonde, in a field, breastfeeding — and then notes: "The aesthetic of Neeleman and other prolific tradwife influencers bears a striking resemblance to that of the young, white, fertile mother in Nazi propaganda in both dress, nationalistic and wholesome tone, agrarian setting, and treatment of prolific childrearing as healthy and patriotic." This is the course arc moment — Brady explicitly connects Neeleman/Ballerina Farm to this historical tradition.

**The double movement: pronatalism and antinatalism as one policy.**

The Trump administration's policy logic is not contradictory; it is coherent:

*Pronatalist side:* "Fertilization president" branding; IVF executive order ("Because we want more babies"); Medal of Motherhood proposal (for 6+ children); opposition to abortion; "ending the Marxist war on women" = opposing feminist policy that might compete with motherhood as women's primary identity.

*Antinatalist side:* Mass deportations of Latinx migrants (including US citizen children deported alongside their mothers); denial of birthright citizenship (executive order revoking 150-year constitutional interpretation); family separations; ICE detention center performing hysterectomies on detainees without informed consent; Latinas showing lower birth weights and more preterm births during Trump's first term; welcoming 49 white Afrikaners for resettlement while deporting thousands of Latinx migrants.

The double movement is unified by racial logic: more white, Christian babies; fewer racialized, immigrant, disabled, queer, or trans people. Brady also notes the irony that pronatalist policies consistently fail to significantly impact fertility rates — but that their actual function may be ideological rather than demographic.

**The tradwife/influencer connection.**

Brady explicitly analyzes the tradwife cultural phenomenon as part of this natalist ecosystem. Conservative influencers like Gretchen Adler and Hannah Neeleman (Ballerina Farm) create content that normalizes prolific white motherhood as a lifestyle aspiration. The politics are accomplished through the aesthetic — not through explicit advocacy but through the performance of an ideal. Brady: "While the less overtly judgmental tone of Neeleman's content might vary from that of Trump and Musk, and conservative tradmom influencers like Adler, its popularity and prototypicality at the same moment as politicized attempts to push natalism amongst specific portions of the population *have the effect of being political.*"

The connection to Elon Musk (14 children with 4 women, "doing my best to help the underpopulation crisis") and Ashley St. Clair (Fox News pundit, Musk's partner, one of his children's mothers, who condemned women who "just want to pursue pleasure and drinking all night, going to Beyoncé concerts") shows the Silicon Valley pronatalist ecosystem feeding into the same cultural logic.

*Instructor note:* The St. Clair case has a dark footnote — she later revealed she had been instructed to keep Musk's child secret, was isolated in a New York apartment for five months, could not tell family or friends, and could not continue her paid work. Brady includes this in a footnote as evidence of the gap between pronatalist ideology and the actual experience of women within it.

### Methods Notes

**Corredor:**

Theoretically-driven qualitative analysis — a countermovement framework applied to a corpus of primary documents (Vatican documents, UN conference records, national political statements) and secondary literature across multiple national contexts. Not a data paper; the method is conceptual integration and cross-national comparison. The appropriate evaluative question is: does the countermovement framework travel? Does it illuminate the phenomenon better than treating each national case individually? Students should be equipped to assess whether Corredor's claim of *coordination* (not just convergence) is adequately supported — she makes a strong transnational claim that requires showing organizational connections, not just rhetorical similarity.

The article is notably more prescriptive about the category of analysis than about the underlying empirical dynamics. Students may ask: what would falsify the claim that these are "coordinated" countermovements rather than independently arriving at similar conclusions because they face a common opponent? This is a productive methodological question.

**Brady:**

Discourse analysis drawing on Derridean and Foucauldian poststructuralist frameworks — examining sign systems, binary oppositions, and how power operates through language and representation. Primary sources include executive orders, political speeches, social media content, journalism, and academic literature. Published in *Social Identities* (a cultural studies journal rather than political science) — the disciplinary home explains the theoretical framing and also the somewhat different standard of evidence compared to quantitative work the course has engaged with.

Brady draws extensively on secondary sources (Togman's historical natalism work, Platt on US eugenics, Rossy on Nazi pronatalist propaganda) to establish historical lineages. Students should note that arguing for historical *resonance* (the aesthetic resembles, the logic echoes) is different from arguing for historical *causation* (this directly descended from). Brady is careful about this distinction in most places; it's worth flagging.

### Anticipated Student Moves

**"Is it fair to compare Trump's natalism to Nazi Germany? Isn't that just Godwin's law?"**

This is the most predictable pushback and worth addressing directly rather than deflecting. Brady's argument is structural and specific, not rhetorical: she identifies a common *logic* (neo-mercantilist natalism — encourage births among the "right" population, discourage/prevent births among others), common *mechanisms* (propaganda constructing ideal mothers; legal measures controlling reproduction of "undesirable" populations; characterizing disabled people as burdens), and documented *historical connections* (US eugenicists and Nazi researchers exchanged research and encouraged one another; the Nazis explicitly modeled their racial hygiene legislation on US examples). Brady is not calling Trump a Nazi; she is identifying a recurring logic of population management with specific historical instantiations. Students who find this comparison uncomfortable should be asked: what would *you* need to see before the comparison would be appropriate? That specifies the empirical question.

**"Is Corredor saying the Catholic Church is part of the far right?"**

No — and this is important. Corredor is making a more specific claim: that the Catholic Church is the *founder and primary architect* of the antigender countermovement, and that this countermovement has convergent interests with far-right political actors in this particular domain. The alliance is issue-specific. The Church also maintains positions (on economic justice, immigration, the death penalty) that are not aligned with the far right. Corredor's countermovement framework requires that the coalition be united around a *common purpose* (defeating feminist/LGBTQ+ policy), not that all members share all political commitments. Students with Catholic backgrounds may find this framing uncomfortable; the productive response is to distinguish between "the Church is far right" (Corredor doesn't say this) and "the Church has been the leading institutional architect of a specific countermovement that benefits far-right politics" (Corredor does say this, with evidence).

**"If feminist mobilization creates backlash (Anduiza), doesn't Corredor's countermovement story just confirm that feminists are to blame for the far right?"**

This is a sophisticated student connecting two readings, and it deserves a serious response. The "feminists caused their own backlash" reading misunderstands Corredor's causal story. Corredor shows that the antigender countermovement emerged when feminism *succeeded* — when it achieved concrete policy gains at the UN level, when it made gender mainstreaming part of international governance. The reaction was to success, not to failure. Moreover, Corredor's framework shows that the reaction was *organized* (not just spontaneous individual backlash), *transnational* (not just local resentment), and *strategically designed* to exploit feminist divisions rather than simply opposing feminist ideas. Anduiza & Rico's backlash dynamic operates at the individual voter level; Corredor's countermovement operates at the organizational and transnational level. These are not the same phenomenon. Blaming feminist success for the backlash would be like arguing that the civil rights movement was responsible for the rise of white supremacy organizations — it mistakes the reactive structure for causal responsibility.

**"Aren't there legitimate concerns about declining birth rates?"**

Brady's argument is not that demographic concerns are inherently illegitimate but that the neo-mercantilist framing of those concerns — *which births* are desirable and which are not — is the problem. Brady notes that demographers actually disagree about what predicted population declines in the Global North mean: declining teen pregnancy rates are often a sign of higher standards of living. The pronatalist movement's premise is itself contested. More importantly, the differential application — encourage white births, suppress immigrant/racialized/disabled births — is what connects it to eugenics and to far-right politics. A genuine concern about population and welfare could lead to policies supporting all families regardless of race, origin, or composition; the specificity of who is targeted reveals the ideology at work.

Blee's question ("how does gender matter in far-right movements?") is answered at a different scale here: not as a dimension of individual movement participation but as a coordinated epistemological and political project.

**To Valentim (5A):** The antigender countermovement is simultaneously a normalization project in Valentim's sense. By making "gender ideology" vocabulary mainstream — placing it in UN documents as a contested term, in national political debates, in presidential speeches — the countermovement has eroded the social norm that treats anti-feminist and anti-LGBTQ+ positions as fringe. Brady's tradwife influencer analysis shows the cultural mechanism: the lifestyle content that makes pronatalism aesthetically aspirational before it becomes politically conscious. This is Valentim's preference falsification in reverse: instead of a hidden preference that eventually surfaces, a preference that is being cultivated and made expressible through deliberate cultural work.

**To Week 4 (Ballerina Farm):** Brady's direct citation of Neeleman/Ballerina Farm in the context of pronatalist natalism gives students who engaged with the Week 4 discussion a second analytical frame for the same phenomenon. In Week 4, Neeleman was analyzed through the lens of normalization and the tradwife genre. In 7B, she appears within a longer historical argument about pronatalism, eugenics, and the construction of the ideal mother citizen. Students should now have two complementary analytical frames for the same object — and can ask which frame is more explanatorily powerful or whether they are describing different aspects of the same phenomenon.

**To Week 6 (Violence):** Corredor's countermovement is, in most of its manifestations, a non-violent political and cultural project. But Brady's historical genealogy connects it to violence at the extremes: the Christchurch shooter's manifesto began with "It's the birthrates"; the Colombian conference in Texas with ties to white supremacist groups advocating violence; ICE detention conditions including non-consensual sterilizations. The continuum from influencer aesthetic to policy to violence is a structural feature of the pronatalist ecosystem, not incidental to it. This parallels the Darby quote in the Ballerina Farm notes about the "ideological supply chain" from normalization content to eliminationist violence.

**To Week 8 (Electoral Politics):** The success of the antigender countermovement at the electoral level — far-right parties winning on platforms that explicitly deploy gender ideology rhetoric — is what Week 8 should address. Corredor's framework raises the question of what electoral politics looks like when a transnational organizational infrastructure is already in place: the party doesn't need to build from scratch; it can activate a pre-existing counter-mobilization. Brady's paper shows what governing looks like when that countermovement achieves executive power.
