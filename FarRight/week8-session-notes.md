## Session 8A: Racial Ideology, Replacement, and the Conservative–Far-Right Spectrum

**Focus:** Week 7 examined gender as a driver of individual far-right support and as a dimension of movement life. This session shifts to race — not as demographic background but as *ideology*: a system of claims, narratives, and interpretive frameworks that the far right actively constructs and deploys. Dixit analyzes the "great replacement" conspiracy as it appears in far-right manifestos, tracing the narrative strategies through which white victimhood is constructed. Hellström (2026) maps the landscape of sociological frameworks for understanding racism and asks which framework best captures what contemporary far-right parties are actually doing — arguing that the answer is neo-racism or "cultural racism," not the scientific racism most people have in mind when they hear the word. A primary source from a contemporary far-right podcast brings both papers into contact with live political speech.

A note on the primary source: this session uses excerpts from a YouTube interview between Nick Fuentes (America First movement) and Joel Webbon (New Christian Right/Right Response Ministries) on "race realism." The conversation is racist and you should prepare students for that before playing any clips. The pedagogical value is precisely that the rhetoric is careful, self-aware, and theologically framed — it's an unusually good specimen of how replacement-adjacent ideology is packaged for a contemporary audience.

### Opening Hook (8 min): "What Am I Hearing?"

Play a 90-second clip from the Fuentes/Webbon "race realism" interview (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jtgsduCeoxM) — the passage beginning roughly at 7:38 where Webbon introduces the "prejudgment" rationalization:

> "I run a business and over the course of the last 20 years I've hired, you know, 100 employees under the age of 25, and 98 of them have stolen from the company. Like if that guy says, you know, from now on I'm not going to hire kids... We wouldn't say that that's sinful. So... help, I'm being overwhelmed by ants. Not all ants — same here."

Play the clip cold, without identifying the speakers. Then ask:

> What is the argument being made here? What's the logical structure? At what point, if any, does it go wrong?

Give students 60 seconds to write a response, then take 3-4 answers. Most will identify the move correctly: a statistical generalization from a personal experience is being used to justify categorical prejudgment. Some will add that the "not all ants" joke normalizes the move by treating the category (insects) as obviously available for generalization. What they may not notice yet is what comes immediately after — the explicit extension of this logic to race.

Reveal context: this is from a 2025 podcast in which Nick Fuentes, a leading figure of the America First movement who traveled with Donald Trump during the 2024 campaign, is explaining why racial stereotyping is legitimate. Joel Webbon is a pastor with an organization, Right Response Ministries, dedicated to training evangelical churches in "America First" politics.

Now play a second short clip (~21:38–22:57) where Webbon applies the same logical structure directly:

> "I would say all white people are... suicidal, toxic empathy and cowardly. And that's why the West is falling apart... And then I feel like I could also say all black people are... impulsive... lazy... And I feel like I could also to go one step further, say all Jews are subversive."

These two clips — the reasonable-sounding setup and the conclusion it leads to — frame the session's analytical task: understanding how racial ideology is constructed to feel like common sense.

### Mini-Lecture 1: Dixit — White Victimhood and the Great Replacement (20 min)

**The chapter's intervention.**

Chapter 6 of Priya Dixit's *Race, Popular Culture, and Far-right Extremism in the United States* (2022) analyzes a specific form of far-right communication: the manifesto. Three mass murderers who wrote and published manifestos are examined as archetypes: Elliot Rodger (Isla Vista, 2014), Dylann Roof (Charleston, 2015), and Patrick Crusius (El Paso, 2019). The chapter also analyzes Renaud Camus's book *You Will Not Replace Us* (2018) as the intellectual apparatus that far-right actors draw on. The argument: all of these texts use a common set of narrative strategies to construct *white victimhood* — to position white men and white society as the targets of displacement, with violence presented as self-defense.

This is not primarily a study of psychological motivations or organizational dynamics. Dixit is doing discourse analysis: asking what rhetorical work these texts do, how they produce meaning, and why they have been effective as tools of radicalization and recruitment.

**Red-pilling: the individual conversion narrative.**

A key concept for the session is "red-pilling," drawn from the 1999 film *The Matrix*. In the film, the protagonist Neo is offered a choice: take the blue pill and remain in comfortable ignorance, or take the red pill and see "how deep the rabbit hole goes." Far-right actors adopted this metaphor to describe the experience of conversion to their worldview: the red-pilled person has been awakened to a hidden truth about race, Jewish power, feminist domination, or civilizational decline that is being suppressed by mainstream institutions.

Red-pilling has several analytically significant features:

*Urgency and irreversibility.* The Matrix framing presents the choice as one-time and final — once you take the red pill, you can't go back. This fuels the sense of existential crisis that Dixit identifies as central to far-right radicalization: you have seen the truth, you cannot un-see it, and the situation requires action.

*Epistemic inversion.* Red-pilling inverts the normal relationship between knowledge and authority. The university, the mainstream media, and the government are not reliable sources of truth but systems of deception. The "real" knowledge is found in online communities, manifestos, and the evidence of your own eyes. This is why Roof's self-radicalization through a Google search for "black on white crime" is analytically important: he was following the red-pilling script, doing "his own research" and discovering a "hidden truth" that contradicted official narratives. Safiya Noble's research shows what he found — not real crime statistics but white nationalist websites algorithmically ranked at the top of his search results.

*Community of the awakened.* Being red-pilled creates a sense of belonging to a select group that understands what others refuse to see. This functions as a social reward that reinforces the conversion, independent of whether the underlying claims are true.

**Three archetypes of the great replacement.**

Dixit shows that the "great replacement" conspiracy is remarkably flexible: any outgroup can be cast as the group doing the "replacing." The three manifesto-writers each instantiated a different version:

*Elliot Rodger — replacement by women and other men.* In the incel/manosphere variant, the "great replacement" logic applies to sexual competition. Rodger narrated himself as a "nice guy" being displaced from his rightful access to women and social status by women who preferred "chads" (conventionally attractive men) and by feminism. Women were both the cause of his victimhood (by rejecting him) and the objects of his resentment. Dixit identifies two white-woman archetypes in Rodger's manifesto: the mother (idealized until she is perceived as betraying him to his sister's boyfriend) and the whore (women who are sexually available to others but not to him). Violence was presented as the restoration of the natural order.

*Dylann Roof — replacement by Black Americans.* Roof's self-radicalization began with a Google search about the Trayvon Martin case and led him to white nationalist websites that he took as authoritative. His manifesto narrates the "awakening" in first-person: he is repeatedly "in disbelief" when he discovers what he claims to be the truth about Black crime. The narrative structure is red-pilling: ordinary person, shocking discovery, conversion to a worldview others refuse to see, action. Roof's view of white women differs from Rodger's — women are not the enemy but rescuable victims of "race mixing" who can be "saved" by white men. His "Last Rhodesian" website signaled his admiration for white minority rule in southern Africa: one of the last redoubts where white men actually did exercise the racial dominance he thought was natural and threatened.

*Patrick Crusius — replacement by Hispanic immigrants.* Crusius's manifesto explicitly references Camus's "great replacement" and frames Hispanic immigration as an "invasion" — paralleling Camus's inversion of colonial language. Crucially, he draws a parallel to the Native American experience under European settlement: white people who don't take the invasion seriously will end up like Native Americans, "just a shadow of what was." This appropriation of Indigenous dispossession to frame white demographic anxieties is one of the most striking rhetorical moves Dixit analyzes. Crusius also explicitly states that the US is a better country than Europe for defending against this "invasion" because of gun rights — making mass murder literally the point of the Second Amendment in this framing.

**Renaud Camus as intellectual legitimation.**

Camus is important because he provided the replacement conspiracy with a book-length intellectual apparatus. He is a French writer who, despite promoting a conspiracy theory that has inspired numerous mass murderers, received flattering mainstream media profiles for years. Dixit analyzes this as revealing: promoters of far-right violence are often treated as eccentric intellectuals rather than dangerous ideologues until enough violence occurs to force reframing.

Camus's argumentative moves are the template for all three manifestos:

*Imagined stable white past.* Camus claims France's population was "remarkably stable" for "about fifteen centuries." This is factually false — France's colonial history ensured significant population movement — but it is presented as observation rather than argument. An imagined past of demographic homogeneity is constructed as the baseline against which immigration is experienced as disruption.

*Personal observation as universal truth.* Camus presents his own subjective experiences of changed neighborhoods, different languages heard on the street, and unfamiliar cultural practices as evidence of a systematic plan. No scholarly sources are cited; anecdote becomes data.

*Weaponized critical vocabulary.* The most analytically interesting move: Camus uses the language of "settler colonialism" — a framework developed to describe European conquest of Indigenous peoples — to describe immigration into France. In his telling, France is the colonized country and African immigrants are the settler colonizers. This inverts the historical meaning of the terms to achieve a rhetorical effect: white French people become the Indigenous victims of dispossession.

*The anti-Semitic supplement.* Since migrants themselves are not powerful enough to organize a demographic takeover, Camus requires a hidden organizing force — which falls back on anti-Semitic tropes of shadowy elites engineering the "colonization."

**The modularity of replacement logic.**

Dixit's core analytical point is that the great replacement conspiracy is not a specific claim about a specific group but a *narrative template* that can accommodate any outgroup as the replacer. Women, Black Americans, Hispanic immigrants, Jews — any of these can be inserted into the role of the group that is "replacing" white men/white society. This modularity explains both the conspiracy's flexibility across different segments of the far right and its capacity to absorb new groups as political circumstances change. It is also what connects Camus's French immigration anxieties to Rodger's California incel experiences: the same narrative structure of white male victimhood and justified violence, applied to different outgroups.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Hellström — Three Frameworks for Understanding Racism in the Far Right (15 min)

**The paper's intervention.**

Anton Hellström's 2026 article in *Sociology Compass*, "You Are Not a Racist, Right?—Racism as a Political Activity," begins from a diagnostic problem: why do contemporary far-right parties consistently deny being racist while simultaneously deploying what scholars recognize as racial thinking? His answer is that the denial is not simple hypocrisy — it depends on exploiting a specific, narrow definition of racism that most people in Western politics have come to share. And understanding the *politics* of racism requires mapping the full landscape of available frameworks rather than accepting any single definition as given.

**Two analytical dimensions.**

Hellström organizes the sociology of racism along two axes:

*Focus:* Does the framework analyze racism primarily at the *individual* level (as a prejudice, a belief, an identity held by particular persons) or at the *societal* level (as a structural condition, an institutional pattern, a property of systems rather than of individuals)?

*Width:* Is the definition *narrow* — including only the most explicit and legible forms of racial hierarchy — or *broad* — encompassing subtler forms in which racial logic operates even when the vocabulary of biological race is not used?

These two dimensions generate a conceptual space in which three major frameworks locate themselves.

**Framework 1: Scientific racism — narrow, individually focused.**

The oldest framework defines racism as belief in biologically determined racial hierarchy: some groups are innately inferior to others, and this should determine how society is organized. This is what most people picture when they hear "racist" — a neo-Nazi or explicit white supremacist. It is *narrow* because it requires explicit biological claims, and *individually focused* because it locates racism in the prejudiced mind of particular persons.

Contemporary far-right parties have learned to exploit this framework strategically. If racism = biological supremacism, and the party does not officially endorse biological hierarchy, then the party is — by definition — not racist. The Swedish Democrats' famous "zero tolerance for racism" policy is premised entirely on this move: they adopt the narrow biological definition, formally reject it, and declare themselves clean. The framework projects racism onto the past (the era of eugenics and colonial racial science) and onto the fringe, not onto the mainstream right.

**Framework 2: Neo-racism / cultural racism / differentialist racism — broad, individual to societal focus.**

The second framework emerged from the 1980s European debate about the Nouvelle Droite and is the analytical core of the article. Associated with theorists including Étienne Balibar, Pierre-André Taguieff, and Martin Barker, it identifies the central innovation of postwar far-right racial politics: *culture replaces biology as the marker of immutable group difference.*

The key move: groups are not ranked as superior or inferior — they are described as *different, incompatible, and naturally belonging to separate national or civilizational spaces.* The vocabulary is not hierarchy but incompatibility. This is what the far right calls *ethnopluralism*: not "whites are better than Arabs" but "each people has the right to preserve its own culture, which means different peoples cannot co-exist in the same political space without destroying what is distinctive about each." The argument is formally identical to biological racism — it just substitutes "culture" for "race" at the surface level.

Hellström argues this is still racism because culture is being used *as nature*: groups are locked into fixed cultural genealogies, treated as though cultural difference were heritable and indelible. This makes neo-racism almost impossible to prosecute or sanction publicly, because it can always restate itself in the language of cultural relativism, minority rights, and anti-colonialism. Different groups just naturally belong in different places — that's not racism, that's respect for diversity.

*Victim-perpetrator reversal* (Wodak) is the rhetorical strategy that makes neo-racism politically potent. By claiming that immigration is a form of cultural genocide against European peoples, far-right actors position themselves as the true anti-racists — defenders of cultural diversity and each culture's right to survive — and accuse pro-immigration liberals of being the racists who want to destroy distinct cultures through forced mixing. The far right does not abandon anti-racism as a value; it appropriates anti-racism vocabulary and turns it against multiculturalism.

The Swedish Democrats are the empirical case Hellström develops. Their zero-tolerance policy on racism (Framework 1) coexists with consistent Framework 2 politics: Islam is described as an ideological system irreconcilable with Swedish values; immigration is framed as cultural displacement; integration is called impossible for cultural reasons. The party can do neo-racism under Framework 1's rhetorical cover.

*White melancholia* (Hübinette & Lundström): Both explicit racists and some well-meaning progressives in majority-white societies mourn a more culturally homogeneous past. This shared mourning cuts across the political spectrum and helps explain why neo-racism finds purchase beyond the far-right base — and why the victim-perpetrator reversal lands with some audiences who don't think of themselves as far-right.

**Framework 3: Institutional racism — broad, societally focused.**

The third framework, developed in the US after the Kerner Commission (1968) and in the UK after the Macpherson Report (1999), focuses not on individual prejudice but on *structural and systemic patterns*: how institutions reproduce racial disadvantage even absent individual intent. A school system that consistently under-resources majority-Black schools, a criminal justice system with racially disparate sentencing, a housing market that steers by neighborhood demographics — all perpetuate racial inequality without requiring any individual to hold explicitly racist beliefs.

This framework is *broad* (it sees racism where no racial language is used) and *societally focused* (the unit of analysis is institutions, not persons). Contemporary far-right parties mostly do not engage at this level — they target individual prejudice or cultural incompatibility. But institutional racism is the background condition that far-right parties exploit: they can point to racially unequal outcomes and attribute them to individual or cultural failings rather than structural causes, making Framework 3 politically available for Framework 2 arguments.

**The political stakes of definitional choice.**

Hellström's key analytical payoff: *which framework a political actor deploys is itself a political act.* The Swedish Democrats' "zero tolerance for racism" is a choice of Framework 1; by adopting the narrow biological definition, they can simultaneously do Framework 2 politics and claim to be anti-racist. The choice of definition is not philosophically neutral — it is a strategic move that determines what counts as racism, who can be accused of it, and who is protected from the accusation.

For the course: the ethnopluralism that appears in the Identity Group's transnational ideology (Wojczewski, Week 9) is a political program built entirely on Framework 2. Students should leave 8A able to name what Wojczewski is analyzing — neo-racism that passes as non-racism under Framework 1's definitions.

**Instructor's Note — Blee & Yates (2015):**

The reading for this session in earlier versions of the course was Blee & Yates (2015), "Conservative and Far-Right Movements and Organizations," *Sociology of Race and Ethnicity* (inaugural issue). Their most distinctive empirical finding is worth sharing with students as context: recruits to neo-Nazi groups were not notably more racist or anti-Semitic than similarly situated people *before joining*. Racist and anti-Semitic views were learned through participation in the movement — the movement produces racist beliefs, not just attracts already-racialized individuals. This "organizational learning" finding challenges the assumption that radicalization is simply a matter of extreme beliefs finding an organizational outlet. If students ask why someone who sounds "reasonable" (like Fuentes's interlocutor in the primary source) ends up holding and spreading explicitly racist views, this is part of the answer: you can enter for the social world, the community, or the identity, and learn the ideology through immersion and escalating commitment.

### Discussion Activity (15 min): Anatomy of a Racist Argument

**Format:** Structured claim-testing exercise — different from the case discussions (5A, 5B, 6A), the jigsaw (6B), the 2×2 mapping (7A), and the discourse analysis in pairs (7B). Students work in groups of 3 to evaluate specific claims from the Fuentes/Webbon transcript against the analytical frameworks from both readings.

**Setup:** Distribute or project four excerpts from the Fuentes/Webbon interview. Groups work through a common set of questions. One group should take each excerpt; debrief brings the analyses together.

**Excerpt A** — Fuentes on "prejudgment" (~7:38–8:22):
> "I run a business and over the course of the last 20 years I've hired, you know, 100 employees under the age of 25, and 98 of them have stolen from the company. Like if that guy says from now on I'm not going to hire kids... We wouldn't say that that's sinful. So... not all ants — same here. There's a way of doing that without being mean-spirited, without being hateful... and yet still it is technically prejudice, but not in the sinful negative connotation... but it's simply exercising prejudgment."

**Excerpt B** — Webbon on applying racial generalizations (~21:38–22:57):
> "I would say all white people are... suicidal, toxic empathy and cowardly... And then I feel like I could also say all black people are... impulsive... lazy... And I feel like I could also to go one step further, say all Jews are subversive... I can say those things and not say and therefore I hate them or I wish them harm... but I can still prejudge."

**Excerpt C** — Fuentes on the end of 500 years of white preeminence (~27:30–30:35):
> "We're coming up on maybe the end of 500 years of preeminence by Europeans, by whites... Whites, because we are white, don't maybe even realize the gravity of this... whites were more developed. That's really the elephant in the room. Right. Nobody wants to [say it]... because where even do you get the power dynamic? Right. It's that whites were able to inflict on other people because of their technological prowess, their organization, their societal development. And these other civilizations were weak."

**Excerpt D** — Fuentes on why he used the N-word (~1:06:37–1:07:40):
> "I think it's just a word... the N-word — you know, this is the only word it's treated more like a blasphemy than like a swear word... you can blaspheme Jesus Christ [and that's fine]. And that was my first realization that something was terribly wrong... [saying it] was never about being a shock jock. It was never about making a point. It was just saying, 'I don't play that game.'"

**Discussion questions for all groups:**

1. What narrative move is the excerpt making? Use the vocabulary from Dixit (red-pilling, white victimhood, imagined past, personal observation as universal truth) or Hellström (scientific racism, neo-racism/cultural racism, ethnopluralism, victim-perpetrator reversal) to name it precisely.

2. Dixit analyzes manifestos as texts that construct white victimhood. Is white victimhood present in this excerpt? If so, how? If not, what is being constructed instead?

3. Hellström distinguishes three frameworks for racism: (1) scientific racism — narrow, individual-focused, biological hierarchy; (2) neo-racism — broad, cultural incompatibility without explicit hierarchy; (3) institutional racism — systemic, structural patterns. Which framework(s) best describe what's happening in this excerpt? Is the speaker using racism in a narrow or broad sense — and is that choice strategic?

4. Hellström describes *victim-perpetrator reversal* as a key far-right rhetorical strategy: positioning oneself as the anti-racist while accusing mainstream liberals of being the true racists (because they destroy cultural distinctiveness through forced mixing). Does the excerpt perform this reversal? If so, how does it work?

**What to listen for in debrief:**

On Excerpt A: Students should notice that the employment analogy is structured to make racial generalization feel like a simple extension of common sense ("everyone already does this"). The "not all ants" joke does specific work: it pre-emptively acknowledges the objection ("but not all X are Y") and brushes it aside as obvious. The key question is what's missing from this analogy — specifically, that using a group characteristic to make individual decisions is only rational if the group characteristic is accurate, predictive, and not itself a product of discrimination. None of those conditions are examined.

On Excerpt B: The direct application makes explicit what the prejudgment framework implies. The biblical framing ("Apostle Paul validates that") is doing crucial work: it converts racial stereotyping into an act of Christian discernment. Note also that Webbon's list of "particular sins" for Black people (impulsive, lazy) and Jews (subversive) maps precisely onto historical antisemitic and anti-Black stereotypes — but the framing presents it as original biblical insight, not received prejudice.

On Excerpt C: This is the closest the transcript comes to the Dixit chapter's "great replacement" framing — the acknowledgment of 500 years of white dominance, presented as "development" rather than violence, followed by anxiety about what happens when that dominance ends. Students should notice how Fuentes simultaneously acknowledges white power ("excelled at organized violence") and frames the current moment as white vulnerability ("don't maybe even realize the gravity of this").

On Excerpt D: This is a live demonstration of Hellström's victim-perpetrator reversal applied to language norms. The N-word functions as what Fuentes later in the interview calls a "shibboleth" — a signal that you're not "bought" by mainstream norms. But notice the rhetorical structure: Fuentes is not defending racial slurs on racial grounds; he is defending them on free-speech and anti-deference grounds, positioning himself as the victim of an irrational taboo enforced by people with power over language. The norm against the slur is reframed as an imposed restriction on authentic expression, while using it becomes an act of principled defiance. This maps exactly onto Hellström's reversal logic: the racist is recast as the persecuted party resisting censorship, while the anti-racist norm is recast as oppressive control. Students should ask: what work is this reversal doing that an explicit defense of racial hierarchy couldn't do?

### Closing Question (6 min): Which Racism Is This?

*Suggested prompt:*

> Nick Fuentes says he is not a racist — he is a "race realist" who simply acknowledges truths others are afraid to say. Joel Webbon says he is not a racist — he is a pastor making scriptural observations. Using Hellström's three-framework typology, what *kind* of racism is operating in the Fuentes/Webbon conversation? And which framework are they using to deny that it's racism at all?

This question should produce a productive split. Webbon's explicit statements ("all Jews are subversive, all Black people are impulsive and lazy") look like Framework 1 in content — asserting group-level characteristics as heritable — but the framing (biblical discernment, not biological supremacism) is Framework 2 work: cultural/genealogical claims wrapped in pastoral authority rather than racial science. Fuentes's explanation of white preeminence as "development" rather than inherent superiority is a textbook Framework 2 move. The denial strategy in both cases is Framework 1 cover: they are not claiming biological hierarchy, therefore — by the narrow definition — they are not racists.

The insight Hellström provides: the denial is not hypocrisy but a definitional exploit. Both men know the Framework 1 definition and have structured their claims to pass its test. This is what makes neo-racism politically durable: it is always available for restatement in non-racial terms.

*Course arc connection:* Students who read Corredor (7B) should notice the parallel mechanism: just as "gender ideology" framing made anti-feminist politics appear as a defense of natural order, neo-racism framing makes anti-immigrant politics appear as a defense of cultural diversity. In both cases, the far right appropriates a progressive vocabulary (anti-racism, cultural rights) and inverts it. The mechanism — converting political extremism into a defense of nature against ideology — is the same even as the content differs.

### Key Passages

**Dixit:**

- *The core argument:* "US far-right extremists narrate an imagined history of the United States where whiteness was dominant and is now under threat." (p. 173) — The one-sentence summary of what manifestos do.

- *Red-pilling as epistemic inversion:* "For US far-right extremists, more broadly, red-pilling is about promoting white supremacist, misogynist, xenophobic worldviews, which they consider as 'learning the truth' about the world." (p. 181) — Sets up the discussion of how radicalization works through constructed epistemology.

- *Roof's self-radicalization via search:* "According to the manifesto, Roof allegedly typed 'black on White crime' in a Google search to make sense of the news reporting on Trayvon Martin... What Roof found was information that confirmed a patently false notion that Black violence on White Americans is an American crisis." (Noble, quoted in Dixit, p. 193) — The mechanism of algorithmic radicalization.

- *Camus on replacement as fact:* "The Great Replacement is not a theory, it is a ghastly fact." (Camus, 2018, p. 135, quoted in Dixit p. 190) — The epistemological claim at the heart of the conspiracy.

- *Camus's inverted colonialism:* "France and Europe are much more colonized by Africa, these days, than they ever colonized it themselves." (Camus, 2018, p. 42, quoted in Dixit p. 191) — The weaponization of postcolonial vocabulary.

- *Modularity of replacement:* "One key factor for the spread of the great replacement conspiracy is that any group of people can be made to fit as the group doing the 'replacing' of the white population." (p. 192) — The structural feature that explains the conspiracy's flexibility and durability.

**Hellström:**

- *The diagnostic problem:* "Racism as a concept has become highly contested in society today, not least in politics where it is often used as a weapon between opposing parties. Most parties, not least far-right parties, deny being racist and often accuse their opponents of being the 'real racists.'" — The starting observation that motivates the typological project.

- *The two-dimensional typology:* Hellström's two axes — focus (individual vs. societal) and width (narrow vs. broad) — generate the framework for distinguishing scientific racism, neo-racism, and institutional racism. The typology is the article's main analytical contribution; any passage that applies the axes to a specific case is a key teaching moment.

- *Neo-racism and culture-as-nature:* Hellström, drawing on Balibar and Taguieff, identifies the central move of neo-racism: culture replaces biology as the marker of immutable group difference, but the logic is formally identical. Groups are locked into cultural genealogies that function as heritable and indelible. This is why ethnopluralism — the doctrine that each people belongs in its own national space — counts as neo-racism even when it explicitly disavows racial hierarchy.

- *Victim-perpetrator reversal:* The article, citing Wodak, identifies the rhetorical strategy by which far-right actors claim to be the true anti-racists: they position themselves as defenders of cultural diversity (each culture has the right to survive) and accuse pro-immigration liberals of destroying that diversity through forced mixing. Anti-racism vocabulary is appropriated and inverted.

- *The Swedish Democrats case:* The party's "zero tolerance for racism" policy operates on a Framework 1 definition — biological supremacism. This coexists with Framework 2 politics: emphasis on cultural incompatibility, the impossibility of integration, Islam as an ideological system irreconcilable with Swedish values. The party performs anti-racism under Framework 1's definitions while practicing neo-racism under Framework 2's.

- *White melancholia* (Hübinette & Lundström, cited in Hellström): The mourning of a more homogeneous past is not confined to racists; it appears across the political spectrum in majority-white societies. This is what makes the victim-perpetrator reversal resonant beyond the far-right base: the emotional substrate (loss of cultural certainty) is widely shared even when the political conclusions are not.

### Methods Notes

**Dixit:**

Discourse analysis of publicly available texts — manifestos, Camus's book, online writings. The method involves close reading of rhetorical strategies: how narrative structure, choice of metaphor, epistemic claims, and historical representation construct ideological meaning. Dixit's analysis is explicitly normative in a way that much social science is not: she names the claims as false and dangerous rather than neutrally describing them. This is worth flagging for students trained in value-neutral social science. The analytical move she makes — distinguishing between what a text *claims* and what rhetorical *work* the claim does — is the core of discourse analysis and is exactly the right tool for analyzing political speech.

Limitation worth raising: Dixit selects three archetypes deliberately; they are not a representative sample of far-right actors or manifestos. The selection is analytically justified (each represents a distinct variant of the replacement conspiracy) but doesn't tell us about the full distribution of ideological content across far-right violence.

**Hellström:**

*Sociology Compass* is a review journal — it commissions scholarly review articles that synthesize and map a literature for a broad social-science audience. Hellström's article is therefore a review essay, not an original empirical study. The core method is systematic literature review combined with corpus linguistics: Hellström reports analyzing 2,543 abstracts from articles in the sociology of race and ethnicity to map which frameworks dominate the literature, how they are distributed across research traditions, and how they have evolved. This is a methodologically distinctive piece in the course — no other reading uses corpus linguistics on a scholarly literature to answer a social science question.

The appropriate evaluative question is not "does the study have a valid identification strategy" (it's not a causal study) but: is the typology well-constructed? Does it genuinely distinguish the frameworks, or does it obscure as much as it clarifies? Are the empirical cases (Swedish Democrats) well-selected and fairly characterized? Students trained in quantitative methods should be pushed to recognize that typological/conceptual work has its own quality criteria — coherence, parsimony, coverage, analytical payoff — that are different from but not inferior to statistical validity.

The choice to use corpus linguistics on scholarly abstracts (rather than, say, on party manifestos or interview transcripts) is also worth discussing: Hellström is mapping the *academic* landscape, not the political landscape. The implication is that scholars of racism are not all working within the same framework and may be talking past each other. This is relevant to the course as a whole: when researchers argue about whether something is racist, they may be applying different frameworks rather than disagreeing about facts.

### Anticipated Student Moves

**"But isn't race biologically real? There are genetic differences between populations."**

This is the most common objection and deserves a careful response. The scientific consensus among geneticists and anthropologists is that human genetic variation is real and structured by ancestry and geography — but that it does not map onto the folk racial categories (Black, white, Hispanic, Asian) that "race realism" discourse uses. Genetic variation is clinal (gradual and continuous), not categorical; the social racial categories we use carve up that continuous variation in ways that reflect historical power relations, not biological fact. The version of "race realism" in the Fuentes/Webbon transcript is not drawing on population genetics — it's drawing on historical stereotypes that predate any genetic research. When Fuentes says race explains behavioral and cognitive differences, he is not citing research; he is presenting the assumption that this is so. Dixit's analysis shows that this rhetorical move — personal observation presented as verified fact — is characteristic of replacement ideology.

**"Isn't there something to the argument that everyone uses group statistics to make decisions?"**

This is Fuentes's "prejudgment" argument and it has a surface plausibility worth taking seriously before dismantling. Three problems: First, the claim that racial stereotypes accurately predict individual behavior is empirically contested — the variance within racial groups is consistently larger than variance between them for virtually any psychological or behavioral characteristic. Second, even if group-level statistics were accurate, applying them to individuals is an ecological inference problem: you can't infer individual characteristics from group averages. Third, the specific stereotypes in use (Black people are "lazy and impulsive," Jews are "subversive") are not derived from disinterested statistical observation but from a tradition of racist dehumanization. The "prejudgment" framing presents these as neutrally derived from experience; Dixit shows they are inherited from a long history of white supremacist ideology.

**"Is Fuentes actually far-right? He seems pretty moderate in this interview."**

This is exactly the Hellström analytical question, and it's productive. Push students to use the typology precisely: Fuentes is not claiming biological supremacism (Framework 1); he's making cultural-incompatibility and behavioral-generalization claims that pass Framework 1's definitional test. Hellström's point is that this apparent moderation is a feature, not a bug — neo-racism is designed to be hard to classify as racism under Framework 1. The question "is he far-right?" recapitulates the definitional choice. Under Framework 1, maybe not. Under Framework 2, clearly yes. What does the definition choice reveal about who decides what counts as racism and on what grounds?

**"Why would anyone believe the great replacement? It seems obviously paranoid."**

Dixit's answer is important: the conspiracy is believable not because the empirical claims are correct but because the *narrative structure* is compelling. Red-pilling offers urgency (you are being deceived right now), community (the awakened can see what others can't), and purpose (your awakening calls for action). These are powerful motivational structures that don't depend on the underlying claims being true. Roof's self-radicalization via Google also shows the role of information ecosystems: when the first search results for "black on white crime" are white nationalist websites, the algorithmic environment does the radicalization work.

**"Isn't there something hypocritical about Fuentes being Mexican-American and promoting white nationalist ideas?"**

This is a tempting question but a distraction from the analytical task. More productive: Blee & Yates note that "people who appear nonwhite can be considered white if they support white supremacism." Fuentes himself said religion trumps race for him; he's Catholic, which positions him within the white Christian civilizational project. His America First movement has made explicit choices about whose membership it welcomes. The question of internal consistency is less analytically interesting than the question of what cultural and ideological work his participation does in the movement.

### Connections Forward

**To Week 7 (gender):** Dixit's three archetypes show gender and race in productive tension. Rodger's replacement logic is gendered — women are the replacers, men are the replaced. Roof's is racialized — Black men are the threat; white women are rescuable victims. Crusius's combines both: Hispanic "invaders" threaten the white homeland, and guns are the means of defense. The gender-race intersection in replacement ideology is not incidental but structural: white women's reproductive capacity is the object of protection in virtually all these narratives, which connects directly to Brady's (7B) pronatalist analysis.

**To Blee 2012 (7A):** The organizational-learning finding (carried in the instructor note, originally Blee & Yates 2015) echoes and extends the belief-driven activism critique from Blee (2012). The mechanism Blee identified in individual joining — action before ideology — appears at the aggregate level in movement socialization: movements don't just attract people who already hold racist beliefs; they produce racist beliefs through participation. Hellström's Framework 2 provides the ideological content of that production process: what members learn is not "we are biologically superior" (Framework 1) but "our culture is threatened and we have the right to defend it" (Framework 2) — a frame that can be absorbed without the psychological cost of identifying as a white supremacist.

**To Valentim (5A):** Dixit's red-pilling concept raises a question Valentim's normalization framework doesn't fully address. The two mechanisms probably coexist but require different explanations and different counter-strategies.

**To Koopmans (6A):** Dixit's analysis of Roof's self-radicalization via Google adds an important dimension to Koopmans's political opportunity structure framework. Koopmans asked about the institutional opportunities that enable far-right mobilization. Dixit's implicit answer is that information environments — specifically, the algorithmic ranking of white nationalist content in search results — constitute a new kind of opportunity structure that doesn't require political allies or institutional openings. **To Corredor (7B):** The biblical theological framing in the Fuentes/Webbon conversation parallels Corredor's analysis of the Catholic Church's role in the antigender countermovement. **To Week 9 (Wojczewski) and course conclusion:** This is the most direct forward connection. That it is neo-racism under Hellström's analysis, not non-racism, is the key analytical move. The Week 9B passage-bridging activity for Group A explicitly connects these two papers; by the time they get there, students should have the vocabulary from 8A to do the analytical work with precision.

## Session 8B: Why Does Immigration Fuel Far-Right Support?

**Focus:** Session 8A asked what far-right racial ideology *is* — how the great replacement conspiracy works, how race is constructed in conservative and extremist movements. This session asks a different question: *what are the mechanisms by which immigration produces far-right electoral gains?* The three readings approach this from complementary angles. Halikiopoulou & Vlandas (2020) argue that far-right success requires mobilizing a coalition of culturally and economically concerned voters, and that existing research underestimates the economic side of this coalition. Magni (2022) shows that welfare chauvinism — the exclusion of immigrants from social solidarity — is not a far-right niche but a hidden consensus across the left-right spectrum. Dinas et al. (2019) provide causal evidence from a natural experiment in Greece: exposure to the refugee crisis increased Golden Dawn vote shares, even without sustained contact or resource competition. Together, the papers force the question: exposure matters, but *why*? Which mechanisms are doing the work?

The Lauren Southern video situates these papers in a concrete media environment. Southern is on Lesvos, framing refugee rescue NGOs as criminal trafficking operations, in the same period and location Dinas studies. She is supplying the discursive frame through which "exposure" gets converted into political response. The papers measure the outcome; the video illustrates one mechanism by which the conversion happens.

### Opening Hook (8 min): The Greek Islands

Present the following scenario without naming the study:

> It's summer 2015. You live on a Greek Aegean island near the Turkish coast. Between May and September, more than 200,000 Syrian refugees passed through your island — roughly 2.5 refugees for every island resident. Most were housed in temporary facilities and left within 48 hours to continue to Athens. There was no sustained contact, no significant resource competition; the refugees were effectively in transit. In September 2015, Greece held a national election.

Ask students to write for 60 seconds: *What do you predict happened to the vote share of Golden Dawn, the neo-fascist party, in your island versus a comparable island that received no refugees?*

Take a few answers. Then reveal: vote shares for Golden Dawn increased by about 2 percentage points — a 44 percent increase at the average. Then ask: *why?* Note that neither sustained contact nor resource competition was a factor. What is the mechanism?

Hold the question open — the session's readings are three different answers.

### Mini-Lecture 1: Halikiopoulou & Vlandas — The Coalition Theory of Far-Right Success (18 min)

**The paper's intervention.**

Halikiopoulou and Vlandas (2020) challenge a growing consensus in the comparative politics literature: that far-right party success is primarily driven by *cultural* concerns about immigration — a "cultural backlash" against multiculturalism, cosmopolitanism, and the perceived erosion of national identity. They do not dispute that individual culturalists (voters who dislike the cultural impact of immigration) are statistically more likely to vote far-right than individual materialists (voters who dislike the economic impact of immigration). What they dispute is whether individual-level predictive power translates into national-level importance. Their argument: it doesn't necessarily, and the economic side of the anti-immigration coalition is systematically underweighted.

**The core distinction: core vs. peripheral voters.**

Far-right parties have a secure core constituency: "culturalists," voters primarily concerned with immigration's threat to national identity, cultural cohesion, and ethnic composition. These voters identify with all elements of the nationalist platform — nativism, Euroscepticism, welfare chauvinism. They see far-right parties as their natural home. Their support is principled and not very contingent.

But core constituencies alone cannot win elections. Far-right parties also need peripheral voters, whom Halikiopoulou and Vlandas call "materialists": voters primarily concerned with immigration's economic impact — on wages, on jobs, on public services. These voters don't necessarily identify with the full far-right nationalist platform; they might be served by other parties as well. Their support for the far right is more contingent, activated by situational triggers that make economic concerns over immigration salient.

**The coalition arithmetic.**

The key analytical move is the distinction between individual-level predictive power and aggregate-level importance. Halikiopoulou and Vlandas offer a crisp illustration: suppose culturalists have a 50% probability of voting far-right and materialists have a 10% probability. In a statistical sense, culturalists have much stronger "predictive power." But if materialists outnumber culturalists ten to one, then materialists contribute more total votes to the far right. Inferring aggregate importance from individual predictive power is what they call the "atomistic fallacy" — a reverse ecological fallacy in which individual-level dynamics are incorrectly scaled up to national-level conclusions.

The ESS data across 19 European countries and eight rounds confirms this: there are consistently more respondents with economic concerns over immigration than with cultural concerns. And while culturalists vote far-right at higher rates, materialists are numerous enough to be the decisive group in many countries' far-right electorates. Cross-national regressions show that the individual-level predictive power of *economic* concerns over immigration is significantly correlated with national far-right party vote shares, while cultural concerns' predictive power is not — an important asymmetry.

**The supply-side implication: welfare chauvinism as the bridge.**

Why are some far-right parties better at mobilizing materialists? Halikiopoulou and Vlandas point to a supply-side shift: many far-right parties have moved from predominantly ethnic-nativist platforms (which appeal mainly to principled culturalists) toward welfare chauvinism — the position that welfare benefits should accrue to native citizens and be denied to immigrants. This is a form of economic nationalism that speaks directly to materialist concerns about competition over public services without requiring voters to endorse the full far-right ideological package.

Welfare chauvinism is therefore analytically important not just as a policy position but as a political bridge: it allows far-right parties to mobilize voters whose primary concern is economic without requiring them to be principled xenophobes.

**Applying to Greece.**

The Halikiopoulou framework suggests one mechanism for the Dinas finding: the 2015 refugee crisis functioned as a situational trigger that activated materialist concerns about immigration's economic and service impacts, converting otherwise-contingent materialists into far-right votes. The sudden influx of refugees — visible, politically salient, occurring simultaneously with Greece's financial crisis and austerity — would have exacerbated socio-tropic economic anxiety about immigration precisely among the peripheral voters far-right parties most need to mobilize.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Magni — Welfare Chauvinism as Hidden Consensus (15 min)

**The paper's intervention.**

Magni (2022) provides the demand-side microfoundations for what Halikiopoulou and Vlandas call "welfare chauvinism." Using conjoint experiments across the US, UK, France, and Italy, Magni asks: who is seen as deserving of welfare support, and what conditions reduce discrimination against immigrants? The answer has two surprising components: welfare chauvinism is both more severe and more widespread than existing research suggested.

**All immigrants face welfare penalties — including Western ones.**

Conjoint design presents respondents with pairs of unemployed individuals and asks which is more deserving of welfare support. Multiple characteristics are randomized: nationality, work history, work attitude, health, education, family situation, religion. This allows Magni to isolate the effect of each attribute.

The central finding: being an immigrant is one of the strongest predictors of welfare disadvantage. Native citizens are chosen as more deserving in 59-65% of cases across the four countries. And crucially, the nationality effect does not diminish much for Western immigrants. In the US, Canadian immigrants face an 11-point welfare penalty relative to American citizens — barely smaller than the 13-point penalty for Mexican and Indian immigrants. In France and the UK, the penalty for German immigrants is not much smaller than the penalty for Moroccan and Romanian immigrants. When it comes to welfare (unlike immigration admission policy, where cultural proximity matters significantly), the fundamental divide is native vs. immigrant *tout court*.

This is the **hidden consensus**: even immigrants from countries that nativists would otherwise regard as culturally compatible are severely penalized. The architecture of the welfare state — historically built within the bounded community of the nation-state — has created a deep-rooted identification of welfare with citizenship. Immigrants, as non-citizens, are structurally positioned as outsiders to the welfare community regardless of their cultural characteristics.

**The penalty is spread across demographics.**

If welfare chauvinism were concentrated among economically anxious, low-education, conservative voters, it would be a political fringe. Magni's subgroup analysis shows it is not. Progressive respondents, economically secure respondents, younger respondents, and college-educated respondents all strongly penalize immigrants. The differences across groups are real but modest. Even among the most immigration-friendly demographic subgroups, natives are selected as more deserving of welfare in well over half of cases.

This is what Magni calls the "hidden consensus" — echoing Hainmueller and Hopkins's (2015) finding of a similar consensus on immigration admission preferences. The welfare chauvinism that far-right parties explicitly endorse is not an extremist departure from mainstream opinion; it is closer to the revealed preference of the general population, including voters who would not self-identify as anti-immigrant.

**What can reduce the gap? Only work history.**

Magni tests four factors that should boost immigrants' perceived deservingness: work history, proactive work attitude, justifiable reason for unemployment (health/injury), and education. The only factor that reliably reduces the immigrant welfare penalty is a *long work history*. In the US and UK, immigrants who worked for 30 years face about 7-8 points of penalty rather than 14-17 — a meaningful reduction, though immigrants are still penalized. In France and Italy, the effect is smaller.

The mechanism: work history provides evidence of *reciprocity* — the immigrant has paid into the system that would now support them. This partially bridges the citizen/non-citizen divide by demonstrating past membership in the economic community.

Other factors often *backfire*. Immigrants actively looking for work face *larger* penalties in some countries (UK), presumably because they're seen as job competitors. Immigrants with health problems are penalized more in the US and UK, likely because they're seen as potential fiscal burdens. Education plays a minimal role. The deservingness criteria that help natives don't help immigrants in the same way.

**Applying to Greece.**

Magni's finding suggests a second mechanism for the Dinas result: far-right parties can capitalize on latent welfare chauvinism that is not concentrated among their ideological base but diffused across the population. When refugee arrivals make immigration suddenly visible and politically salient on the Greek islands, they activate a welfare chauvinism that was already present as a latent preference — particularly among the materialist peripheral voters who are the decisive electoral group in Halikiopoulou and Vlandas's framework.

### Discussion Activity (15 min): Evidence Assembly

**Format:** This is a new format — *evidence assembly* — requiring students to reason from evidence to mechanism, rather than applying a given theory to cases. Students have seen the Dinas finding (exposure → far-right support) and now have three potential mechanisms from the papers. Their task is to evaluate how well each mechanism accounts for the evidence, and to identify what each mechanism leaves unexplained.

**Setup:** Four groups, each assigned one mechanism to represent and defend. Groups have 8 minutes to assemble evidence from the readings; then a 7-minute structured debrief in which each group presents and fields challenges.

**Mechanism A — Cultural threat activation (Halikiopoulou's culturalists):** The arrival of 200,000 refugees activated cultural concerns among voters who were already culturally anxious about immigration. The sudden visibility of Muslim asylum seekers in Greek island communities triggered ethno-cultural threat perceptions and drove core culturalist voters to Golden Dawn.

*Evidence to assemble:* Halikiopoulou's finding that cultural concerns are the strongest individual-level predictor; the refugee crisis making immigration "salient within the context of the transnational cleavage"; the ethnic and cultural profile of Syrian refugees (majority Muslim, non-European).

*Evidence against / gap:* This mechanism predicts that the effect should be concentrated among pre-existing culturalists. But Dinas shows the effect also came from new voter mobilization and vote-switching from center-right Nea Dimokratia — not a party associated with cultural xenophobia. Halikiopoulou's own argument is that cultural concerns alone can't explain aggregate success.

**Mechanism B — Economic anxiety activation (Halikiopoulou's materialists):** The refugee crisis, concurrent with Greece's financial crisis, activated economic concerns among peripheral voters who worried about the fiscal and labor-market burden of sudden refugee inflows.

*Evidence to assemble:* Halikiopoulou's finding that materialists are numerically more important than culturalists; that situational triggers exacerbate socio-tropic concerns; that the financial crisis context would have maximized anxiety about public resource allocation.

*Evidence against / gap:* Dinas shows the effect occurred even though the refugees were in transit for less than 48 hours — they posed no actual economic burden. Halikiopoulou's mechanism predicts that economically anxious regions should show stronger effects, but Dinas's design is about *proximity to arrivals*, not local economic conditions.

**Mechanism C — Welfare chauvinism activation (Magni):** The refugee arrivals made the native-vs.-immigrant divide salient, activating latent welfare chauvinism that was already present as a hidden consensus. Voters who didn't think of themselves as anti-immigrant nonetheless moved toward the party most explicitly committed to keeping refugees out of the welfare community.

*Evidence to assemble:* Magni's finding that welfare chauvinism is widespread across ideological groups; that in the absence of information, respondents default to negative assumptions about immigrants; that even progressive and educated voters penalize immigrants.

*Evidence against / gap:* Magni's mechanism would predict effects even on islands without refugee arrivals, since the latent attitude is already there. Dinas's design shows that *differential exposure* explains the effect — not just the general political environment of 2015. Something about the local presence of refugees specifically matters.

**Mechanism D — Media framing (not directly in the papers):** The conversion of refugee exposure into far-right votes required a media environment that constructed refugees as threatening. Without a frame that interpreted refugee arrivals as dangerous, voters might have responded with humanitarian concern rather than electoral backlash.

*Evidence to assemble:* The Lauren Southern video — active construction of the NGO-as-trafficker frame in this exact location and period. Dixit (8A) on how information environments structure political responses. Dinas's own note that the effect was "relatively modest" given the scale of arrivals — perhaps because media framing was not uniform.

*Evidence against / gap:* This mechanism requires evidence about what media environment Greek island voters were actually exposed to. The papers don't measure this. The mechanism is plausible but underspecified.

**What to listen for in debrief:**

Push students to be precise about what *would* distinguish these mechanisms empirically. Mechanism A predicts effects concentrated among prior far-right sympathizers; Mechanism B predicts effects concentrated in more economically stressed areas; Mechanism C predicts effects diffuse across the ideological spectrum; Mechanism D predicts effects moderated by media consumption. Dinas's design can't distinguish among these because it measures only aggregate vote shares.

The most important learning objective is not to declare a winner but to understand that the same observable outcome (increased far-right voting) can be consistent with multiple underlying mechanisms, and that distinguishing them requires different data and designs. This is what motivates the broader research agenda all three papers point toward.

### Closing Question (7 min): What the Papers Don't Explain

*Prompt:*

### Methods Notes

**Halikiopoulou & Vlandas:**

Multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression on eight waves of ESS data covering 19 European countries and 31 far-right parties. The key analytical innovation is a multi-level analysis: individual-level regressions to estimate predictive power of cultural and economic concerns, then a second stage that regresses national-level far-right vote shares on those individual-level coefficients. The simulations (varying the distribution of economic and cultural concerns hypothetically) are useful to show students what it would take to establish causal importance vs. statistical association.

The central methodological contribution — naming and illustrating the atomistic fallacy — is worth spending time on. Students trained in regression may assume that a coefficient's size tells them how important a variable is in the world; Halikiopoulou and Vlandas show that this only holds within a level of analysis, not across levels. The paper provides an excellent teaching moment about multi-level inference.

**Magni:**

Conjoint survey experiment — one of the increasingly standard methods in political behavior research for recovering causal effects of multiple attributes simultaneously. The design asks respondents to make forced choices between pairs of profiles whose characteristics are randomized, allowing estimation of Average Marginal Component Effects (AMCEs) — the effect of each attribute, averaging over the distribution of all other attributes. The forced-choice design (must pick one of the two profiles) avoids the acquiescence bias of rating-scale questions.

Worth flagging the limitation Magni acknowledges: the conjoint does not vary immigrant legal status. This means the welfare penalties observed could partly reflect concerns about illegality rather than immigration per se. Magni argues this is unlikely to fully explain the findings (EU immigrants who are legal face similar penalties) but it's a genuine inferential limitation.

The timing observation in the conclusion is also pedagogically useful: Magni's surveys were conducted 2016-2018, after Brexit, Trump, and the refugee crisis had raised immigration's salience. A 2014 study (Kootstra 2016) found that clear effort eliminated the immigrant welfare gap — Magni finds it doesn't. The implication: the political environment shapes whether and when immigrants can overcome the otherness attribution, which means the papers' findings are not timeless but historically situated.

**Dinas et al.:**

Natural experiment using geographic distance to the Turkish coast as a source of quasi-random variation in refugee exposure across Greek islands. Two complementary identification strategies: (1) difference-in-differences comparing vote shares in exposed vs. unexposed islands before and after the 2015 refugee crisis, and (2) instrumental variables using logged distance to Turkish coast as an instrument for refugee arrivals. Both converge on the same estimate (~2 pp increase in GD vote share). The placebo tests — showing that distance to Turkey has no effect on GD vote shares in pre-crisis elections, and that non-resident voters registered on affected islands show no behavioral change — are excellent examples of how good natural experiments validate their own identification assumptions.

The study is an 11-page article in *Political Analysis*, so it's methodologically rigorous in a relatively compact form. Students should note the honest discussion of interpretive limits: "we interpret the 2 percentage point increase in GD vote shares as a relatively modest effect," given the scale of the crisis. The authors do not oversell the finding.

### Anticipated Student Moves

**"Doesn't this mean anti-immigration attitudes are basically just prejudice? The Magni paper shows that even facts about economic contributions don't move opinions."**

Magni's finding is more specific: facts about future work attitude and current health status don't reliably reduce welfare penalties; only past work *history* — which establishes a reciprocity claim — partially does. This suggests it's not pure outgroup hostility (which would be impervious to any information) but a structural feature of how welfare deservingness is cognitively organized. The welfare state developed within nation-state boundaries, and citizenship became the primary criterion for membership in the welfare community. Immigrants who can demonstrate they've already been members of the economic community (long work history) can partially overcome that structural disadvantage. This is not "prejudice" in the simple sense — it's an institutional legacy that shapes cognition about welfare.

**"Couldn't the Greek result just be explained by the financial crisis? Greece was economically devastated in 2015. Wouldn't any political shock have moved people toward the extremes?"**

This is a good methodological intuition — Dinas addresses it directly. The design controls for this by comparing islands within Greece, all subject to the same national economic context, that differ only in whether they received refugees. The placebo test using the January 2015 election (before significant refugee arrivals) shows that proximity to Turkey had no effect on GD support then — so it's not simply that islands closer to Turkey are systematically different in ways that predict far-right support. The refugee arrivals specifically, not the financial crisis generally, drove the September 2015 increase.

**"Halikiopoulou says economic concerns matter, but Magni says even rich, educated, progressive people are welfare chauvinist. Don't these papers contradict each other?"**

They're actually complementary. Halikiopoulou is analyzing what drives far-right *voting*, not welfare attitudes per se. She shows that among people who have anti-immigration concerns, the economically motivated ones are numerically more important to far-right party success. Magni is analyzing welfare *attitudes* more broadly. His finding that welfare chauvinism is widespread actually supports Halikiopoulou's theory: if even progressives and the educated are welfare chauvinist, then the pool of potential "materialist" voters that far-right parties can activate is larger than a purely economic competition story would suggest. It's not just the economically anxious; it's also the ideologically moderate who have welfare chauvinist instincts they may not act on politically until a trigger event makes immigration salient.

**"If the Southern video is framing NGO aid workers as traffickers, isn't it just wrong? Why spend time on it?"**

This is the "is it true?" question, and it's worth addressing head-on. The ERCI founder Panos denied all allegations in his on-camera interview with Southern. The charges included money laundering, but his lawyer's comment ("500,000 euros is nothing compared to what other NGOs do") is ambiguous and being interpreted through Southern's conspiratorial frame. The empirical question of what ERCI did or didn't do is not what the video is analytically interesting for. What's analytically interesting is the *rhetorical construction* — how a humanitarian rescue operation gets reframed as trafficking through the video's narrative structure. This is the same kind of analysis Dixit (8A) applied to far-right manifestos: not asking whether the claims are true but asking what rhetorical *work* the framing does. Southern's video is doing the supply-side work that Halikiopoulou's framework points toward as the mechanism by which far-right parties activate materialist concerns — creating a frame in which NGO aid = organized crime = immigration = threat to ordinary Greek citizens.

### Connections Forward

**To Week 9 (course conclusion):** The three papers this session all point to what political scientists have called the "immigration-welfare state dilemma": diverse societies face a tradeoff between immigration and solidarity, because the social trust that sustains generous welfare states may erode when the welfare community becomes more heterogeneous. Week 9 should be positioned to ask: where does this leave us? If the coalition theory (Halikiopoulou), the hidden consensus (Magni), and the exposure effects (Dinas) are all correct, what are the political implications? Is far-right electoral success on immigration a problem for which democratic institutions have a solution?

**To Halikiopoulou's own framework ↔ Valentim (5A):** Valentim's normalization argument explains how far-right parties *expand* their electorate over time as social stigma around far-right voting declines. Halikiopoulou's coalition argument explains the *cross-sectional* structure of that electorate: culturalists as the stable core, materialists as the expandable periphery. These frameworks are complements: as normalization reduces the stigma cost of voting far-right, the pool of activatable materialists grows. The interaction between supply-side legitimation (normalization) and demand-side coalition structure is an important theoretical integration.

**To Magni ↔ Hellström (8A):** Magni's finding that welfare chauvinism is widespread among progressive voters maps directly onto Hellström's Framework 2. Respondents who penalize immigrants in welfare allocation are not necessarily endorsing explicit racial hierarchy (Framework 1); they are acting on a native/immigrant incompatibility logic — a sense that welfare community membership belongs to citizens, and that immigrants are structural outsiders regardless of cultural proximity. This is Framework 2 operating at the level of revealed preference rather than stated ideology. The "hidden consensus" Magni identifies is the demand-side substrate on which ethnopluralist welfare politics operates — not biological racism, but a bounded-solidarity logic that produces racially exclusionary outcomes without requiring racially explicit motivation.

**To Dinas ↔ Koopmans (6A):** Koopmans's political opportunity structure framework asked about the institutional conditions that enable far-right mobilization. Dinas provides a complementary mechanism: issue salience created by an exogenous event (the refugee crisis) temporarily transforms the political opportunity structure even without institutional change. The refugee crisis didn't change Greek electoral law, party systems, or the strength of cultural traditions of tolerance — it just made immigration suddenly, viscerally salient in ways that activated latent anti-immigrant sentiment. This is what Koopmans would call a shift in "discursive opportunity" — the public framing suddenly became favorable to far-right mobilization.

**To the Southern video ↔ Corredor (7B):** Corredor analyzed how far-right actors *constructed* the "gender ideology" frame as an artifact that mobilized antigender sentiment. Southern's video is doing exactly the same thing in the immigration domain: constructing a frame ("NGO smugglers") that converts humanitarian rescue operations into a threatening conspiracy. The mechanism is identical — a political entrepreneur creates a narrative that activates diffuse public anxiety (about borders, immigration, trafficking) and directs it toward a specific political target (NGOs, then by extension immigration generally). The Corredor analysis gives students the analytical vocabulary to name what Southern is doing without simply dismissing it as propaganda.

**To Brady (7B):** Magni's finding that immigrants who are out of work for health reasons face larger welfare penalties (because they're seen as fiscal burdens) connects to Brady's pronatalism. Brady's argument is that far-right reproductive politics are partly driven by concern about who will reproduce the welfare-state community. If the welfare community is bounded by citizenship, and citizenship is increasingly associated with nativity, then demographic anxiety about the "wrong" people reproducing (immigrants, in this framing) becomes instrumentally connected to welfare politics. The logical connection: if immigrants are seen as net drains on welfare, then policy favoring native births over immigration is the supply-side solution to demographic decline — which is precisely the pronatalist logic Brady analyzes.
