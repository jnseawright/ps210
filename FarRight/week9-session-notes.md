# Week 9 Session Notes: Transnational Dimensions of the Far Right

**Readings:**
- Part 1 (Session 9A): Roumanias, Rori & Georgiadou (2022); Turnbull-Dugarte & Rama (2022); Heft et al. (2021)
- Part 2 (Session 9B): Wojczewski (2026); Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak (2023); Giani & Méon (2021)

**Syllabus framing question:** Is far-right politics an inherently national phenomenon — driven by domestic economic conditions, cultural anxieties, and institutional opportunities — or is it a transnational movement whose spread across borders requires transnational explanation?

---

## Session 9A: Contagion, Viability, and the Transnational Information Ecology

**Focus:** The preceding eight weeks have addressed the domestic politics of the far right: why individuals vote for far-right parties, how movements recruit and retain members, what ideologies animate them, and how gender and race structure movement participation. These explanations are essentially national in scope — they take the political opportunity structures, cultural values, and economic conditions of individual countries as their explanatory variables. This session asks whether that framework is complete. If far-right parties across countries share ideological platforms, institutional networks, and media ecosystems — and if the electoral success of one can influence the electoral prospects of others — then domestic explanations are incomplete by definition. Transnational dynamics must be part of the story.

Three papers build different pieces of this argument. Roumanias et al. (2022) propose an integrated framework for understanding transnational political contagion and provide panel evidence across European countries. Turnbull-Dugarte and Rama (2022) provide causal evidence of a specific contagion event — Trump's 2020 electoral defeat reducing support for Spain's VOX. Heft et al. (2021) examine the media infrastructure through which transnational far-right networks operate: the hyperlink networks connecting right-wing digital news sites in six Western democracies.

The Alex Jones/Eduardo Bolsonaro CPAC video is a primary source that sits at the intersection of all three papers. Jones's InfoWars is exactly the kind of US-based right-wing news site that Heft finds functioning as a hub for European connections. CPAC is the same institutional network through which VOX leaders connected to Trump strategists. And Bolsonaro/Trump/Farage are mentioned in the same breath by Jones as constituting a global nationalist movement — the demand-side coalition that Turnbull-Dugarte and Roumanias analyze quantitatively.

### Mini-Lecture 1: Roumanias et al. — An Integrated Framework of Far-Right Contagion (20 min)

**The paper's intervention.**

Roumanias, Rori & Georgiadou (2022) argue that existing explanations of far-right party success are essentially national in scope — they explain why voters in a particular country support the far right given the conditions in that country. This is insufficient if far-right parties across countries are interdependent: if what happens to the French National Rally affects the Swedish Democrats, and what happens to the German AfD affects the Italian Brothers of Italy, then nationally-bounded explanations will miss important variation. The paper proposes an integrated framework for understanding these transnational dynamics and tests it against a regional panel dataset of 7,652 far-right electoral outcomes from 2000 to 2017.

**The framework's five pillars.**

The paper organizes its theoretical contribution around five concepts:

*Domains* — the dimensions along which contagion can occur. The political domain distinguishes between demand (why voters support far-right parties) and supply (how far-right parties position themselves). The territorial domain distinguishes between what happens in foreign countries and what happens domestically. The socioeconomic domain captures contextual factors like immigration levels and economic conditions that can interact with both political and territorial dynamics. This generates a 2×2 matrix of possible contagion channels: foreign supply can affect domestic supply (parties copy each other); foreign supply can affect domestic demand (foreign party success signals viability to domestic voters); foreign demand can affect domestic demand (successful mobilization abroad inspires mobilization at home); and contextual factors abroad can trigger responses at home.

*Pathways* — the mechanisms through which contagion travels. The paper distinguishes between social learning (parties observe what works elsewhere and adapt), emulation (parties copy successful strategies), and signaling (electoral outcomes abroad provide heuristics about what voters want domestically).

*Neighborhoods* — proximity conditions that facilitate or inhibit contagion. Geographical proximity matters: countries sharing borders or in close spatial proximity are more likely to experience contagion than countries separated by distance. Cultural proximity also matters: shared language, religion, and historical ties create "similar frames of reference" that make political developments in one country legible and relevant to voters and parties in another. The distinction between geographical and cultural proximity is important: it implies that a Brazilian nationalist movement (culturally proximate to Iberian politics) and a Spanish far-right party may be more susceptible to mutual influence than geographically closer but culturally distinct countries.

*Mechanisms* — the operational processes through which contagion operates. The paper distinguishes between fear-based short-run responses and learning-based long-run responses, which leads to its key empirical finding.

*Mediums* — the channels through which contagion travels, including media, party networks, institutional connections, and information flows.

**The key empirical findings.**

The paper produces four main results from its panel analysis:

*Short-run fear effect followed by long-run emulation.* In the short run (controlling for foreign contextual factors like immigration), domestic far-right vote shares respond negatively to foreign far-right gains. The intuition: when the far right rises abroad, it tends to be because immigration or economic conditions have created anxiety that is then visible to neighboring countries, which activates similar anxieties domestically through a *fear* mechanism. But if the foreign far-right vote itself rises — independent of those contextual triggers — it initially discourages domestic far-right support (perhaps because it seems threatening or excessive). Over time, however, this reverses: 4-5 years after an unexpected shock in a foreign far-right vote share, the domestic far-right vote share increases. The mechanism shifts from fear to *emulation* — parties and voters learn that far-right mobilization can be electorally successful and adapt accordingly.

*Supply-side substitution effect.* When foreign far-right parties adopt successful extreme positions, domestic far-right parties can *relax* their own extreme stances — not abandon them, but moderate their expression. The mechanism: successful foreign far-right positions function as a destigmatization signal, making anti-immigrant politics more mainstream. This allows the domestic party to pursue the same anti-immigrant voter pool while appearing less extreme, broadening their appeal.

*Contextual triggers.* Immigration increases abroad function as a signal for immigration pressures at home, activating anti-immigrant sentiment even before immigrants actually arrive domestically. Economic asymmetries between countries — particularly wage differentials that create migration pressure — increase far-right support in destination countries.

*Policy implications.* Because spillovers create voting externalities, national counter-strategies are insufficient by themselves. A country that successfully reduces far-right support domestically may see those gains eroded by spillovers from neighboring countries. This implies the need for European-level (or at minimum coordinated multi-country) responses.

**The Jones/Bolsonaro video as illustration.**

The CPAC interview is an illustration of the *supply-side* aspect of Roumanias's framework. Jones is broadcasting to a US audience; Eduardo Bolsonaro is speaking to both the US audience and (through social media) a Brazilian and global audience. Both are participating in an institutional network (CPAC) that explicitly coordinates across national right-wing parties — the same network that connected VOX leaders to Trump strategists. This is the transnational supply-side infrastructure through which party strategies, rhetorical frames, and ideological innovations diffuse across national boundaries.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Turnbull-Dugarte & Rama — The Titanic Effect (12 min)

**The paper's intervention.**

While Roumanias et al. establish the general pattern of transnational far-right contagion using observational panel data, Turnbull-Dugarte and Rama (2022) provide *causal* evidence of a specific contagion event. Their design exploits the coincidental timing of Trump's November 2020 electoral defeat with the fieldwork period for Spain's monthly CIS barometer survey. Respondents interviewed before November 7, 2020 (when the result was announced) and those interviewed after are compared — an as-good-as-random natural experiment.

**The mechanism: bandwagon and titanic effects.**

Domestic elections feature what researchers call *bandwagon effects* — voters update their party preferences based on signals of a party's viability and popularity. When a party is doing well (in polls, in elections), sympathizers become more willing to express support publicly; when a party is declining, sympathizers may distance themselves to avoid backing a loser. The theoretical contribution of Turnbull-Dugarte and Rama is to argue that these viability signals cross national borders. An ideologically symmetrical far-right party's victory abroad signals that the domestic party is viable; a defeat abroad signals it is not. The *titanic effect* — the negative counterpart of the bandwagon — can operate in international waters.

**The Spain-US connection.**

Spain makes a "most likely" case for detecting these effects because VOX has been unusually explicit about its relationship with Trump. The party's debut slogan was "Make Spain Great Again." It sent delegates to CPAC at the American Conservative Union Foundation's expense. Steve Bannon formed links with VOX's leadership and communications team. VOX's voters were particularly sympathetic to Trump compared to even other European far-right electorates. If the titanic effect operates anywhere in the European context, Spain is where it should be most detectable.

**The results.**

Respondents interviewed after Trump's defeat were 3-6 percentage points less likely to express support for VOX and significantly less approving of VOX leader Santiago Abascal (a 17% fall relative to the control group mean). The conditional analysis shows the effect is concentrated where theory predicts it: among former right-wing voters (11 pp decline) rather than former left-wing voters (negligible effect). The Titanic effect hypothesis is supported; the Backlash hypothesis (defeat energizes supporters) is not.

A methodological note worth raising in class: the results measure *self-reported* voting intentions, not actual voting behavior. One alternative explanation is that Trump's defeat made social desirability concerns temporarily more salient, reducing willingness to report VOX support rather than actually changing preferences. The authors argue against this (the balance check shows equal past VOX vote recall across conditions), but the design cannot definitively distinguish between preference change and reporting change.

**What the titanic effect implies.**

If the viability signal mechanism is correct, it implies that the electoral fortunes of far-right parties in prominent countries function as a public good or bad for the transnational movement. Trump's 2016 victory was a positive viability signal for the international far-right; his 2020 defeat was a negative one. The Jones/Bolsonaro interview, filmed at CPAC while Trump was still in office, is essentially an exercise in positive signal amplification: Jones is explicitly broadcasting to his audience that the global far-right wave is strong, growing, and internationally connected. This is the information environment that the titanic effect's mechanism depends on — voters who believe the movement is strong are more likely to express support; the signal can travel transnationally because the media infrastructure makes it available globally.

### Mini-Lecture 3: Heft et al. — The Transnational Information Ecology (12 min)

**The paper's intervention.**

Heft et al. (2021) ask a different question: through what media infrastructure do transnational far-right connections actually operate? They analyze hyperlink networks among 65 right-wing news sites (RNS) in six countries — the US, UK, Germany, Austria, Sweden, and Denmark — asking which sites link to which, within and across national borders. Hyperlinking, in their framework, is not a neutral technical practice but a *political strategy*: it creates alliances, amplifies shared positions, builds community identity, and signals credibility. The pattern of hyperlinks reveals the architecture of the transnational far-right information ecology.

**RNS as hybrid actors.**

The paper defines RNS as sites that describe themselves as alternatives to mainstream media, exhibit hyper-partisanship, and have a clear right-wing ideological stance. These sites occupy an unusual position: they are simultaneously media actors (producing and distributing news content) and political movement actors (building coalitions, amplifying political agendas). This hybrid nature shapes their linking behavior. Media actors link primarily for journalistic reasons — to cite sources, provide context, maintain credibility. Movement actors link for strategic reasons — to force multiply (draw attention to shared issues), build identity, and dismiss opponents. RNS do both, and the two logics can conflict (linking to a mainstream source for credibility vs. positioning yourself against the "liberal mainstream media").

**Key findings: domestic integration and transnational hubs.**

The pattern of hyperlinks varies substantially across countries. German and Swedish RNS link heavily to each other domestically — a pattern Heft et al. interpret as reflecting the *restrictive* domestic context in both countries, where far-right positions are more ostracized in mainstream politics and media. Building a dense domestic network compensates for marginalization: the sites collectively elevate each other's visibility and create a sense of integrated community. Danish RNS, by contrast, link more transnationally — perhaps because Denmark's domestic partisan news sphere is less developed, making integration into the broader transnational community more attractive.

The US is the decisive outlier. American RNS are both densely domestically integrated and function as a *hub* for transnational connections from European sites. European right-wing news sites link heavily to US sites, but not vice versa. Heft et al. interpret the US's position as reflecting its historical and cultural hegemony — US political events and media figures command attention from European audiences in ways that European events do not typically command from American audiences. The paper suggests the US right-wing news ecology might represent a *model* for what European right-wing media ecologies are evolving toward: a dense, integrated, internally reinforcing network.

**The mainstream media paradox.**

One of the paper's most counterintuitive findings concerns what counts as a "transnationally shared reference point" — the sites to which RNS across all six countries link. The top ten include: YouTube/Google, Twitter, Facebook, then predominantly *mainstream* media — BBC, Reuters, AP, CNN, New York Times — alongside some ideologically aligned outlets like Fox News, Washington Examiner, and the German Welt. Donald Trump's Twitter account ranks at number eight. Legacy mainstream media dominate the list.

This finding complicates the "echo chamber" narrative. RNS are not insulated from the mainstream information environment; they link heavily to mainstream media, presumably to cite, rebut, or reframe mainstream reporting for their audiences. Heft et al. call this "recontextualization and reframing" — taking mainstream-credentialed content and embedding it in an alternative interpretive frame. The Jones/Bolsonaro video is an excellent example: Jones's InfoWars regularly features clips from mainstream news, government documents, and official statements — not to accept the mainstream interpretation but to embed them in a conspiratorial counter-narrative. The paradox is that this practice simultaneously uses and undermines mainstream media credibility.

**The Jones/Bolsonaro video as instance of the network.**

InfoWars — Jones's platform — is precisely the kind of US-based RNS that Heft et al. find functioning as a transnational hub. The interview itself is an instance of the network in operation: a US media platform (InfoWars) hosts a representative of a foreign far-right political family (the Bolsonaros), filmed at a US institutional gathering (CPAC) with established links to European far-right parties (VOX via Bannon), to produce content that circulates on YouTube (a shared platform) and reaches both American and international audiences. The hyperlinks Heft et al. study are the digital traces of exactly this kind of transnational content-sharing and alliance-building.

### Discussion Activity (15 min): Cross-Framework Application

**Format:** Three-paper synthesis in assigned-reading groups. A new format — rather than testing a framework against data (as in previous activities), students are asked to *compare* frameworks against the same evidence. This requires a different kind of analytical thinking: not "does this theory explain this fact?" but "what does each theory uniquely add to our understanding?"

**Setup:** Three groups, each assigned one paper, given 8 minutes to address the same two questions about the Jones/Bolsonaro video. Then 7 minutes of structured debrief in which each group presents its answers.

**Question 1 for all groups:** What does your paper's framework *specifically* help explain about the Jones/Bolsonaro video? Be precise — identify a specific mechanism or finding from your paper that illuminates something about the video.

**Question 2 for all groups:** What does your paper *not* explain about the video, or what question does the video raise that your paper leaves unanswered?

**Group A — Roumanias et al.:** The integrated contagion framework asks about neighborhoods, pathways, and mechanisms. The video involves actors from Brazil and the US appearing at a US institutional venue. How does the Roumanias framework characterize the Brazil-US-Spain triangle in terms of cultural and geographical proximity? What does the framework predict about how Bolsonaro's rise would have affected European far-right parties? What does the long-run emulation vs. short-run fear distinction mean for understanding what Jones is doing when he celebrates the global nationalist wave?

*Evidence to emphasize:* Cultural proximity as a pathway of contagion (Brazil and Spain share Iberian cultural heritage and linguistic proximity); the supply-side substitution effect (Bolsonaro's success allows Spanish or European parties to cite a successful Latin American model while moderating their own rhetoric); the 4-5 year emulation peak (Bolsonaro was elected October 2018 — what does the framework predict about European contagion around 2022-2023?).

*What the framework leaves unanswered:* Roumanias uses electoral outcome data — it measures the aggregate effects of contagion but not the media mechanisms through which contagion travels. The video is in some ways *evidence of the mechanism*, not just an outcome to explain.

**Group B — Turnbull-Dugarte & Rama:** The titanic/bandwagon framework asks about viability signals and their cross-national effects. Jones's interview is explicitly a *positive viability signal* — he frames the global wave of nationalism as "getting stronger," lists multiple leaders, and tells Bolsonaro that "globalists are getting weaker but people need to know this is a total war." 

*Evidence to emphasize:* The interview functions as deliberate signal amplification — Jones is manufacturing and broadcasting the perception that the transnational far-right is thriving and viable, which (per the titanic/bandwagon mechanism) should increase sympathizers' willingness to express and act on their preferences. The conditional hypothesis (effects concentrated among former right-wing voters) maps onto the audience for InfoWars, which is primarily right-of-center. VOX's explicit Trump-mirroring strategy (Make Spain Great Again) shows that the viability signal was being deliberately manufactured on the supply side.

*What the framework leaves unanswered:* Turnbull-Dugarte focuses on a single discrete event (Trump's defeat). The Jones/Bolsonaro interview is a *continuous* signal-production activity — Jones regularly produces content celebrating the global far-right. Does the bandwagon/titanic mechanism apply to this kind of ongoing signal as well as to discrete electoral outcomes? And does the mechanism operate differently for audiences who distrust mainstream media coverage of electoral outcomes (as far-right audiences often do)?

**Group C — Heft et al.:** The hyperlink network framework asks about the architecture of the transnational information ecology and who functions as a hub. The video involves InfoWars (a US RNS hub in Heft's taxonomy), CPAC (an institutional network), and YouTube (a shared platform).

*Evidence to emphasize:* InfoWars is exactly the US hub that Heft et al. find European sites linking to; the interview produces the kind of content — prominent political figure, ideologically charged, global frame — that functions as a "transnationally shared reference point" for right-wing sites across borders; the CPAC venue represents an institutional layer underlying the digital network; Jones's mention of "independent channels" growing as they're "frenzied trying to control the internet" is a direct articulation of the RNS political strategy Heft describes (building alternative media as resistance to perceived mainstream suppression).

*What the framework leaves unanswered:* Heft et al. study 2018 hyperlink data — static snapshot of an evolving network. The video shows *people* and *institutions* networking in person, offline, at physical events. How do offline institutional networks (CPAC, European Parliament groupings, Bannon's network) relate to the digital hyperlink networks Heft measures? Are they causes, effects, or independent channels of the same transnational community-building?

**What to listen for in debrief:**

The synthesis question: the three papers study the same phenomenon (transnational far-right diffusion) at different levels of analysis and with different methods. Roumanias uses long-panel aggregate electoral data. Turnbull-Dugarte uses a natural experiment on survey data. Heft uses computational network analysis of hyperlinks. Each approach has different strengths and blind spots. What would you need to know that no single paper provides?

The metapedagogical point: this is an unusually good session for showing students that social science knowledge is cumulative and that different methods capture different aspects of the same underlying phenomenon. The CPAC interview exists in the middle of all three papers' empirical scope, but none of them could directly explain it on their own.

### Closing Question (7 min): What Does Transnationalism Mean for Counter-Strategies?

*Prompt:*

> Roumanias et al. end their paper with a blunt policy conclusion: because far-right support is contagious across borders and creates voting externalities, national-level counter-strategies are insufficient. European-level policy is needed to internalize the externalities. Is that conclusion right? And if it is, what would a European-level response to far-right contagion actually look like?

This question is deliberately broad — it's week 9, and you want students to bring the whole course to bear. Some threads to pull:

*The normalization literature (Valentim, 5A, Bischof & Wagner, 5B):* Normalization is partly a domestic process (stigma removal when far-right parties enter parliament) and partly a transnational one (Trump's victory reduced social desirability costs of expressing far-right preferences in Europe). An EU-level response to normalization would need to counter not just domestic legitimation but international legitimation signals.

*The Halikiopoulou coalition (8B):* The materialist peripheral voters who are crucial for far-right success are susceptible to economic anxiety triggers. Cross-national economic asymmetries (wage differentials, migration pressure) are among Roumanias's contagion mechanisms. An EU-level response could address the economic inequalities that drive migration-fueled anxiety — but this is also exactly what EU fiscal policy debates have been deadlocked on.

*The Heft information ecology:* If the transnational far-right depends on a shared information infrastructure (US RNS hubs, YouTube, Twitter/X, CPAC-style institutional networks), then content moderation, platform governance, and institutional network disruption are potential intervention points. But these raise serious free speech concerns, and research on counter-extremism consistently shows that deplatforming can radicalize audiences and push activity to less regulated spaces.

*The Dinas refugee exposure finding (8B):* If mere exposure to large-scale immigration events produces far-right electoral effects, and if such events are inherently transnational (refugee crises don't respect national borders), then EU-level refugee allocation policies are directly relevant to far-right success.

No single answer is correct here. The point is to establish that the transnational frame doesn't just add complexity to the domestic explanations — it changes what kinds of responses are potentially effective.

### Key Passages

**Roumanias et al.:**

- *Defining political contagion:* "We define contagion of the FR as a process of diffusion across space and time, of policy positions and voting behavior between units of analysis (countries, parties, voters) associated with notions of geographical or cultural proximity." (p. 2) — The framework's foundation.

- *The fundamental asymmetry:* "Whereas domestic demand shows an initial negative response to foreign demand which is replaced by a positive effect after four to five years, the response of supply is always negative, pointing towards what we call substitution effect: successful foreign FR policy positions can act as a substitute tactic and destigmatization opportunity which allows domestic FR parties to relax their own extreme policy stances." (p. 2) — The core empirical finding.

- *The policy implication:* "Spillovers entail strong voting externalities which render national policies ineffective, highlighting the need for European-level policies, which can internalize these externalities." (Abstract) — The blunt conclusion that closes the paper and opens the course's final discussion.

- *Short-run fear vs. long-run emulation:* "In the short run, we uncover a positive spatial correlation between domestic and foreign demand which becomes negative when we control for foreign contextual factors, indicating that the short-term mechanism of contagion might be through a fear-based response of domestic voters to foreign contextual stimuli. In the long run however, the effect becomes negative, indicating that positive spillovers between foreign and domestic demand require time." (p. 3)

**Turnbull-Dugarte & Rama:**

- *The core theory:* "Our theoretical proposition is that the bandwagon/titanic effects engendered by performance signals can also permeate state boundaries. The electoral defeat of an ideologically symmetrical party in country A is likely to serve as a heuristic of the potential viability and popularity of the comparable party unit in country B." (p. 4) — The theoretical extension of domestic voting models to the international level.

- *Spain as most likely case:* "VOX has very much sought to mirror Trumpian messages in its communication strategy. Their debut campaign slogan promised to 'Make Spain Great Again' [Hacer España Grande Otra Vez] and called for 'Spain First' [España lo primero] in what were clear examples of the party's attempt to directly plagiarise Trump's campaign rhetoric." (p. 5) — The supply-side connection between the two parties.

- *The titanic finding:* "Those respondents interviewed immediately after the announcement of the US election results were significantly less likely to support Spain's far-right party... On average, those interviewed after Trump's loss were 5 percentage-points less likely to vote for VOX compared to those interviewed before the results were known." (p. 7) — The headline causal finding.

- *Conditional effect:* "Right-wing voters in the treatment condition are 11 percentage-points (p < 0.06) less likely to report an intention of voting for VOX vis-à-vis the control group (equal to a 43% change relative the control mean). Left-wing observe a 1.8 percentage-point decrease." (p. 8) — Where the effect is concentrated and why.

- *Caution in interpretation:* "Whilst our results illustrate that Trump's electoral defeat has struck a blow against the far-right in Spain (at least in the immediate short term), we are cautious in our interpretation of these results. Our findings do not lead us to support any conclusions regarding Trump's demise signalling a reversal of the trajectory of far-right success in the long-term." (p. 10)

**Heft et al.:**

- *US as hub:* "The United States represents an outlier in that it features the largest and domestically most integrated network of RNS, while U.S. sites function as hubs for transnational connections from European RNS." (Abstract) — The key structural finding for understanding the Jones/Bolsonaro video.

- *Hyperlinking as political strategy:* "Among the main functions frequently described in party and movement research — also in the field of right-wing actors — are force multiplication, identity building and reinforcement, and opponent dismissal and issue control." (p. 488) — The analytical framework for interpreting RNS linking behavior.

- *The mainstream media paradox:* "Legacy news media are crucial transnationally shared reference points... Rather than presenting an insulated, alternative sphere, the emerging digital news ecology on the right seeks to link up to the broader information environment across borders." (Abstract) — The finding that challenges the echo chamber thesis.

- *Recontextualization as strategy:* "Rather than building an insulated alternative sphere, such recontextualization is common practice among RNS. Given those sites' often limited resources, linking to existing content can also be a (professionally motivated) cost-saving strategy." (p. 490, adapted from Ekman 2019) — Why RNS link to mainstream media even as they position against it.

- *US as potential future for European ecology:* "The United States might, therefore, point toward a potential future for the other countries' news ecologies, where the digital right-wing is densely interconnected and, thereby, fosters a more insular alternative right-wing news ecology." (p. 499)

### Methods Notes

**Roumanias et al.:**

The paper uses a regional panel dataset at the NUTS-3 level spanning 7,652 far-right electoral outcomes across European countries from 2000 to 2017. This is their major data contribution — existing studies typically use national-level data, which obscures within-country variation and limits the number of observations. NUTS-3 regional data allows much more statistical power and can capture geographic proximity effects that national data cannot.

The main analytical tools are spatial panel models with impulse response functions, which allow the paper to trace not just whether contagion exists but *how long it takes*. The impulse response analysis is what produces the 4-5 year peak finding — a sophisticated tool that students may not have encountered. The key methodological contribution is the identification of the time dynamics of contagion (the shift from fear to emulation).

A limitation worth noting: the observational panel design cannot fully rule out common causes. If multiple European countries face similar immigration pressures or economic shocks in the same period, their far-right vote shares might increase together without any actual contagion between them. The paper addresses this through the geographic and cultural proximity analysis (contagion should be stronger between proximate countries) but cannot definitively establish causation.

**Turnbull-Dugarte & Rama:**

The unexpected event during survey design (UESD) approach uses the coincidental timing of a major event with an ongoing survey to create a natural quasi-experiment. The key assumption is that the election result was exogenous to survey respondents' characteristics — those interviewed before and after the result are exchangeable. The paper provides extensive balance checks (demographic characteristics of pre- and post-treatment respondents are similar) and placebo tests (no treatment effect on evaluations of the economy or COVID-19 concern, which should not be affected by Trump's electoral loss).

The ITT vs. CACE distinction is worth a brief mention: the intent-to-treat effect (ITT) measures the effect of being in the treatment group (interviewed after the result), not the effect of actually knowing about the result. If only 70% of treated respondents actually heard the news, the true complier average causal effect (CACE) would be approximately ITT/0.7 = larger. Google Trends data showing a massive spike in Spanish interest in Trump during election week provides evidence that awareness was high, but the design cannot directly measure individual-level awareness.

**Heft et al.:**

The hyperlink network analysis harvests all outgoing hyperlinks from 65 RNS websites over a three-month period in 2018. The key measure is the Transnational Reference Score (TRS) — based on how many different national RNS communities link to a given site, normalized by the number of links. A site scores high on TRS not by receiving many links from one country but by being linked to by sites from multiple different countries.

The paper is primarily descriptive — it maps a network rather than testing a causal hypothesis. This is appropriate for the research question (does a transnational right-wing information ecology exist?) but limits causal inference. The 2018 snapshot is also a fixed time point; given how rapidly right-wing media ecosystems have evolved (Parler, Telegram, Rumble, Truth Social emerging after 2018), the specific network topology identified is likely outdated even if the general findings about US hub status and mainstream-media reference pointing remain robust.

**Instructor's Research Note — Related Ongoing Work**

A working paper by Holverscheid, McGovern & Seawright (2026) studies the same network these three papers theorize, combining process tracing of leader meetings and organizational ties with multilingual sentence embeddings and dynamic topic models of complete speech corpora for Trump, Modi, Bolsonaro, Orbán, Meloni, and Milei. Key findings relevant to this session: Bolsonaro emerged as the primary network entrepreneur after his 2018 election — his rhetorical clusters have the highest betweenness centrality in the cross-leader similarity network despite Brazil's geopolitical periphery — with Orbán succeeding him as hub after the pandemic. Eduardo Bolsonaro, who appears in the opening video, is identified as an early CPAC network node: he emceed CPAC Brazil in 2019 and brokered the Bolsonaro–Orbán connection through Budapest meetings that year. Trump and Modi show more domestically oriented rhetorical profiles, consistent with Roumanias et al.'s finding that geographic and cultural proximity drive contagion. The paper cites Roumanias, Turnbull-Dugarte, Heft, Wojczewski, and Giani directly — useful if students ask about empirical work that directly measures the network these papers theorize, and relevant to the 9B synthesis question about which theoretical contributions will travel beyond the course.

### Anticipated Student Moves

**"Doesn't this just show that all right-wing politics is connected? What's specifically 'contagious' about it?"**

This is a fair challenge to the concept. Roumanias et al. distinguish contagion (transmission of electoral behavior or policy positions beyond what common causes would predict) from mere correlation. The methodological test is whether proximity — geographical or cultural — predicts contagion after controlling for common contextual factors. If contagion were just "all countries facing the same immigration shock at the same time," proximity should not predict who influences whom. The finding that proximate countries show stronger contagion after controlling for contextual factors is the empirical signature of genuine transmission rather than common causes.

**"The Turnbull-Dugarte results could just reflect social desirability — people not wanting to admit they support VOX right after Trump lost. That's not real contagion, just embarrassment."**

This is exactly the alternative mechanism the authors acknowledge. The balance check on past VOX vote recall (treatment and control groups report similar rates of having voted VOX in the past) is their main argument against this interpretation: if it were pure social desirability, you would expect people to also downplay their past VOX support, not just their intentions. But the design cannot definitively distinguish between actual preference change, changed expectations of viability, and social desirability effects. This is a genuine limitation of the quasi-experimental approach when the outcome is self-reported political preference rather than actual behavior.

**"If the US is the hub for transnational right-wing media, does that mean Trump matters more for global far-right politics than European leaders do?"**

Heft et al.'s finding suggests something like this for the media infrastructure, but the causal direction is not clear. US RNS being hubs could reflect the pre-existing cultural and economic hegemony of American media generally — American media companies are simply larger, have more content, and attract more global traffic — rather than specifically the political influence of Trump. Roumanias et al.'s framework, by contrast, predicts contagion based on proximity, which would give European leaders (who are proximate to each other) more relative influence over European far-right parties than an American figure would have. The papers are studying different aspects of the phenomenon (media networks vs. electoral outcomes) and might produce different answers to the hub question.

**"CPAC seems like just a conservative conference — why is it being treated as evidence of far-right transnational networking?"**

The key is that CPAC has been explicitly used as a node in exactly the networks these papers describe. VOX leaders attended at the American Conservative Union Foundation's invitation and expense. Steve Bannon formed operational links with VOX through the CPAC network. Turnbull-Dugarte and Rama identify these connections as evidence of the supply-side symmetry between Trump's Republicans and VOX. CPAC in 2019 included multiple European far-right parties and explicitly framed the event as a gathering of a global movement — which is exactly how Jones and Bolsonaro are describing it in the interview. The line between "conservative conference" and "far-right transnational organizing" is contested, but the Jones/Bolsonaro interview itself explicitly frames CPAC as part of the global far-right movement.

### Connections Forward

**To course synthesis (Week 9B and beyond):** This session is the pivot to the course's final question: how do domestic and transnational explanations fit together? Weeks 1-8 established the domestic building blocks; Week 9 shows that those blocks connect across borders in ways that change what kinds of interventions are possible. The Closing Question is designed to set up this synthesis: once you understand that far-right support is partly a transnational phenomenon, what follows for both explanation and response?

**To Valentim (5A) and Bischof & Wagner (5B):** Turnbull-Dugarte's Titanic finding is the cross-national extension of Valentim's normalization argument. Valentim showed that domestic far-right electoral success reduces stigma and increases willingness to express far-right support. Turnbull-Dugarte shows that this legitimizing mechanism crosses national borders: Trump's victory was a positive normalization signal for Spanish far-right sympathizers, and his defeat was a delegitimizing signal. Bischof & Wagner's finding that far-right parliamentary entry increases polarization has a transnational analog: the entry of far-right parties into governments across Europe during the 2016-2019 period may have created a cross-national normalization wave.

**To Koopmans (6A):** Koopmans's political opportunity structure framework asked about the institutional conditions that enable far-right mobilization. Roumanias et al. add a transnational dimension: political opportunities in neighboring countries (successful far-right mobilization abroad) can function as an opportunity for domestic far-right parties by demonstrating viability and destigmatizing positions. The POS framework was originally designed for individual countries; the contagion literature suggests it needs a transnational extension.

**To Halikiopoulou (8B):** Halikiopoulou's coalition theory identified the materialist peripheral voters as the decisive electoral group for far-right success. The Roumanias paper identifies cross-national economic asymmetries as a contagion trigger — not just cultural threat but economic anxiety activated by immigration pressure from neighboring countries. The economic side of Halikiopoulou's coalition is partly a transnational phenomenon, driven by labor market integration and cross-border migration.

**To Heft's information ecology and Dixit's red-pilling (8A):** Dixit's red-pilling concept describes individual conversion to far-right worldviews through online information environments. Heft maps the media infrastructure through which that information circulates transnationally. The two papers together suggest that red-pilling is not a purely domestic process: the algorithmic and hyperlink networks Heft identifies mean that someone red-pilling in Denmark is likely being exposed to content produced in and curated by US-based right-wing media hubs. The transnational information ecology is the medium through which individual radicalization often occurs.

## Session 9B: Discourse, Illiberalism, and Individual-Level Contagion — Course Synthesis

**Focus:** The 9A session asked how the far right spreads transnationally — through what mechanisms, along what pathways, via what media infrastructure. This session shifts the question: what exactly is spreading? Wojczewski (2026) examines the ideological content and discursive architecture that enables far-right parties to cooperate across national borders despite their chauvinistic nationalism. Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak (2023) trace one detailed case — Hungary's Orbán and his embrace by American conservative media — that illustrates how that architecture operates through the actions of individual public diplomacy actors. Giani & Méon (2021) provide the individual-level downstream evidence: Trump's election demonstrably shifted willingness to express racist attitudes in 13 European countries, an effect consistent with norm contagion operating through the same transnational networks the other papers map.

This is also the final session of the course. The last thirty minutes are devoted to four integrative synthesis questions — not to cap what students have already learned but to ask them to commit to positions they can defend.

**Timing note:** The session is compressed on the front end to create room for the synthesis discussion. Two mini-lectures (not three), a shorter passage-bridging activity, and then the four synthesis questions as the main event.

---

### Opening Hook (5 min): What Tucker Carlson Said from Budapest

In August 2021, Fox News host Tucker Carlson traveled to Budapest, Hungary, and broadcast his nightly program from there for a week. In the opening segment of his first broadcast, he said the following:

> "If you care about Western civilization, and democracy, and family — and the ferocious assault on all three of those things by leaders of our global institutions — you should know what is happening in Hungary right now."

Write this quotation on the board. Ask students, briefly, to respond to three prompts before any discussion:

> Who is the audience for this statement? Who is Carlson saying is attacking Western civilization, democracy, and family? Why would an American news host broadcast from Budapest?

Take 3-4 quick answers. The goal is not a full analysis but to surface what students already notice: the globalist elite framing ("leaders of our global institutions"), the reactionary logic (civilization under assault), the exportability of Hungary as a model, and the specific choice of Budapest as a staging ground. 

Tell students: every idea in that sentence will reappear in each of the three papers today.

### Mini-Lecture 1: Wojczewski — The Discursive Infrastructure of Counter-Hegemony (17 min)

**The puzzle the paper addresses.**

Here is a genuine puzzle: far-right parties are chauvinistic nationalists. They believe that the nation is the natural unit of political community, that national culture and identity must be protected against foreign influence, and that sovereignty means freedom from international obligation. And yet they cooperate internationally. Nigel Farage speaks at Trump rallies. Marine Le Pen and Matteo Salvini appear together at AfD-organized conferences. Orbán speaks at CPAC Dallas; CPAC holds a conference in Budapest. Steve Bannon tours European capitals trying to build an international alliance of nationalist parties. If nationalism is the core of their politics, why would these parties build transnational networks?

Wojczewski (2026) argues that existing scholarship has not answered this question well, partly because it has defaulted to calling these parties "populist" and assuming that populism explains their transnational collaboration. This default is a conceptual mistake with political consequences.

**The critique of "populism" as master concept.**

Wojczewski draws on Laclau and Mouffe's poststructuralist discourse theory to argue that populism is, in itself, content-free. A populist discourse constructs a people-vs.-elite antagonism and claims to represent "the people" against an unresponsive "establishment." But populism cannot tell you who "the people" are, why they are frustrated, or what they want. A left-wing democratic socialist movement can be populist (Bernie Sanders, Chávez). A right-wing ethnonationalist movement can be populist (Trump, Le Pen). Populism is the form; the substance comes from other discourses with which populism is articulated.

The practical consequence is that calling racist nationalism "populism" unintentionally legitimizes it. If racism and xenophobia are described as expressions of "the frustrated will of the common people," they acquire a democratic veneer they do not deserve. Wojczewski explicitly calls out scholars who, in their analyses of Trump's rhetoric, have ended up describing the racialized targeting of immigrants simply as "populist rhetoric." This framing contributes to mainstreaming, the process by which far-right positions move from the political margins to the center by being continuously described in less alarming vocabulary.

**The three logics of articulation.**

Wojczewski argues that the radical right should be understood as a discursive project articulating three political logics together — not as an ideology with fixed programmatic content, but as a way of constructing meaning and identity:

*Populism* provides the antagonistic form: people vs. elite, down vs. up. It tells the audience that their demands have been frustrated by an unresponsive and illegitimate power structure. By employing the populist logic, the radical right can present its nationalism and reactionary politics as the democratic will of "the common people" rather than as the ideological preferences of an exclusionary ethnocultural movement.

*Nativist nationalism* provides the content: who "the people" are (the ethnocultural in-group — native, white, Christian) and who the out-groups are (immigrants, minorities, "foreigners"). The nationalist mode of articulating radical right politics operates through a racializing logic that constitutes non-natives as existential threats to the identity and security of the nation.

*Reactionism* provides the temporal axis: the interpellated subject is told that a once-whole and secure identity is now under assault, and that this lost order can be restored through political action. "Take our country back," "Make America Great Again," "put the 'Great' back into Great Britain" — these all employ a reactionary logic that promises to reverse the changes (globalization, multiculturalism, immigration) responsible for the perceived decline.

*Globalism* as the shared enemy: across all the PRR discourses Wojczewski analyzes, "globalists" function as the common antagonist. Wojczewski is direct about the ideological genealogy: the globalist conspiracy theory derives from extreme-right and neo-Nazi movements and typically functions as a coded antisemitic reference to the "cosmopolitan Jew." Mainstreaming this conspiracy theory under the populism label obscures what it actually is.

**How transnational cooperation becomes possible.**

The shared discursive structure — common antagonist (globalists), compatible mode of constituting the political subject (endangered ethnocultural nation), and shared reactionary promise (restoration) — allows PRR parties in different countries to establish what Laclau calls "equivalential ties": my political project and your political project are different, but we share the same enemy, and our struggles are therefore equivalent. Farage at a Trump rally saying "you're fighting the same globalists we're fighting in Britain" is an act of equivalence-forging: he is not claiming that American and British politics are the same, but that they are aligned against the same threat.

**Why the transatlantic alliance failed; why the European alliance partly succeeded.**

The empirical finding: Bannon's attempt to formalize the transatlantic alliance through an organization called "The Movement" failed — no major European PRR party formally joined. The European PRR parties' coalition (the Identity & Democracy group in the European Parliament) succeeded better, uniting parties from 10 EU member states.

The discursive reason: Trump's nationalism located the enemy primarily in the US establishment (corrupt Washington elites). The European PRR parties located their enemies in the EU/Brussels (supranational technocrats undermining national sovereignty). These are compatible enough for symbolic solidarity (both invoke "globalists"), but not compatible enough for a joint organizational project — Trump's "America First" and Marine Le Pen's "France First" both push against the same symbolic adversary, but they also potentially push against each other if they imagine their national interest in competing terms.

Within Europe, the parties can instead construct a shared civilizational identity: "Christian-European Occident" against "Islamisation" and "African" migration. This construction — which Wojczewski calls "ethnopluralism" — is the actual basis of the ID group's international vision. Ethnopluralism is the far-right's answer to the apparent contradiction of nationalist internationalism: each nation defends its own ethnically homogenous culture; all nations share the project of preventing migration and multiculturalism that would threaten this order. It is, as Wojczewski emphasizes, a form of cultural racism and, in practice, a blueprint for "global apartheid" — though the ID group frames it as respect for cultural diversity.

**Tucker Carlson from Budapest, revisited.**

Return to the opening quote. Carlson's framing — "Western civilization," "democracy," "family," "global institutions" — is now visible as a textbook deployment of the Wojczewski framework: reactionary (civilization under assault), ethnopluralist (Western rather than American), populist (global institutions vs. the people who care about democracy and family). His choice to broadcast from Budapest positions Hungary as the place where the counter-hegemonic project is most visibly succeeding — which is exactly what Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak analyze.

### Mini-Lecture 2: Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak and Giani & Méon — The Case and the Effect (12 min)

**From abstract discourse to individual actors: deinstitutionalized public diplomacy.**

Wojczewski maps the discourse structure and the events that constitute the counter-hegemonic network. Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak (2023) examine one specific node in that network in fine detail: how Hungary and Viktor Orbán have come to occupy a prominent and largely favorable place in American conservative media.

Public diplomacy — the attempt by a government to manage its image with foreign publics — has traditionally been conducted through institutional channels: cultural institutes, educational exchange programs, journalism accreditation, formal foreign policy communications. Since the rise of populist leaders, Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak argue, public diplomacy has been "deinstitutionalized." Leaders bypass official channels and conduct public diplomacy personally — through social media, media appearances, and direct appeals to foreign audiences. They call this "hyper-presidentialization": the leader, not the foreign ministry, is the primary vehicle for the country's international image.

The argument in Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak: Orbán has successfully used American conservative media to build a favorable image of Hungary among American conservatives, largely by positioning himself as the leader who has successfully implemented what those conservatives aspire to do — restrict immigration, protect "traditional" family values, resist LGBTQ+ rights, challenge EU-mandated multiculturalism. The mechanism is deinstitutionalized: it runs not through the Hungarian Embassy in Washington or through official cultural diplomacy but through Tucker Carlson's prime-time slot on Fox News, Rod Dreher's prolific blogging at *The American Conservative*, and Orbán's English-language Twitter account.

**The empirical evidence.**

The study codes 1,643 articles from 13 American conservative media outlets from 2019 to 2022. Of these, 558 are substantively relevant to Hungarian politics. A clear trend of increasing coverage appears from 2021 onward — after Carlson's Budapest broadcasts — with 146 articles (26%) actively defending or praising Hungarian policies or Orbán's leadership. Among those praising, the focus is almost entirely on Orbán personally rather than on Fidesz, the Hungarian government as an institution, or Hungarian politics more generally. The term "American Orbánism" appears in multiple outlets. Rod Dreher explicitly tells American conservatives they "have a lot to learn from Orbán." Tucker Carlson broadcasts "let's make America more like Hungary."

The language is instructive: it is not "Hungary is doing interesting things we should study" but "Orbán is a leader we should emulate." The supply side of the Roumanias contagion framework operates precisely through this individual-leader-as-model mechanism: Orbán's successful implementation of illiberal policies destigmatizes those policies in the American conservative imagination and provides a demonstration that they can be done within a nominally democratic framework (elections are still held; Orbán wins them; he is not overtly authoritarian in the classical sense).

**What illiberalism means, and why it matters conceptually.**

The paper's conceptual contribution is the category of illiberalism, distinguished both from mere non-liberalism (not everything that is not liberal is illiberal) and from conservatism. Drawing on Marlene Laruelle's definition, they describe illiberalism as a backlash specifically against contemporary liberalism — its political, economic, cultural, and civilizational forms — in the name of majoritarian, nation-centric, sovereignty-asserting politics that favor traditional hierarchies and cultural homogeneity. Crucially, illiberalism wins elections; it does not simply reject democratic processes. This distinguishes it from classical authoritarian politics and makes it both harder to counter through democratic mechanisms and more exportable to consolidated democracies.

This is worth pausing on in class: Hungary's European Parliament colleagues demoted it from democracy to "hybrid regime of electoral autocracy" not because Orbán abandoned elections but because the institutional conditions for competitive elections were systematically degraded while elections continued to be held. The far right's political project, at its most sophisticated, is to use democratic processes to progressively undermine the conditions that make democratic competition meaningful.

**Giani & Méon: the individual-level effect.**

The chain of papers in this session — Wojczewski (discourse structure), Cabrera Cuadrado (individual-actor case), Giani (individual-level downstream effect) — is designed to walk from the ideological to the institutional to the psychological. Giani & Méon (2021) provide the most precisely identified causal evidence in the session and in the transnational thread of the course overall.

The design is by now familiar to students (Turnbull-Dugarte used the same approach): ESS Round 8 fieldwork coincided with the 2016 US presidential election; respondents interviewed after November 8 can be compared to those interviewed before. The outcome variable is a behavioral measure of racial bias in policy attitudes: respondents are asked how many immigrants of the *same* race as the country's majority should be admitted, and how many of a *different* race. Revealing a differential preference — wanting fewer immigrants of a different race — is a report of racial bias, one that carries a social cost in face-to-face interview settings because it publicly expresses a norm violation.

Trump's election increased the probability of reporting racial bias by 2.3 percentage points across 13 European countries. The effect is specific: Obama's first election reduced reported racial bias; second-term elections (which did not change the status quo) had no effect. The mechanism Giani & Méon propose is norm signaling: Trump's election told European respondents that the social norm of racial neutrality was less mainstream than they had assumed, lowering the expected social cost of expressing racist attitudes.

The geographic patterning is analytically interesting: Scandinavian countries (Sweden, Finland, Estonia) showed below-average effects; Austria, Switzerland, and the Netherlands showed above-average effects. Students can generate hypotheses: Does Scandinavian non-updating reflect stronger domestic norms of racial equality? Or weaker US political influence in Northern Europe? Does Austria's stronger update reflect the existing strength of the FPÖ (making the Trump signal more resonant for an existing movement)?

**The chain across this week's readings.**

By now students should see how the three 9B papers, together with the three 9A papers, form a coherent explanatory chain:

*Supply side — infrastructure:* Heft shows that right-wing news sites form an interconnected transnational network with the US as hub. Tucker Carlson's Fox News and Jones's InfoWars are central nodes. Cabrera Cuadrado shows one specific supply-side operation: Orbán's illiberal public diplomacy, channeled through those same US-hub outlets, building a favorable image among American conservatives.

*Demand side — electoral contagion:* Roumanias shows that far-right electoral performance is contagious across geographically and culturally proximate countries. Turnbull-Dugarte shows the causal mechanism: viability signals cross national borders, affecting voting intentions in the short term.

*Ideological content:* Wojczewski shows what ideology enables the cooperation: shared logics of populism, nativist nationalism, and reactionism, organized around the globalist enemy and an ethnopluralist vision of the international order.

*Individual-level downstream effect:* Giani shows that a major far-right electoral success abroad (Trump 2016) shifts social norms about expressing racist attitudes across 13 European countries. This is the micro-level consequence of the macro-level discourse and media networks the other papers study.

### Discussion Activity (12 min): Passage-Bridging

**Format:** A new format — passage-bridging in trios. Each group receives one passage from a 9B paper and one prior-course reading to connect it to. The task is both synthetic and critical: identify the conceptual connection, and identify any tension or incompatibility. Five minutes in groups, five minutes of structured debrief, two minutes of transition to synthesis discussion.

This format differs from all previous activities in that it requires students to make a cross-text argument — not defending one framework against others (9A), not testing a claim against evidence (8A), not assembling evidence for a mechanism (8B), but identifying the theoretical relationship between two texts from different weeks.

**Group A — Hellström (8A) ↔ Wojczewski:**

Passage: "The ID group defines the defence of 'identity', 'sovereignty' and 'borders' as its overarching political goals... the fierce opposition to multiculturalism: 'multicultural societies are the most violent, the most criminal, the most divided and hostile communities.'" (Wojczewski, p. 19, adapted)

Connection question: Hellström (8A) argues that contemporary far-right racial politics operates primarily through Framework 2 — neo-racism or cultural racism — in which cultural incompatibility replaces biological hierarchy as the organizing logic. Groups are not ranked as superior or inferior; they are said to be naturally distinct, incompatible, and each deserving of its own national or civilizational space. This is what the far right calls ethnopluralism. When the ID group defends "identity, sovereignty, and borders" and argues that "multicultural societies are the most violent," which of Hellström's three frameworks are they deploying — and which are they explicitly *not* deploying? Wojczewski's poststructuralist analysis argues that the globalism/nationalism opposition in PRR discourse descends from extreme-right ideological genealogies. Does this analysis converge with Hellström's claim that ethnopluralism is neo-racism even when it disavows racial hierarchy — or does Wojczewski's focus on *mode of articulation* rather than *content of beliefs* prevent him from making that claim directly?

**Group B — Giani & Méon ↔ Valentim (5A):**

Passage: "In a world where the social norm of racial neutrality is mainstream, reporting a racial bias entails a social cost that racially biased respondents may avoid by insincerely reporting no bias. However, the election of Donald Trump, a candidate with racially biased views, signaled that the social norm of racial neutrality was less mainstream than previously assumed."

Connection question: Valentim's normalization argument holds that far-right parties' entry into parliament reduces stigma and makes it socially more acceptable to support them domestically. Giani & Méon argue that a far-right electoral victory abroad shifts social norms about expressing racial bias internationally. Are these the same mechanism operating at different levels (domestic vs. transnational), or are they different mechanisms with different implications for how counter-strategies should work? And: if both are true simultaneously, what does that suggest about how quickly far-right political normalization can travel?

**Group C — Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak ↔ Heft et al. (9A):**

Passage: "Hungary's hosting of CPAC in Budapest and Orbán's subsequent invitation to CPAC Dallas has elevated the profile of the prime minister among conservative American audiences, and has proven to be a resource for the hyper-presidentialization of Hungarian public diplomacy."

Connection question: Heft et al. studied hyperlink networks among right-wing news sites and found the US functions as a hub for European connections. Cabrera Cuadrado shows the human and institutional layer underlying that network: Tucker Carlson, Rod Dreher, CPAC, the Danube Institute, Orbán's English-language Twitter. Identify one specific example from Cabrera Cuadrado and map it onto Heft's analytical vocabulary (hub, transnationally shared reference point, recontextualization, force multiplication, identity building). Then identify one thing Heft's framework cannot capture about the Orbán case that Cabrera Cuadrado's public diplomacy framing helps explain.

**Debrief prompts:**
- What is at stake, practically or politically, in the conceptual choice each paper makes?
- What surprised you in making the connection?
- If you were designing a study to answer a question neither paper answers, what would it be?

### Course Synthesis: Four Integrative Questions (30 min)

This is the main event of the final session. These are not questions with correct answers that the instructor will eventually reveal. They are questions that require students to take positions, defend them with evidence from the course, and engage with each other's reasoning.

For each question: give students approximately 60 seconds to write a brief response before opening the floor. Aim for roughly 6-8 minutes per question, with some flexibility based on where discussion is most generative.

---

**Question 1: What is the far right?**

> "Thinking about the global phenomenon we've been discussing this week, but also about the wave of national and sometimes smaller movements we've looked at throughout the quarter — what is the far right? If someone were to ask you what you've been studying this quarter, how would you define the subject matter?"

This is a definitional question that is harder than it looks. Students should be aware by now of at least three distinct ways the course has implicitly defined the object of study:

*Mudde's ideational minimum* (implicit throughout): nativism + authoritarianism + populism, operating through a thin ideology that constrains but does not determine specific policy positions. This definition captures the structural features that unite the AfD, VOX, Fidesz, FN, and Trump across very different national contexts.

*Wojczewski's discursive project*: a formation articulating populism, nativist nationalism, and reactionism — where what matters is not the programmatic content but the mode of constructing political subjects and antagonisms. On this view, the far right is not a set of beliefs but a way of doing politics.

*Hellström's definitional view*: the far right as a political actor that strategically selects which definition of racism to use in order to do neo-racist politics while maintaining anti-racist credibility. On this view, what defines the far right is not a fixed set of beliefs but a set of definitional moves — the exploitation of Framework 1 cover to practice Framework 2 politics. The far right is a definitional exploit.

*The demand/supply distinction* (from Halikiopoulou, Roumanias, etc.): the far right as a two-sided political market — a supply of parties and politicians offering a particular product, and a demand from voters whose preferences are activated by cultural threat, economic anxiety, or welfare chauvinism.

*Heft's media ecosystem view*: the far right as partly constituted by the alternative media infrastructure it has built and inhabits — RNS sites, social media channels, CPAC-style institutional networks.

*The lived experience view*: the Nick Fuentes followers, the women in Blee's study, the people in Dixit's red-pilling narratives. These individuals would not primarily self-identify as "far right"; they might say nationalist, patriot, traditionalist, or simply that they are standing up for what they believe.

What unites these views? What does any definition of "the far right" need to be able to do — empirically distinguish its members from non-members, capture what is distinctive about the phenomenon, avoid conflating it with adjacent phenomena (conservatism, illiberalism, populism)?

*Key tension to surface:* The more precise the definition, the less it fits the full range of movements the course has studied. The more capacious the definition, the more it risks including things that don't belong and legitimizing things that should be contested.

---

**Question 2: What are the social-science ideas that seem most useful?**

> "What are the social-science ideas that seem most useful for explaining the timing and pattern of the current wave of far-right activity? We have read a lot of different perspectives using a lot of different theories. Which two or three theoretical ideas will you take with you?"

This is deliberately asking students to be selective — the value of the question lies in having to choose and defend a choice, not in cataloguing everything the course covered.

Some likely candidates, with brief notes on their strengths:

*Political opportunity structure (Koopmans):* Explains variation across countries in far-right success — why the same demand-side pressures (immigration, economic anxiety) produce different electoral outcomes in different institutional contexts. Strong on cross-national variation; weaker on timing and the transnational dimension.

*Demand/supply distinction and welfare chauvinism (Halikiopoulou):* The most tractable analytical framework for understanding who votes for the far right and why. The materialist/culturalist distinction has structured a generation of research. The supply-side's ability to bridge the two through welfare chauvinism is a genuine theoretical contribution. Weakness: still operates primarily within national context.

*Social desirability and norm signaling (Giani, Valentim, Turnbull-Dugarte):* Perhaps the most analytically precise family of ideas in the course — the insight that what we measure in surveys is often not attitudes but the social cost of expressing attitudes, and that political events shift those costs both domestically and transnationally. This reframes the question of whether "real" attitudes are changing or just their expression, and has direct implications for survey-based empirical work.

*Transnational political contagion (Roumanias):* The integration of domestic and transnational explanatory factors — the insight that far-right success creates externalities that cross national borders, making nationally-bounded explanations necessarily incomplete. The short-run fear vs. long-run emulation dynamic is genuinely novel and empirically supported.

*Counter-hegemonic discourse building (Wojczewski):* A theoretically distinctive contribution — the insight that what enables transnational cooperation is not shared policy preferences but shared logics of articulation (populism + nationalism + reactionism), and that the political consequences of scholarly vocabulary choices matter.

*Racism typology and definitional politics (Hellström):* The insight that far-right racial politics operates primarily through neo-racism (Framework 2) — cultural incompatibility rather than biological hierarchy — and that the strategic choice of a narrow definition (Framework 1) provides political cover for the practice. Important because it implies that counter-strategies that accept the narrow biological definition of racism will systematically fail to name what contemporary far-right parties are actually doing.

Instructor prompt if discussion stalls: If you had to explain the rise of far-right parties in Europe and the United States between 2015 and 2020 using only two theoretical ideas — one from Week 5 or 6 and one from Week 9 — which would they be and how would they combine?

**Question 3: Does social science add value?**

> "Does a social-science approach to methods add to the study of the far right, relative to what we learn from disciplines such as history, journalism, and the humanities? Why or why not, in your view?"

This question invites genuine epistemological disagreement. There is no consensus view in political science on this, and reasonable people hold very different positions.

*The case for social science's distinctive contribution:*

Giani & Méon's paper could not have been written without survey data, a natural experimental design, and statistical analysis. Turnbull-Dugarte & Rama's quasi-experiment provides causal evidence that Roumanias et al.'s observational panel data cannot. The claim that Trump's election *caused* an increase in self-reported racial bias in 13 European countries, rather than simply co-occurring with it, requires the methodological apparatus of a natural experiment. History and journalism could document that a spike in hate crimes followed Trump's election; only a study exploiting quasi-random survey timing can isolate the causal effect.

*The case for history, journalism, and the humanities:*

Many of the most illuminating insights in this course came from non-social-science sources. The red-pilling dynamics in Dixit's chapter, Hellström's typological argument about how far-right parties exploit definitional ambiguity, the primary source material in the Jones/Bolsonaro and Lauren Southern videos — these are not improved by regression coefficients. Wojczewski's discourse analysis is humanities methodology applied to political questions; it illuminates the ideological architecture of the far-right counter-hegemonic project in a way that no survey or panel dataset could. And Hellström's systematic review with corpus linguistics occupies a methodological middle ground: it is systematic and data-driven, but the output is a conceptual typology rather than a causal estimate.

History in particular provides what social science often cannot: the long view, the comparative context, and the warning about category mistakes. The debate over whether Trump or Orbán or Le Pen constitutes "fascism" is essentially a historical argument requiring close attention to what fascism actually was, how it operated, and what distinguishes it from contemporary illiberal nationalism. Social science methods cannot settle that question.

*The synthesis position:*

The strongest position is probably that different methods are appropriate for different questions. "Does X cause Y, on average, across cases?" — social science, preferably causal inference. "What does it feel like to be part of X, and what functions does it serve for participants?" — ethnography, journalism, or history. "What ideology structures X, and what political consequences does calling it by different names have?" — discourse analysis, intellectual history. "How did X compare to earlier phenomena that look similar?" — comparative history. The question is not whether social science adds value but whether scholars have chosen the right tool for the question they are asking.

Instructor prompt: The course has included both quantitative social science (Giani, Turnbull-Dugarte, Roumanias, Halikiopoulou, Magni) and qualitative and interpretive approaches (Hellström, Wojczewski, Cabrera Cuadrado). Has one type been more convincing to you? More useful? Are "convincing" and "useful" the same thing in this context?

**Question 4: Which questions remain most urgently incomplete?**

> "Which questions that we've raised this quarter still seem most urgently incomplete to you? Which seem most satisfactorily resolved?"

This is the most open-ended of the four questions, and the most directly generative for students thinking about research agendas. Some scaffolding:

*Reasonably well-resolved:*

The existence of a global far-right wave with transnational dimensions is well-established across multiple methods and research designs. The debate about whether it is primarily driven by cultural threat or economic anxiety has probably produced a more accurate picture: both matter, the coalition is heterogeneous, and the supply side can strategically bridge them (welfare chauvinism). The basic causal claim that far-right political success normalizes far-right attitude expression — domestically and transnationally — has now been replicated with credible designs across several papers.

*Urgently incomplete:*

Counter-mobilization and deradicalization are nearly absent from the course. The readings have largely focused on explaining far-right success; they have not systematically addressed when it fails, what makes counter-movements effective, or how individuals exit far-right movements (Blee's own work on exit is not in the syllabus). Given that the policy question most practitioners face is "how do we counter this?", the gap is significant.

The interaction between far-right politics and democratic backsliding is touched on in Cabrera Cuadrado (Hungary as hybrid regime of electoral autocracy) but not systematically studied. When does a far-right party in government undermine democratic institutions vs. when does it operate within them? What are the conditions that separate Orbán from, say, Italy's Brothers of Italy?

The role of race in European far-right politics is analytically underdeveloped. The US literature, particularly in the Giani paper, is explicit about racial bias and racial norm signaling. The European literature more often uses "immigration" and "cultural threat" as proxies, which may obscure the degree to which these are coded racial categories. Cross-Pacific and Global South dimensions of the far-right wave (what explains Bolsonaro? What explains Modi? Are these the same phenomenon?) appear mostly as brief references rather than sustained analysis.

The long-run question: Do far-right governments deliver what they promise? If far-right parties are partly sustained by genuine economic anxiety, what happens when their policies — typically anti-immigration, protectionist, anti-EU — do not resolve that anxiety? Does failure produce democratic correction or entrenchment?

*Instructor's option:* share your own view about what the most important open question is for the field, and why. The final session is a reasonable occasion for a moment of instructor transparency about where you think the literature is weakest.

No formal summary — students have just spent 30 minutes producing the synthesis. ### Methods Notes

**Wojczewski:**

Poststructuralist discourse analysis in the Laclau-Mouffe tradition approaches texts not to determine what actors "really mean" or what causal variable they represent, but to map the relational structure of meaning — which signifiers are linked to which, which antagonisms are constructed, what subject positions are constituted. The method requires reading primary material (speeches, manifestos, tweets, conference statements) analytically rather than as raw data to be coded. The corpus here spans 2015–2021, covers PRR parties in the US and Europe, and includes self-generated transcripts of joint events.

This is a qualitative method with no sampling strategy in the statistical sense and no hypothesis-testing procedure in the normal social-science sense. Its epistemological ambition is interpretive rather than causal: to answer "what does this mean and how does it work?" rather than "what is the effect of X on Y?" Students may reasonably ask how one assesses whether a discourse analysis is correct. The answer in the Laclauian tradition is whether the interpretation is consistent across the full corpus, whether it illuminates rather than distorts the material, and whether it opens up productive questions. It is evaluated like a close reading in literary studies, not like a regression coefficient.

**Cabrera Cuadrado & Chrobak:**

A mixed-methods content analysis: quantitative (counting articles across outlets, tracking trends over time) combined with qualitative (reading and categorizing article content). The quantitative component establishes the trend (increasing coverage, increasing proportion of favorable coverage). The qualitative component identifies the specific framings, vocabulary, and characterizations that constitute the trend. Neither alone is sufficient: the quantitative trend without the qualitative characterization would be consistent with a story about Hungary getting more media attention for any reason; the qualitative characterization without the quantitative trend would be illustrative but potentially cherry-picked.

Limitations the paper acknowledges: the study covers 13 outlets selected to represent American conservative thought, excluding some large-audience outlets (Breitbart, OAN). It has no opinion polling data on American conservative attitudes toward Hungary, which would be necessary to confirm that the media trend actually changed minds. The trend in media coverage is not direct evidence of successful public diplomacy; it is evidence that something changed in the media landscape.

**Giani & Méon:**

See also the 9A methods note on Turnbull-Dugarte, which uses the same UESD (unexpected event during survey design) approach. A distinctive feature of Giani & Méon is the outcome measure: self-reported racial bias is measured not as a single item but as a gap between two items that differ only in the racial background of the immigrants being asked about. This construction is clever because it isolates racial bias from immigration attitudes per se: someone who opposes all immigration equally is not reporting racial bias; only someone who opposes immigration more when the immigrants are of a different race than the country majority is. And because the two questions are asked together, the respondent must actively reveal a differential preference — a stronger social desirability disincentive than a single-item measure would produce.

The entropy balancing step (matching control and treatment groups on covariate distributions) is worth explaining briefly: unlike propensity score matching, entropy balancing directly weights the control group so that its covariate distribution matches the treatment group's, rather than discarding unmatched observations. This reduces post-matching imbalance without the sample loss of traditional matching.

### Anticipated Student Moves

**"Isn't Wojczewski just saying that we should call racists 'racists' instead of 'populists'? That's not a social-science argument, it's a political one."**

This is a fair challenge that deserves a nuanced response rather than a dismissal. Wojczewski would reply that the distinction between analytical and political consequences of conceptual choices is itself unstable: concepts organize what we see and what we miss, what we treat as legitimate and what we don't. In a discipline that claims to study social reality, the categories we use shape the reality we produce. That said, students are right that there is a normative charge in Wojczewski's argument — he thinks that calling nativist nationalism "populism" is analytically wrong *and* politically harmful. Whether those two objections are separable is itself a methodological question worth pursuing.

**"Giani & Méon show that Trump's election shifted racial bias reports in 13 countries, but 2.3 percentage points seems small. Does this actually matter?"**

The effect is small in absolute terms but should be evaluated in context. A 2.3 pp shift in the probability of reporting racial bias across 13 countries represents millions of individuals, and the ESS is a face-to-face survey with strong social desirability pressure — meaning the underlying attitude change (or norm-shifting) is likely larger than the measured behavioral change. The Obama first-election decrease and the robustness across different bandwidths and specifications suggest the effect is real. More important than the specific magnitude is what the effect implies: a single foreign electoral event, with no direct policy consequences for the European countries studied, measurably shifted attitude expression across much of Europe within a 30-day window. Scale that to a sustained media ecosystem (Heft) and an ongoing public diplomacy operation (Cabrera Cuadrado), and the aggregate effect over years is far larger than any single event effect can measure.

**"Hungary is a small country. Is it really a useful case for understanding transnational far-right dynamics?"**

Hungary is small but analytically useful for precisely the reason Orbán has made it large: it has gone further than any other EU member state in implementing an illiberal program while remaining nominally within European institutional frameworks. Its significance for transnational dynamics is not as a large source of influence but as a *proof of concept* — a demonstration to far-right parties elsewhere that it can be done. Le Pen, Salvini, and Tucker Carlson are not interested in Hungary for its geopolitical weight; they are interested in it as an existence proof for the illiberal program. Cabrera Cuadrado documents this precisely: the coverage is not about Hungary's economy or international relations but about Orbán as a model leader.

**"Does any of this apply to non-Western far-right movements — India under Modi, Turkey under Erdoğan, Brazil under Bolsonaro?"**

The course has primarily focused on Europe and the US, with Bolsonaro appearing as a transnational symbol (in the Jones interview) rather than as an object of systematic study. Students raising this question are identifying a real gap in the course's scope. Brief answer: the same analytical frameworks — Mudde's ideational approach, the demand/supply distinction, Wojczewski's discourse analysis — have been applied to non-Western contexts, and scholars have found significant structural similarities (nativist nationalism, reactionary promise to restore a lost golden age, anti-globalism) combined with significant variation (the specific outgroups, the relationship to religion, the role of colonial history in constructing national identity). Whether these constitute the same political phenomenon or distinct regional variants is genuinely unresolved.

### Course Synthesis — Connections Back

This section replaces the standard "Connections Forward" because this is the final session.

**The demand-side thread:** The course began with the question of who votes for the far right (Weeks 5-6). The consensus view that emerged — from Koopmans's opportunity structure framework, through Halikiopoulou's coalition theory, Valentim's normalization argument, and the Dinas natural experiment — is that far-right demand is heterogeneous (cultural conservatives and economically precarious peripheral voters, united by supply-side welfare chauvinism) and responsive to political opportunity (institutional channels, normalization) and transnational signals (Giani, Turnbull-Dugarte).

**The supply-side thread:** The course's attention to supply side developed through Halikiopoulou's framework, through Koopmans's analysis of electoral opportunity structures, and into the transnational supply networks of Weeks 8-9. Roumanias's substitution effect — where far-right parties moderate their stances once destigmatization allows it — connects directly to Hellström's Framework 1/Framework 2 insight: "moderation" in practice often means shifting from explicit biological racism (Framework 1) to ethnopluralist cultural incompatibility discourse (Framework 2) while maintaining anti-racist self-presentation. The Tucker Carlson Budapest operation is supply side at its most deliberate.

**The movement thread:** Hellström (8A) and Dixit's Nick Fuentes material (8A) provided the ideological and rhetorical inside view — how racism is defined, denied, and deployed; how red-pilling constructs white victimhood; how the definitional exploit of Framework 1 cover enables Framework 2 politics in practice. Wojczewski's counter-hegemonic project is the abstract formulation of what those movements are doing at the international ideological level: ethnopluralism as neo-racism theorized as a political program. Heft's hyperlink networks are the media infrastructure through which that project is built and sustained.

**The methodological thread:** The course has moved across a wide range of methods: conjoint experiments (Magni), natural experiments (Giani, Turnbull-Dugarte, Dinas), observational panel data (Roumanias, Bischof & Wagner), network analysis (Heft), content analysis (Cabrera Cuadrado), discourse analysis (Wojczewski), systematic literature review with corpus linguistics (Hellström), and primary source video evidence. Each method has illuminated something different. The synthesis argument the course points toward: no single method is adequate, and the most robust knowledge emerges from multiple methods triangulating on the same phenomenon from different directions.
